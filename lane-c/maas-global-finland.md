## One-liner

MaaS Global, the Helsinki-founded pioneer of the "Mobility-as-a-Service" category behind the Whim app, filed for bankruptcy in March 2024 after raising more than $162M, undone by a subscription-package business model that required prepayment regardless of whether customers actually used their transport allowance.

## What happened

Founded in 2015, MaaS Global built the Whim app, bundling public transport, taxis, city bikes, electric scooters, and rental cars into a single subscription-based mobility platform — operating across cities including Helsinki, Vienna, Antwerp, Tokyo, Birmingham, and throughout Switzerland. The company is widely credited with popularizing "Mobility-as-a-Service" as a category, attracting over $162M from investors including NordicNinja, BP Ventures, Toyota, and Mitsubishi — strategic backing from major automotive and energy companies betting on the future of integrated urban mobility.

The core business model — B2C subscription packages requiring customers to prepay for a bundle of transportation credits — became a structural liability rather than a strength once COVID-19 sharply reduced public transportation usage: customers paying upfront for unused transport allowances generated losses for the company (€9.3M lost in 2022 against just €3.8M in revenue), since Whim bore the cost of guaranteed transport access whether or not customers actually traveled. With only around 10,000 active users in Helsinki despite years of operation and heavy funding, the unit economics never recovered post-pandemic, and MaaS Global filed for bankruptcy in March 2024. Dutch MaaS platform umob acquired the bankrupt company the following month.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Built the entire business model around prepaid subscription bundles — initially seen as an innovative, "game changer" product structure — which became a direct financial liability once usage patterns shifted (COVID-19-driven reduction in public transit use) and unused prepaid credits translated directly into company losses.
- Pursued a B2C subscription strategy in a category (urban mobility) with notoriously thin margins and high fixed infrastructure costs, rather than exploring B2B or municipal-partnership revenue models that might have provided steadier cash flow.
- Attracted major strategic corporate investors (Toyota, Mitsubishi, BP Ventures) whose capital and validation didn't translate into a path to sustainable unit economics, showing that strategic investor interest doesn't guarantee business model viability.
- Never achieved user-base scale (only ~10,000 active users in its home city, Helsinki, after nearly a decade) sufficient to make the subscription-bundling model work at the volume needed to smooth out prepayment risk.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

MaaS Global's collapse is a cautionary case for prepaid-subscription business models in any category exposed to demand volatility: what looks like an innovative retention mechanism (locking in customer prepayment) can become a direct financial trap when usage patterns shift unpredictably, since the company — not the customer — absorbs the cost of unused capacity. It's also a specific lesson for the broader "Mobility-as-a-Service" category the company pioneered: bundling multiple transport modes into a single subscription requires either enormous scale to smooth out individual usage variance, or a fundamentally different pricing model (pay-per-use rather than prepaid bundles) to avoid the same structural exposure that sank MaaS Global.
