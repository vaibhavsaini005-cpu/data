## One-liner

Jüsto, Mexico's 100% digital grocery platform which raised over $300M including a $70M round as recently as October 2024, ceased Mexican operations entirely on December 15, 2025, following earlier exits from Peru and Brazil the year before.

## What happened

Jüsto built a fully digital supermarket platform in Mexico, backed by major investors including General Atlantic, Bimbo Ventures, Foundation Capital, and FEMSA Ventures — a mix of traditional venture capital and strategic corporate investors from Mexico's food and beverage industry. The company expanded regionally into Brazil and Peru in 2021, acquiring local player Freshmart in Peru, pursuing a broader Latin American digital-grocery footprint beyond its Mexican home base.

Despite closing a $70M round as recently as October 2024 — $50M in equity led by General Atlantic and $20M in structured debt financing from HSBC — and even announcing a strategic partnership with Amazon in November 2024 to distribute Jüsto's product categories to Amazon customers in Mexico City, the company began unwinding regional operations almost immediately after: it announced a gradual shutdown of Peruvian operations in November 2024 and complete exit from Brazil in December 2024. The final domino fell just over a year later, when Jüsto ceased all Mexican operations on December 15, 2025, ending the Amazon partnership as well.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Expanded aggressively into Brazil and Peru via acquisition (Freshmart) in 2021, building a multi-country footprint that ultimately proved unsustainable and had to be unwound market-by-market starting in late 2024.
- Raised a substantial $70M round (including structured debt from HSBC) in October 2024 and pursued a high-profile Amazon partnership the same month — signals of confidence and expansion — only to begin regional retrenchment within weeks and complete Mexican shutdown just over a year later.
- Sequenced its retreat from largest-to-smallest markets in reverse order of commitment: pulling out of Peru and Brazil first (late 2024) while attempting to preserve and even reinforce its core Mexican market (via the Amazon deal) before ultimately failing there too.
- Combining equity and structured debt financing (HSBC) in its final major round suggests the company was already managing a complex capital structure that likely constrained its options once the underlying business couldn't sustain the debt service and growth targets.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Jüsto's trajectory — a major funding round and headline partnership (Amazon) immediately followed by total regional collapse within about 14 months — illustrates how quickly capital-intensive, thin-margin grocery-delivery businesses can go from apparent strength to total failure once the underlying unit economics don't support the cost of continued operations, regardless of recent fundraising or partnership announcements. The use of structured debt financing (HSBC) alongside equity in its last major round is also a pattern worth recognizing: companies layering debt onto an already-struggling growth-stage business face compounding pressure, since debt service continues regardless of revenue performance, unlike pure equity capital.
