## One-liner

Banxso, a South African online trading platform, was placed under provisional liquidation by the Western Cape High Court in August 2025 — confirmed as final liquidation in March 2026 — after the court found the company's business model was itself illegal, following a scheme that used deepfake videos of Elon Musk and businessman Johann Rupert to allegedly steal R1 billion from South African investors.

## What happened

Banxso built an online trading platform in South Africa, gaining a public profile in part through sponsoring Bafana Bafana, the national football team — a legitimacy-building move common among fintechs seeking mainstream consumer trust. Behind that public-facing legitimacy, however, the company allegedly ran a scheme using deceptive deepfake video advertisements featuring fabricated or manipulated footage of prominent figures including Elon Musk and South African businessman Johann Rupert, falsely promising extraordinary investment returns to lure victims.

The scheme allegedly syphoned approximately R1 billion from South African investors before regulatory and judicial action caught up with it. In August 2025, the Western Cape High Court placed Banxso under provisional liquidation, with presiding Judge André le Grange stating he was satisfied the company's business model was itself illegal — not merely that individual bad actors had misused an otherwise legitimate platform. The provisional order was confirmed as final liquidation in a judgment delivered March 2, 2026, though proceedings were subsequently complicated by a legal challenge from Flamingo Clearing House, a company implicated as a key player in the broader scheme.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Used sports sponsorship (Bafana Bafana, the national football team) as a legitimacy-building strategy, exploiting the trust South Africans place in national sporting institutions to lend credibility to what the court ultimately found was an illegal business model.
- Deployed deepfake video technology featuring real public figures (Elon Musk, Johann Rupert) without their consent to fabricate false investment endorsements, a technically sophisticated and deliberately deceptive marketing approach rather than more conventional (if still fraudulent) advertising.
- The court's finding that the business model itself — not just specific marketing tactics — was illegal represents a stronger judicial determination than a narrower finding of isolated misconduct, directly justifying full liquidation rather than a lesser remedy.
- A related company (Flamingo Clearing House) implicated in the scheme subsequently challenged the liquidation proceedings legally, extending the case's resolution timeline well beyond the initial court rulings.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Banxso's collapse illustrates an emerging and increasingly dangerous fraud pattern: using AI-generated deepfake videos of real, trusted public figures to lend false credibility to investment schemes, combined with legitimate-seeming institutional partnerships (national sports team sponsorship) to further disguise fraudulent intent from potential victims and regulators alike. The South African court's finding that the entire business model — not just its marketing — was illegal also signals a judicial willingness to treat AI-enabled deepfake fraud as a fundamental characteristic of an illegitimate enterprise rather than as a separable marketing violation, a precedent with implications for how regulators and courts elsewhere may approach similar deepfake-driven investment fraud schemes.
