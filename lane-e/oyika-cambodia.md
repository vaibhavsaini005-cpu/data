## One-liner

Oyika, an electric motorbike battery-swapping infrastructure operator active in Cambodia, secured backing from global electronics distributor Arrow Electronics in January 2024, supporting its expansion of swappable-battery infrastructure for electric two-wheelers.

## What happened

Oyika built battery-swapping infrastructure for electric motorbikes — physical stations where riders exchange depleted batteries for fully charged ones in seconds, addressing the core adoption barrier for electric two-wheelers in emerging Southeast Asian markets where slow home charging and limited charging infrastructure otherwise make EV motorbikes impractical for delivery riders and daily commuters who need continuous uptime. This infrastructure-first approach positioned Oyika as a platform enabling EV adoption across multiple motorbike brands and riders, rather than selling vehicles directly.

The January 2024 partnership with Arrow Electronics, a major global electronics components distributor, brought industrial-scale supply chain and component sourcing support to Oyika's battery and charging infrastructure buildout — a strategic relationship distinct from a typical venture capital funding round, reflecting how infrastructure-heavy hardware startups sometimes need supply chain partners as much as, or in addition to, capital investors.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Built shared battery-swapping infrastructure rather than manufacturing or selling electric motorbikes directly, positioning the company as enabling infrastructure across the broader two-wheeler EV ecosystem rather than competing as a single vehicle brand.
- Targeted the operational-uptime pain point specifically (fast battery swaps versus slow charging) that most directly blocks EV adoption among commercial riders (delivery, ride-hailing) who cannot tolerate long charging downtime.
- Secured a strategic supply chain partnership with a global electronics distributor (Arrow Electronics) rather than relying solely on traditional venture capital, addressing hardware sourcing and component-supply risk directly alongside capital needs.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Oyika demonstrates that infrastructure-layer plays in emerging-market EV adoption — solving the charging/energy-access bottleneck rather than competing on vehicles themselves — can attract strategic industrial partners whose core business (electronics component distribution) benefits directly from EV infrastructure scaling, not just financial investors. This is a useful pattern for hardware-intensive climate/mobility startups in developing markets: pairing capital-focused fundraising with supply-chain-focused strategic partnerships can de-risk both the financing and sourcing sides of physical infrastructure buildout simultaneously.
