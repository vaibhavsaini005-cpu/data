## One-liner

CoinStats, a Yerevan-headquartered crypto portfolio tracking platform, was forced to temporarily shut down its app in June 2024 after suspected North Korean state-sponsored hackers drained over $2 million from roughly 1,590 user wallets via a socially engineered employee, forcing the company to rebuild with a more limited, security-hardened service.

## What happened

CoinStats built a crypto portfolio tracking and wallet management platform out of Yerevan, Armenia, serving users who wanted a unified view of holdings across multiple cryptocurrencies and exchanges. In June 2024, the company suffered a serious security breach in which suspected North Korean state-sponsored hackers gained access to internal systems, compromising approximately 1,590 user wallets — about 1.3% of all CoinStats wallets — and draining more than $2 million in assets.

The company's own investigation, according to its CEO, traced the root cause to a social-engineering attack against a CoinStats employee, who was manipulated into downloading malicious software onto a work computer, giving attackers an internal foothold. In response, CoinStats immediately suspended its application entirely rather than attempting to keep it running during the crisis, only restoring service days later with deliberately limited functionality as a stopgap measure "to ensure a smooth and stable experience" while security was hardened — a pivot from unrestricted, full-featured service toward a more constrained, security-first operating posture in the incident's aftermath.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Chose to fully suspend the application rather than attempt to keep it partially running during an active security crisis, prioritizing containment over service continuity.
- Publicly attributed the breach specifically to a social-engineering attack against an employee rather than a purely technical vulnerability, shaping both its security remediation priorities and its public narrative around the incident.
- Restored service with deliberately reduced functionality rather than restoring full features immediately, treating the relaunch as a controlled, security-first pivot rather than a return to business as usual.
- Left users without a clear reimbursement policy in the incident's immediate aftermath, a decision that drew continued criticism and scrutiny into 2025 even as the core service resumed operating.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

CoinStats' breach illustrates a growing risk pattern for crypto infrastructure companies: state-sponsored attackers increasingly target employees through social engineering as an entry point, rather than relying solely on exploiting technical vulnerabilities in the platform itself, meaning security investment in employee training and internal access controls has become as critical as product-level security engineering. The company's decision to relaunch with intentionally reduced functionality, rather than either shutting down permanently or resuming full service immediately, also demonstrates a middle-path crisis-recovery pattern: a security-first, incrementally-restored service can preserve a company's user base and brand through a serious breach, though unresolved questions like reimbursement policy can leave lasting reputational damage even after technical operations resume.
