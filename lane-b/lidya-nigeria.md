## One-liner

Lidya, a Nigerian digital lender founded by Jumia alumni that disbursed over $150M to 32,000 small businesses and raised $16.45M across its funding history, shut down in October 2025 after nearly a decade, citing "severe financial distress" following a leadership crisis and a disbanded Portugal-based tech team.

## What happened

Lidya was founded in 2016 by Tunde Kehinde and Ercin Eksin, both alumni of Jumia's founding team, to provide fast, collateral-free loans to small and medium enterprises through an online platform — addressing the persistent SME credit gap across Nigeria and, later, other African markets. At its peak, the company reviewed over $50 billion in credit applications and disbursed more than $150 million to 32,000 small businesses, funded through a $6.9M Series A in 2018 and an $8.3M pre-Series B round in 2021, totaling $16.45M raised.

The company's decline unfolded over roughly a year before the October 2025 shutdown: co-founder Tunde Kehinde departed in October 2024, followed shortly by CTO Cristiano Machado in September, and the company's Portugal-based technology team was disbanded between May and September 2024 after Lidya failed to meet its payroll obligations to that team. In October 2025, the company sent customers an email announcing immediate cessation of all operations, stating that despite efforts to restructure, it had "encountered severe financial distress and is no longer able to continue in business."

## Key decisions / turning points

- Built its lending business on collateral-free SME credit, a model requiring continuous access to capital and funding to sustain loan disbursement — a structural vulnerability once fresh funding became harder to secure.
- Experienced a leadership exodus (co-founder and CTO both departing within roughly a year of each other) that coincided with, and likely both reflected and accelerated, the company's underlying financial distress.
- Failed to meet payroll obligations for its Portugal-based technology team specifically, months before the full company shutdown — an early, concrete signal of cash-flow problems that preceded the final closure by about a year.
- Chose to communicate the shutdown directly and transparently to customers via email rather than a slow wind-down or asset sale, ending operations immediately once the financial distress became terminal.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Lidya's collapse illustrates how digital lending startups are structurally exposed to funding-market conditions in a way that other business models aren't: the core product (loan disbursement) directly requires continuous capital access, so a prolonged fundraising drought doesn't just slow growth — it can halt the ability to originate new loans and service existing operations entirely. The roughly year-long sequence of warning signs before the final shutdown (payroll failures, team disbandment, executive departures) also shows that African fintech failures are frequently visible in slow motion to close observers well before the official closure announcement, rather than being sudden, unforeseeable events.
