## One-liner

Koko Networks, an 11-year-old Nairobi clean-cooking startup that had built one of Africa's most celebrated climate-tech success stories, abruptly laid off its entire 700-person workforce and shut down all Kenyan operations on January 31, 2026, after the Kenyan government blocked its planned sale of carbon credits — a decision that triggered insolvency proceedings just months after the company had secured a $179.64 million World Bank guarantee for expansion.

## What happened

Koko Networks built a network of fuel dispensing points across Kenya selling bioethanol as a clean, affordable substitute for charcoal and kerosene in low-income households' cooking, reaching roughly 1.5 million households at its peak and positioning itself as a flagship example of climate-tech success in East Africa. The company's business model depended heavily on monetizing carbon credits generated by households switching away from dirtier cooking fuels — credits it planned to sell internationally to fund continued below-cost fuel pricing and expansion.

The collapse came after two days of intense internal meetings at Koko's Nairobi headquarters in late January 2026, following the Kenyan government's rejection of a Letter of Authorization the company needed to sell its carbon credits internationally — a decision reportedly driven by government concern that the volume of credits Koko intended to sell would monopolize the country's national carbon market quota. Without the ability to monetize those credits, the company's below-cost pricing model became unsustainable overnight. On January 30, 2026, customers learned of the shutdown via a terse SMS notification rather than any advance announcement, and the company filed for insolvency the following day — a stunningly abrupt end for a company that had, barely a year earlier, secured a $179.64 million guarantee from the World Bank to support its Kenyan expansion.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Built a core business model dependent on monetizing carbon credits through international sales, creating a single point of regulatory failure that could unwind the entire company's unit economics if government approval was withdrawn.
- Continued scaling toward the $179.64 million World Bank-guaranteed expansion without securing durable, long-term certainty over its carbon-credit export rights from the Kenyan government.
- Communicated the shutdown to 1.5 million customers via a single SMS rather than a structured wind-down, reflecting how suddenly the collapse occurred once the Letter of Authorization was denied.
- Filed for insolvency within 48 hours of the government's rejection, rather than attempting to restructure the business around a purely domestic, non-carbon-credit-dependent model.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Koko Networks' collapse is a stark illustration of regulatory concentration risk in climate-tech and carbon-credit-dependent business models: a company can build over a decade of genuine operational success and still be rendered instantly insolvent when a single government decision removes the specific revenue mechanism its unit economics depend on. The case also highlights growing tension between national governments and climate-tech companies over who captures the value of carbon markets — as more countries begin asserting sovereign control over carbon-credit export quotas, companies whose business models rely on selling those credits internationally face a structural vulnerability that no amount of prior operational success or institutional backing (including a major World Bank guarantee) can fully insulate against.
