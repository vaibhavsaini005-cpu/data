## One-liner

Pakistan's best-funded startup shut down after an $85 million Series B, a pandemic-era pivot from mass transit to quick commerce, costly expansion, and a financing round that failed at the wire stage.

## What happened

Airlift began in Lahore in 2019 as an app-based mass-transit service. When COVID-19 disrupted transit, it pivoted to 30-minute grocery delivery and expanded across eight Pakistani cities. In August 2021 it raised an $85 million Series B at a reported $275 million valuation—the largest single funding round for a Pakistani startup at the time.

The capital-intensive quick-commerce model and an early South Africa expansion raised costs. By May 2022, Airlift had laid off nearly one-third of its workforce, reduced its Pakistani footprint from eight cities to three, and closed South Africa. Although the company said it had cut burn by 66% and reached order-level profitability, its planned Series C1 collapsed as participants delayed or declined to wire funds amid the market downturn. Airlift permanently ceased operations in July 2022.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Pivoted from buses to grocery delivery when the pandemic made the original transit model unworkable.
- Used a record Series B to pursue rapid quick-commerce growth, including a South Africa launch that increased expenses.
- Retrenched in May 2022, but the savings came too late to remove dependence on another funding round.
- Shut down when committed financing could not be disbursed in time, despite claimed progress toward profitability.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Airlift shows that a well-executed emergency pivot can create growth without creating resilience. A high-burn model remained dependent on continuous external financing, so operational improvements could not compensate for timing risk when capital markets tightened. International expansion before durable company-level profitability further reduced the margin for error.
