## One-liner

Chaldal, the pioneer of online grocery shopping in Bangladesh founded in 2013 that raised roughly $40 million between 2015 and 2025, faced a liquidity crisis in early 2026 after anticipated foreign investment failed to materialize amid political uncertainty — leaving hundreds of Jashore facility staff unpaid for three to four months, cutting its workforce from 3,300 to 2,200, and forcing emergency negotiations with the state-run Startup Bangladesh fund.

## What happened

Chaldal built Bangladesh's first major online grocery platform, establishing the category over more than a decade of operation and raising approximately $40 million across its funding history — a substantial sum for a Bangladeshi startup and evidence of years of sustained investor confidence in the business. Founder and CEO Waseem Alim stated the company had never previously faced difficulty paying salaries in its 12-13 years of operation before problems began in August 2025, underscoring how abruptly the crisis emerged relative to the company's long operating history.

By early March 2026, the scale of the crisis became public: the company was sitting on a Tk40 crore (roughly $3.3 million) working capital shortfall, had quietly reduced its workforce from 3,300 to 2,200 employees over the preceding year, and had formally written to the state-run Startup Bangladesh fund requesting emergency bridge financing after anticipated foreign investment failed to materialize — a failure the company attributed partly to broader political uncertainty in Bangladesh. Several hundred workers at Chaldal's Jashore Hi-Tech Park facility staged demonstrations demanding three to four months of unpaid wages ranging from Tk20,000 to Tk50,000 per employee, ultimately reaching an agreement to settle the overdue December 2025 salary on March 3, January 2026 wages by March 10, and February salaries by March 30.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Sustained profitable-seeming operations for over a decade (12-13 years) without wage-payment difficulties before the crisis emerged in August 2025, suggesting the liquidity crunch stemmed from a specific external funding failure rather than chronic operational unprofitability.
- Reduced headcount from 3,300 to 2,200 quietly over roughly a year before the crisis became public, attempting to manage the capital shortfall internally before seeking external emergency support.
- Formally sought emergency bridge financing from the state-run Startup Bangladesh fund once foreign investment failed to materialize, turning to domestic public-sector capital as anticipated private international funding fell through.
- Negotiated a structured, staggered repayment schedule for overdue wages (December through February, paid across March) with protesting employees, rather than an immediate lump-sum settlement, reflecting the severity of the ongoing cash constraints even during resolution.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Chaldal's crisis illustrates how even a well-established, category-defining startup with over a decade of stable operations and roughly $40 million in cumulative funding can face sudden existential liquidity pressure when a single anticipated foreign funding round fails to close — particularly in markets where political uncertainty can abruptly close off international capital that had previously seemed reliable. The case also demonstrates the risks of dependency on foreign investment specifically: when that channel closed amid political instability, the company had to turn to a state-run domestic fund as a last resort, highlighting the importance of diversified funding sources and adequate cash reserves for even mature startups operating in markets where political and macroeconomic conditions can shift rapidly enough to derail an otherwise-expected capital raise.
