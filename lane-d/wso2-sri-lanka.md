## One-liner

Founder and CEO Sanjiva Weerawarana agreed in May 2024 to sell all outstanding WSO2 shares to EQT in a $600 million transaction that closed on August 13, returning nearly 30% of the proceeds to current and former employees while preserving the company's Sri Lankan operating core.

## What happened

Weerawarana built WSO2 from Colombo into a global API-management, integration, and identity-software company. By the transaction, WSO2 employed roughly 800 people, operated in more than 90 countries, and was approaching $100 million in annual recurring revenue. TechCrunch reported an exact transaction valuation of $600 million.

On May 3, 2024, Weerawarana told employees that WSO2 had signed a definitive agreement for EQT to acquire 100% of the company. He explained that the sale would cash out investor, employee, and vested-option holdings, with nearly 30% of total proceeds going to current and former employees. WSO2 announced completion on August 13, 2024; Weerawarana remained CEO and joined the new board, and EQT set out to support the next phase of global growth.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Weerawarana had resisted earlier acquisition interest in pursuit of an independent company and eventual IPO, but accepted EQT's proposal because the buyer planned to build around WSO2 rather than merge it away and a controlling shareholder wanted liquidity.
- Made broad employee ownership a foundational policy, producing a measurable result at exit: nearly 30% of transaction proceeds were allocated to current and former employees.
- Kept WSO2's center of gravity in Sri Lanka despite repeated pressure to relocate; roughly 80% of 780 employees were still in the country when TechCrunch profiled the transaction.
- Used only about $70 million in primary capital to approach $100 million ARR, then chose a buyer that proposed using both organic investment and acquisitions to target $300 million to $500 million ARR over five years.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

WSO2 demonstrates that a globally competitive enterprise-software company can retain its engineering base in a smaller ecosystem and still create a large liquidity event. Its employee-ownership structure converted the exit into distributed local wealth, while the founder's buyer-selection criterion protected organizational continuity. The case also shows that a founder can treat a private-equity sale as a financing and governance transition rather than the end of the company's product mission.
