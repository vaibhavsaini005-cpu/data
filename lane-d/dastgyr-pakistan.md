## One-liner

Pakistani B2B e-commerce marketplace Dastgyr, which had raised $3.5M in seed funding and $37M in a June 2022 Series A, cut its workforce by 50-80% in January 2024 and cleared out warehouses, pivoting from a capital-intensive full-stack retail-supply model toward a leaner operation amid Pakistan's broader startup funding collapse.

## What happened

Founded in 2020, Dastgyr built a B2B e-commerce marketplace connecting small retailers to suppliers across Pakistan, digitizing a supply chain traditionally run through informal wholesaler relationships. The company raised a $3.5M seed round led by SOSV — with participation from Asian Development Bank's VC arm ADB Ventures, Seedstars, Edgebrook Partners, Bahrain's Zayani Venture Capital, and Dubai's Tricap Investments — before closing a much larger $37M Series A in June 2022, positioning it as one of the better-capitalized players in Pakistan's B2B e-commerce space.

By January 2024, amid a broader collapse in Pakistani startup funding (from $355M in 2022 to just $43M in 2024) and following the near-total shutdown of ride-hailing peer Cheetay, Dastgyr laid off between 50% and 80% of its workforce depending on the report, having already cleared most of its warehouse staff before year-end. Co-founder Muhammad Owais Qureshi cited rising operational costs as the reason for the reduction, effectively pivoting the company away from the warehouse-heavy, full-stack logistics model it had scaled toward under Series A capital.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Scaled a capital-intensive, warehouse-heavy B2B distribution model during a period (2022) when growth capital was abundant, leaving the company overextended once Pakistan's funding environment collapsed in 2023-2024.
- Cut warehouse staff first, before white-collar layoffs — sequencing cost reduction from the most capital-intensive part of the operation (physical inventory/logistics) toward the corporate structure, rather than the reverse.
- Attempted to preserve the company as a leaner operation rather than shutting down entirely (unlike peer Jugnu, which collapsed outright), positioning the layoffs as a pivot toward sustainability rather than a wind-down.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Dastgyr's near-collapse illustrates how B2B distribution startups that raise large rounds to build physical logistics infrastructure (warehouses, inventory) become acutely exposed when funding markets tighten, since that infrastructure carries high fixed costs that don't scale down easily. The sequencing of cuts — warehouse operations first, then corporate staff — is a pattern worth recognizing across capital-intensive logistics startups broadly: physical infrastructure investments made during a growth-capital boom become the first liability when that capital disappears.
