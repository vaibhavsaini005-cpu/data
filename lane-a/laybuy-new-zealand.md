## One-liner

New Zealand's Laybuy listed on the ASX in 2020 at the height of the buy-now-pay-later boom, expanded into Australia and the UK, then collapsed into administration in January 2023 as rising interest rates made its receivables-funded model uneconomical.

## What happened

Laybuy was founded in 2017 in Auckland by Gary Rohloff (a serial entrepreneur who had previously co-founded finance company Flexirent) and his son Alex Rohloff. The business offered a six-week, interest-free instalment plan: shoppers split purchases into six equal weekly payments with no fees to consumers, while Laybuy charged merchants a transaction fee of roughly 4–6%.

In September 2020, Laybuy raised approximately A$80 million via an ASX IPO (ticker: LBY), listing at A$1.44 per share. On day one the shares opened at A$2.00, briefly valuing the company at around A$300 million. The listing was timed almost perfectly with the global BNPL bubble driven by Afterpay's meteoric rise and a surge in e-commerce during COVID lockdowns. By early 2021 Laybuy was active in New Zealand, Australia, and the United Kingdom, with more than 700,000 active customers and over 12,000 merchant partners.

The business model had a structural vulnerability that was invisible in a low-rate environment: Laybuy funded its receivables book with external debt. When global central banks began rapidly raising interest rates through 2022 — the Reserve Bank of New Zealand lifted the OCR from 0.25% to over 5% in just 18 months — the cost of funding Laybuy's loan book outstripped the merchant fees the business collected. At the same time, regulatory pressure on BNPL providers in Australia and the UK increased and consumer delinquencies ticked up as households tightened spending.

By late 2022 the share price had collapsed to less than A$0.03, down more than 98% from the IPO price. Laybuy sought new capital but could not raise on viable terms. On January 13, 2023, the company announced it was immediately ceasing all operations, closing all customer and merchant accounts, and entering voluntary administration. The wind-down took weeks. Creditors recovered a fraction of outstanding balances.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Raising capital at BNPL peak, not before**: The A$1.44 IPO price and subsequent expansion into the UK were funded by the BNPL market's irrationally exuberant 2020 valuation. A private-market raise at realistic rates would have imposed capital discipline the IPO allowed the company to skip.
- **UK expansion over New Zealand profitability**: Management chose to enter the UK — a saturated market with Klarna, Clearpay (Afterpay), and Zilch already established — rather than achieving unit-economics profitability in its home market first. The UK operations consumed cash without reaching scale.
- **No exit at peak**: Afterpay was acquired by Square (now Block) for US$29 billion in August 2021, just 10 months after Laybuy's IPO. Laybuy was the right size to have been an acqui-hire target for a larger BNPL or payments player at that moment; management chose to continue building independently.
- **Rising rates as existential, not cyclical**: The board treated early 2022 rate rises as a temporary headwind rather than a structural threat to the model. By the time the severity was clear, the funding window had closed.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Laybuy is a textbook illustration of a business model that is **viable only in a specific macro regime** and cannot adapt once that regime ends. The BNPL model — borrow short at low rates, earn thin merchant fees, lend short to consumers — is a spread business masquerading as a tech business. When spreads compress and credit conditions tighten, there is no software moat to fall back on. The lesson for founders and investors: capital-intensive models with no intrinsic pricing power need either a rapid exit window or a genuine path to deposit funding (becoming a bank) before the rate cycle turns. Laybuy had neither.
