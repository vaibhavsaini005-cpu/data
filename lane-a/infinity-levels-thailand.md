## One-liner

Bangkok's Infinity Levels started as a dating-app company, and in 2013 CEO and co-founder Nikki Assavathorn moved the whole ten-person team onto freemium mobile games instead — keeping the original dating app alive but reallocating all attention to the category she judged more fertile, a bet that pulled in a $500,000 convertible note from AIS-linked VC InVent and a $2 million publishing deal within roughly a year.

## What happened

Infinity Levels began life building dating apps, shipping a product called Avalable. In 2013 Assavathorn concluded that the ground was better in gaming — specifically freemium mobile games — and moved the company's ten-person team across. Notably, she did not shut the original product down: Avalable remained in service while the team's focus shifted entirely. The pivot was an allocation of effort rather than a liquidation.

The target she picked was explicit and immodest: the model Supercell had popularised with *Clash of Clans*. The first game out of the pivot was *Ranch Run*, described as a hybrid of *Clash of Clans*, *FarmVille* and a racing game, slated for a January 2015 launch on iOS and Android, with two further titles planned for early 2015.

Assavathorn's reasoning for why a small Bangkok studio could compete in a category notorious for its hit-rate problem is the most transferable part of the record: "The games business is not hit and miss if you understand trends and you have a formula." She paired that with a deliberate constraint on output — the intention was to "keep our games collection small and stay focused on building quality titles" — which is the opposite of the shots-on-goal strategy most small studios adopt to manage the same risk.

The capital followed the pivot rather than preceding it. Early seed funding had come from Thai e-book startup Ookbee and from Singles Solution, Assavathorn's other business. In December 2014 the company raised $500,000 structured as a convertible note, led by InVent, a VC firm linked to Thai mobile operator AIS. Separately, Infinity Levels secured a $2 million publishing partnership with Pocket Playlab, another Bangkok games studio — meaning the company lined up a distribution partner with local scale rather than attempting to publish into global app stores unaided.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Pivoting on category attractiveness rather than product failure.** The stated trigger was a judgement that freemium gaming was more fertile ground than dating, not that Avalable had collapsed. Moving while the original product still functioned is a materially different decision from moving after it dies.
- **Keeping Avalable in service instead of killing it.** The old product kept running with no team attention. That avoided the write-off and any user backlash, at the cost of leaving an unsupported asset in the portfolio.
- **Rejecting the portfolio approach to hit risk.** Most small studios hedge by shipping many titles. Assavathorn committed to a deliberately small, high-quality collection on the thesis that trend-reading plus a repeatable formula substitutes for volume — a concentrated bet on process over shots on goal.
- **Taking a convertible note rather than a priced round immediately after pivoting.** A note defers valuation, which is the rational instrument when the company has just changed what it does and has no traction in the new category yet.
- **Securing a $2M local publishing partnership alongside the equity.** The company solved distribution through a Bangkok partner with existing reach rather than treating global app-store discovery as a problem it could win on its own.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Infinity Levels illustrates the pivot that is driven by market selection rather than product death — the harder and rarer kind. Avalable was still running; nothing forced the move. Assavathorn relocated ten people because she thought the other category paid better, which means the decision rested entirely on her read of where freemium monetisation was heading rather than on any signal from her own users. Pivots of this type are much more defensible to execute early, before a dying product consumes the runway needed to attempt anything else.

The second lesson is about how small studios in secondary markets handle the hit-driven economics of games. Assavathorn's formulation — "not hit and miss if you understand trends and you have a formula" — is a bet that the category's randomness is actually legible, and that a disciplined small team reading trends can beat a larger team taking more swings. Pairing it with a deliberately small catalogue makes the bet fully concentrated: if the formula is wrong, there is no portfolio to absorb it.

Worth noting as a caution on reading the record: the sources here capture the pivot and the capital that followed it within about a year, which is the decision point this entry documents. They do not establish what the *Ranch Run* strategy ultimately returned. The lesson is in the structure of the decision, not in a proven outcome.
