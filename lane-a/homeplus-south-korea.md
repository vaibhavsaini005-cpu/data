## One-liner

MBK Partners' 2015 purchase of Homeplus from Tesco for ₩7.2 trillion, Asia's largest leveraged buyout at the time, loaded Korea's No. 2 hypermarket with debt and lease obligations that it serviced by selling its own stores; a February 2025 credit downgrade tripped the structure into court rehabilitation, a year-long fight over ₩200 billion of rescue funding nearly liquidated the chain in July 2026, and a rehabilitation plan approved on 2 September 2026 left it with roughly half its stores, half its staff and no profit forecast before 2029.

## What happened

**The setup (2015).** Homeplus began in 1997 as a Samsung C&T retail unit, became a Samsung–Tesco joint venture in 1999, absorbed the Homever chain in 2008, and by the early 2010s was the second-largest hypermarket operator in South Korea behind E-mart, with consolidated sales of about ₩9 trillion and operating profit of ₩338 billion in 2013 and roughly 142 hypermarkets at its peak. In September 2015 Tesco, retreating from overseas markets after its own accounting scandal, sold 100% of Homeplus to a consortium led by Seoul-based MBK Partners, with Canada Pension Plan Investment Board and Temasek, for ₩7.2 trillion (about $4.9 billion). It was MBK's largest deal and the largest leveraged buyout in Asia to that point. MBK contributed about ₩2.2 trillion of equity and financed roughly ₩5 trillion with acquisition debt secured against Homeplus's real estate.

**The strategy that followed (2015–2024).** MBK's plan, in the words of Herald Business's 2026 retrospective, was essentially a real-estate play: pay down acquisition debt by selling Homeplus's freehold stores and leasing them back. Over the decade Homeplus disposed of about ₩3.45 trillion of tangible assets, including 68 sale-and-leaseback stores, with total monetised assets exceeding ₩4 trillion. The debt did not go away; it changed form. As Hana Securities analyst Kim Sang-man put it, "the form of debt has only shifted from general borrowings to lease liabilities," which reached about ₩4 trillion. Annual interest expense roughly tripled from a pre-buyout average of ₩94 billion to around ₩270 billion, and cumulative interest paid over ten years was about ₩2.7 trillion. A planned 2019 REIT listing that would have refinanced the property portfolio failed. Meanwhile the underlying business eroded: revenue fell from ₩7.66 trillion in 2018 to ₩6.48 trillion in 2021, operating profit slid from ₩259.9 billion (2018) to ₩93.3 billion (2020) and then flipped to losses of ₩133.5 billion (2021), ₩260.1 billion (2022), ₩199.4 billion (2023) and ₩314.2 billion (2024). Coupang and other e-commerce players took share while hypermarkets remained bound by the Distribution Industry Development Act's mandatory twice-monthly closures and delivery restrictions. E-mart and Lotte Mart responded with store renovations and online investment; Homeplus, starved of capital, could not. By November 2024 net debt stood at ₩5.31 trillion and the debt-to-equity ratio at 1,408.6%.

**The trigger (February–March 2025).** On 28 February 2025 Korea Investors Service and Korea Ratings cut Homeplus's corporate bonds one notch from A3 to A3-, citing "declining profitability, a heavy debt burden and growing uncertainty over its long-term competitiveness." The downgrade triggered mandatory early-repayment clauses on Homeplus's short-term funding. On 4 March 2025, four days later and without prior negotiation with creditors, Homeplus filed for court-led corporate rehabilitation at the Seoul Bankruptcy Court, describing it as a "preemptive move to avoid a liquidity crisis." The filing froze financial liabilities while wages and supplier payments were to continue. It quickly became a political and legal scandal because Homeplus had kept selling short-term securities (asset-backed short-term bonds and commercial paper) to retail investors in the days before the downgrade. Prosecutors later concluded from internal MBK documents that MBK and Homeplus knew of the impending downgrade at least eleven days earlier, before 17 February. The Financial Supervisory Service's sanctions committee recommended disciplinary action against MBK under the Capital Markets Act, and in January 2026 prosecutors sought arrest warrants for MBK chairman Michael ByungJu Kim (Kim Byung-ju) and three executives on fraud charges; a court rejected the warrants for insufficient evidence, but Kim was summoned again as a suspect on 12 September 2026. MBK issued a public apology in September 2025 and pledged to repay retail investors.

**The near-death (2025–July 2026).** Under rehabilitation Homeplus tried to sell itself. A stalking-horse process faltered in October 2025 and turned into an open auction; no buyer materialised, because the company was worth more in liquidation than as a going concern. Homeplus Express, the smaller-format chain once valued at around ₩1 trillion, was sold to Harim Group for about ₩120 billion in May 2026. Losses widened to ₩546.3 billion in 2025, and administrative claims exceeded ₩1 trillion by May 2026. The court set a minimum of ₩200 billion in new funding as a condition of any plan. Meritz Financial Group, the largest secured creditor with about ₩1.2 trillion of loans collateralised by 62 stores, offered only ₩100 billion and demanded a personal guarantee from Kim, which MBK called "highly unusual" and initially refused. On 3 July 2026 the court terminated the rehabilitation proceedings as "not feasible," putting some 12,000–13,000 employees, more than 4,600 vendors and, by one count, nearly 100,000 people at risk. Unpaid wages exceeded ₩100 billion. On 13 July 2026 every Homeplus store in the country suspended operations for lack of cash, and the government announced ₩440 billion of emergency liquidity for affected suppliers.

**The rescue and the outcome (July–September 2026).** Within days Kim provided the joint guarantee, Meritz approved a ₩200 billion debtor-in-possession loan, Homeplus appealed on 20 July, and on 21 July 2026 the court reversed its termination. Sixty-seven stores reopened on 13 August 2026, many with thinly stocked shelves, and took ₩116.4 billion in sales in their first weeks. On 2 September 2026 creditors approved the rehabilitation plan (100% of secured creditors, 75.9% of unsecured creditors, 100% of shareholders) and the court confirmed it the same day. The plan closes 37 stores and sells 19 of them to repay secured trust debt, keeps pursuing new money and a possible M&A, and forecasts no profit until 2029. The court warned it could still declare bankruptcy if scheduled repayments are missed. Homeplus is now down from 142 stores and more than 20,000 employees at peak to 67 stores and about 10,000 employees.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **MBK's 2015 decision to finance ₩7.2 trillion with roughly 70% debt and lean on the real estate to repay it (Michael ByungJu Kim, MBK Partners).** This was the original sin. It assumed hypermarket cash flows and property values would hold long enough to deleverage. Neither did, and the sale-and-leaseback programme converted acquisition debt into permanent rent, a fixed cost the operating business then had to carry through its decline.

- **Selling the prime stores (2015–2024).** An industry observer quoted by Herald Business: "They sold even the prime stores that any normal operator would keep as directly managed locations, just to raise cash quickly." Choosing liquidity today over the best-performing assets is the recurring trade in leveraged retail buyouts, and it removed the very stores that could have funded a renovation strategy like E-mart's.

- **Failing to refinance via a REIT (2019).** The one attempt to solve the property problem structurally, listing the stores in a real-estate investment trust, was withdrawn; from then on the capital structure had no exit other than selling more stores.

- **Continuing to sell short-term paper to retail investors while a downgrade was pending (February 2025).** Whatever the courts ultimately decide, this decision turned a corporate failure into a securities scandal that consumed MBK's reputation, drew FSS sanctions and criminal investigation of the firm's chairman, and poisoned the rescue negotiations that followed.

- **Filing for rehabilitation four days after the downgrade, without talking to creditors (4 March 2025).** MBK framed it as pre-emptive; creditors and the union experienced it as an ambush. The lack of a pre-negotiated deal is a major reason the process dragged for 18 months and nearly ended in liquidation.

- **The ₩200 billion standoff between MBK and Meritz (June–July 2026).** MBK's initial refusal to give a personal guarantee, and Meritz's insistence on one, pushed the company past the court's deadline and into a nationwide store shutdown. Kim's reversal within two weeks is what saved the company, which raises the question of why the guarantee was withheld until liquidation was imminent.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Homeplus is the reference case for what happens when private-equity leverage meets a structurally declining format. The buyout maths only worked if store real estate could be sold and the business could keep paying rent from a profit pool that e-commerce was already draining. Any operator or investor in physical retail should read the sale-and-leaseback line on the balance sheet as debt, because that is how it behaved here. It also shows how a downgrade-triggered acceleration clause can turn a slow decline into a sudden collapse: the company did not run out of customers, it ran out of the ability to roll short-term paper.

The broader pattern for founders and operators is the second-order cost of losing trust. Once the regulator, prosecutors and creditors believed MBK had sold securities while knowing a downgrade was coming, every later negotiation, including the one over ₩200 billion that nearly killed the company, was conducted without goodwill. In a rescue, credibility is the scarcest form of capital, and Homeplus's sponsor had spent it before the rescue began.
