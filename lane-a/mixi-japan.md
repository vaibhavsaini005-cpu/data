## One-liner

Mixi went from Japan's dominant social network with 27 million users — then a near-death stock collapse to under 10% of its 2008 peak — to a billion-dollar mobile gaming company after launching Monster Strike in 2013, one of Japan's most commercially successful games ever.

## What happened

Mixi was Japan's first major social network, launching in 2004 and IPO'ing on the Tokyo Stock Exchange Mothers market in September 2006. At its 2008 height it had 27 million registered users (roughly one in five Japanese) and its stock briefly traded above 100,000 yen per share.

Then Facebook, Twitter Japan, and LINE arrived. Mixi failed to adapt: it was slow to open up an API, struggled to transition to mobile, and its real-name-friendly registration made migration awkward for users who wanted pseudonymity. By mid-2012, shares had collapsed to roughly 1,500 yen — under 10% of the 2008 peak — and the company was burning through its social-media lead.

In October 2013, Mixi launched Monster Strike, a physics-based multiplayer action RPG. The key design insight was to embed the "play with friends" social mechanic directly into gameplay: you pulled monsters with friends physically present in the room, blending digital and physical co-op. The game was optimised for Japan's densely social commuter culture.

It exploded. Monster Strike surpassed $1 billion in annual revenue by 2015 — the first mobile game outside the US to achieve this — and peaked at $1.7 billion in 2016. By April 2025, the game had accumulated over $11 billion in lifetime gross revenue, with ~99% coming from Japan.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **2012** — Mixi hired Hiroki Morita to lead an autonomous game studio inside the company, giving it breathing room from the social-network product.
- **October 2013** — Monster Strike launched, deliberately designed around the social/friendship mechanics Mixi understood from its social network DNA.
- **2014** — Hiroki Morita became Mixi CEO, signalling the company's full pivot to mobile gaming.
- **Post-2016** — As Monster Strike revenues matured, Mixi diversified: FC Tokyo football club ownership, family communication app Mitene, and later sports/entertainment ventures.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Mixi is a canonical "dying asset repurposed" pivot. The social network's core insight — that Japanese users' strongest social bonds were with people physically nearby — became the game mechanic that generated $1B+ annually. The shadow lesson: Mixi has never replicated Monster Strike's scale, illustrating that a single monster hit rescues a company without guaranteeing a repeatable product engine.
