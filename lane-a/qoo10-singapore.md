## One-liner

Qoo10 founder Ku Young-bae publicly pledged to sell or pledge his own Qoo10 stake to cover a liquidity crisis at group units WeMakePrice and TMON on 29 July 2024 — the same day Korea's Justice Ministry hit him with a travel ban — and Singapore's MAS suspended Qoo10's own payment services two months later.

## What happened

Ku Young-bae, founder of Singapore-headquartered e-commerce group Qoo10, built the company partly through acquisitions of Korean platforms including TMON and WeMakePrice. When those two units failed to pay merchants on time in mid-2024, triggering a liquidity crisis, Ku issued a statement on 29 July 2024 committing to "selling all my shares in Qoo10, which account for most of my wealth, or use them as collateral to help sort out the situation," and said he would also try to offload Qoo10 assets for cash. He estimated customer damages at roughly 50 billion won ($36 million). Despite that pledge, Korea's Justice Ministry imposed an overseas travel ban on Ku the same afternoon, and the Korean government separately moved to inject $405 million to stabilize the affected platforms. The crisis wasn't contained to the Korean units: MAS found that Qoo10's own Singapore e-commerce platform had also been delaying merchant payments (complaints ran from April through August 2024), and on 23 September 2024 it directed Qoo10 to suspend all payment services covered under Singapore's Payment Services Act, after Qoo10 could not show it had the resources to meet its payment obligations.

## Key decisions / turning points

- 29 July 2024: Ku Young-bae publicly pledges his personal Qoo10 stake to cover merchant/customer losses at TMON and WeMakePrice, hours before being travel-banned.
- 23 September 2024: MAS suspends Qoo10's Singapore payment services after the company can't demonstrate it can meet its own payment obligations to merchants.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

The founder's personal-asset pledge and the regulator's suspension order landed on opposite sides of the same underlying problem: a rollup built through acquisition had let payment-processing risk spread from two subsidiaries back to the parent platform itself. A founder offering his own equity as collateral didn't resolve the liquidity gap fast enough to stop a second regulator, in a second country, from acting independently within two months.
