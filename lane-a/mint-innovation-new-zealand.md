## One-liner

Auckland-founded Mint Innovation, which uses a proprietary biological process to extract gold and other precious metals from e-waste, closed a NZ$60 million Series C in early 2023 and used it to open its first commercial biorefinery — in Sydney, not New Zealand.

## What happened

Mint Innovation was founded in Auckland out of research (with support from University of Auckland and Crown-backed Callaghan Innovation) into using organic biomass, instead of energy-intensive furnace smelting, to strip gold and roughly 40 other valuable materials out of discarded electronics like phones and laptops. The approach targeted a genuine environmental and economic gap: most e-waste globally is landfilled or crudely processed, losing recoverable precious metals in the process.

In February 2023, Mint closed a $60 million Series C funding round, hitting the top end of its $40-60 million target range. The round was led by Inspire Impact (Liverpool Partners' impact-investing arm), with participation from returning investors Movac, Icehouse Ventures, and K1W1. Rather than build its first commercial-scale plant at home, Mint opened it in Sydney in August 2022, ahead of the raise closing, citing Australia's mandatory e-waste recycling regulations as a stronger commercial foundation than New Zealand's voluntary regime — a point Movac partner David Beard made explicitly when discussing why regulatory support mattered for site selection. The Sydney facility was expected to process around 4,000 tonnes of e-waste a month at full capacity, generating roughly $60 million in annual revenue, and the company signalled plans for a second biorefinery in the UK, followed by the US or continental Europe.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Choosing biological over thermal extraction**: Betting on a microbe/biomass-based recovery process rather than conventional smelting gave Mint a lower energy-intensity and (it argued) better-economics pitch to both investors and regulators.
- **Building the first plant offshore in Sydney rather than at home**: Mint explicitly prioritized jurisdictions with mandatory e-waste recycling laws over its home market, treating regulation as a primary site-selection input rather than deferring to national pride or founder convenience.
- **Sequencing international expansion (UK, then US/Europe) ahead of a New Zealand plant**: The company kept the door open to eventually building in NZ but subordinated it to markets it judged commercially larger and more supportive.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Mint Innovation is a useful counter-example to the assumption that a startup should scale first in its home market: it treated regulatory environment as more decisive than geography or brand narrative, and was willing to say so publicly even though it meant NZ policymakers effectively lost a flagship cleantech manufacturing site to Australia. It's also a good illustration of deep-tech biotech-adjacent IP (a proprietary biological extraction process) being the actual moat, with plant location and expansion sequencing treated as a separate, purely commercial decision.
