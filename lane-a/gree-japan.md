## One-liner

Japan's dominant mobile social gaming platform lost a quarter of its market cap in a single day when regulators banned its core monetization mechanic in May 2012, forcing a decade-long pivot toward anime IP, VR, and virtual live streaming.

## What happened

GREE was founded in December 2004 by Yoshikazu Tanaka (ex-Rakuten) as a social network, then evolved into Japan's leading mobile social gaming platform by 2009. Its games used "complete gacha" (コンプガチャ, kompu gacha) — players bought randomised virtual card packs and received a special rare prize only when they collected a full set. The mechanic was enormously effective: GREE's annual revenue grew to roughly ¥155 billion in fiscal year 2013 (ending June 2013).

In May 2012, Japan's Consumer Affairs Agency ruled that complete gacha violated the Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations, effectively classifying it as illegal gambling. When markets opened on May 7, 2012, GREE's stock dropped roughly 20–25% in a single session. DeNA, GREE's closest rival, fell by a similar margin. Within weeks, every major Japanese social game provider had voluntarily removed the mechanic.

GREE cut its full-year sales forecast by 17% immediately after the ban. Revenue declined from its ¥155 billion peak across three consecutive fiscal years, falling below ¥60 billion by FY2016 (ending June 2016). The company closed its San Francisco studio, laid off around a third of its workforce globally, and wound down its platform-based third-party game distribution model.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Complete gacha as primary revenue driver**: Over-dependence on a single contested mechanic left GREE structurally exposed to regulatory action that was telegraphed months before enforcement.
- **Voluntary industry-wide pullback**: GREE and DeNA both announced removal of kompu gacha before the formal ban took effect, limiting legal exposure but confirming the scale of the revenue hit.
- **Pivot to self-owned IP**: Rather than try to rebuild the same platform model, GREE shifted to publishing original titles through its studio Wright Flyer Studios (WFS) and licensing Japanese IP internationally through GREE International.
- **REALITY app launch (2018)**: GREE relaunched as a "virtual live streaming" platform where users create 3D avatars and broadcast or watch performances. By 2022, REALITY had grown into GREE's highest-profile product, serving users in Japan and globally — a genuine reinvention rather than a line extension.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

GREE is a canonical case of a platform business built on a mechanic that regulators ultimately classified as gambling. The speed of the collapse (one regulatory announcement → 25% market cap loss overnight) shows how quickly platform value can evaporate when the monetisation layer is legally fragile. The subsequent pivot — from transactional gaming platform to creator-led virtual live streaming — took nearly a decade and required a near-complete rebuild of the product surface. Companies that survive regulatory shocks of this magnitude typically do so by holding strong cash reserves and retaining core engineering talent through the restructuring window.
