## One-liner

New Zealand's telco-backed SIM-based NFC mobile wallet, jointly owned by Spark, Vodafone, 2degrees, and Paymark, shut down just 16 months after launch due to insufficient consumer uptake.

## What happened

Semble launched in March 2015 as New Zealand's first national mobile wallet, built on SIM-based NFC technology. The joint venture was owned by the country's four major payments and telco players: Paymark (NZ's largest payment network), Spark, Vodafone, and 2degrees. Two major banks — ASB and BNZ — also participated as issuers. Despite extraordinary institutional backing, consumer adoption never gained critical mass. In July 2016 — just 16 months after launch — CEO Rob Ellis announced Semble would cease contactless payment services, citing "low levels of consumer awareness of mobile payments" and relatively low volumes of contactless terminals outside major retail chains. The Semble app was removed from Google Play in August 2016. A narrow carve-out survived: users paying for public transport via the Snapper card integration could continue, but without the full wallet function.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **March 2015**: Semble launches — simultaneously the most institutionally supported and most technically constrained mobile payment venture in NZ history. SIM-based NFC required telco infrastructure involvement for every transaction.
- **2015–2016**: Apple Pay and Android Pay expand globally using handset-based NFC that bypasses carriers entirely — immediately undercutting Semble's technical architecture without requiring a single NZ policy change.
- **July 2016**: Shutdown announced — Ellis acknowledged that NFC contactless terminal rollout had been slower than modelled and that consumer education requirements were underestimated.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Semble is a textbook case of consortium-led fintech failing to out-manoeuvre platform-native alternatives. The SIM-based approach gave telcos control but created friction: consumers needed compatible handsets, refreshed SIMs, and a specific onboarding flow — while Apple Pay required only a fingerprint. Four co-owners also produced governance overhead and slower iteration cadence. New Zealand's small population (~4.7M in 2015) made consumer education costs disproportionately high relative to addressable scale. The Snapper carve-out surviving as a transit use case suggests that high-frequency, captive-user contexts (public transport) can sustain mobile payments even where general retail cannot.
