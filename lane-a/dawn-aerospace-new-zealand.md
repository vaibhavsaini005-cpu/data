## One-liner

A New Zealand-designed, uncrewed rocket plane built by Dawn Aerospace broke the sound barrier over Mount Cook in November 2024 — the first civil aircraft to fly supersonic since Concorde was retired — after the company spent 2024 clearing the regulatory path to do it.

## What happened

Dawn Aerospace, founded in 2017 in Delft/Christchurch and now headquartered in New Zealand, builds the Mk-II Aurora, a 13-foot, rocket-powered, uncrewed spaceplane designed to fly to the edge of space repeatedly, like an aircraft rather than an expendable rocket. In July 2024, the company received certification from New Zealand's Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) allowing the Mk-II Aurora to fly at supersonic speeds without altitude or speed restrictions — a regulatory milestone very few aerospace companies have secured for a reusable vehicle.

On November 12, 2024, flying out of Glentanner Aerodrome near Aoraki/Mount Cook, the Mk-II Aurora reached Mach 1.1 while climbing to roughly 82,500 feet, making it the first New Zealand-designed aircraft to go supersonic and the first civil (non-military, non-Concorde) aircraft to break the sound barrier since Concorde's retirement. The same flight also set a world climb-rate record, reaching 66,000 feet in 118.6 seconds — beating a mark set by a modified F-15 "Streak Eagle" in the 1970s. Dawn Aerospace's stated long-term goal is to push the Mk-II Aurora to Mach 3.5 and near-space altitudes for repeatable microgravity, atmospheric science, and high-speed test flights.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Chasing certification before the flight, not after**: securing CAA supersonic clearance in July 2024 before attempting the record flight let Dawn fly openly and repeatably rather than as a one-off experimental stunt.
- **Aircraft-like reusability instead of expendable rockets**: designing the Mk-II Aurora to take off and land like a plane, refuel, and fly again is a structurally different bet than most space-launch startups' single-use rocket model.
- **New Zealand as the flight-test base**: using Aoraki/Mount Cook's remote, high-altitude airspace let Dawn run a record-setting supersonic test without the airspace constraints faced by rocket companies operating near populated coastlines.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Dawn Aerospace shows a small, capital-efficient hardware startup achieving a milestone (supersonic flight) that only defense primes and Concorde's operators had previously cleared for civil aviation — done with a 200 kg, uncrewed aircraft rather than a multi-billion-dollar program. It reinforces New Zealand's emergence (alongside Rocket Lab) as a legitimate aerospace hub built on favorable airspace/regulatory access rather than incumbent industrial scale, and it's a useful contrast to failure/pivot stories in this dataset: the success pattern here is regulatory groundwork done deliberately ahead of the technical milestone, not around it.
