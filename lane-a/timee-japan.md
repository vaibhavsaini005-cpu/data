## One-liner

Japan's largest on-demand short-shift staffing platform IPO'd in July 2024 at a ¥46.8 billion offering — the country's biggest IPO of 2024 — after building a two-sided labor marketplace connecting 7 million gig workers with 98,000 businesses.

## What happened

Timee was founded in 2018 by Ryo Ogawa to address Japan's chronic labor shortage, particularly in hospitality, logistics, and retail. Unlike traditional part-time hiring, Timee lets workers book single shifts hours in advance via smartphone — no resume, no interview, no long-term commitment. Employers post shifts and pay only for hours worked; Timee takes a percentage fee.

The platform grew by exploiting a gap traditional staffing agencies had missed: Japan's labor market had rigid hiring norms (full-time or long-term part-time) while a growing segment of workers — students, carers, retirees — wanted extreme flexibility. By 2024, Timee had 7 million registered workers and approximately 98,000 client businesses.

In July 2024, Timee listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange Growth Market, pricing at ¥1,450 per share and raising ¥46.8 billion (~$300M). The listing was Japan's largest IPO of 2024 to that point, with shares closing above the IPO price on debut. For FY2023 (ending October 2023), Timee posted revenue of ¥16.14 billion and operating profit of approximately ¥1.92 billion — marking profitability ahead of its public listing.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **No-resume, instant-booking UX**: Removing the traditional interview barrier dramatically lowered friction on both sides. Businesses trusted the app because background vetting was embedded in the platform.
- **Targeting Japan's structural labor shortage**: Demographic headwinds (aging population, shrinking workforce) created a structural tailwind rather than cyclical demand.
- **Profitability before IPO**: Timee was profitable before listing, reducing the risk premium investors priced in. This contrasts sharply with many gig-economy IPOs globally that listed while still loss-making.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Timee illustrates that gig-economy platforms can succeed in markets with strong worker protections and conservative labor norms — provided the value proposition is positioned as supplemental income and scheduling flexibility rather than as a replacement for traditional employment. Japan's labor culture made Timee a harder sell than many predicted, but the platform aligned with employer incentives (cost control, peak-hour staffing) while giving workers genuine agency over their time.
