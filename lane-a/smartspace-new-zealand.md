## One-liner

Founder and majority shareholder Tim Boyne sold the SmartSpace AI platform to CreativeQ in late 2025 after a sharp falloff in sales from September 2025, then put his holding company SS Holdings into liquidation, leaving investors facing a roughly $3m loss.

## What happened

SmartSpace was a New Zealand-founded enterprise AI platform built on Azure, run through holding company SS Holdings, of which founder Tim Boyne held a 70.98% stake. From September 2025 the business hit a sustained drop in new sales opportunities, which Boyne — as the controlling shareholder and CEO — attributed to intensifying competition in the enterprise AI space and prior under-investment in the platform. Rather than keep funding the shortfall himself, Boyne arranged a sale of the SmartSpace platform and its staff to CreativeQ, which now continues to operate it as "SmartSpace.ai (by CreativeQ)." With no further operating business left inside SS Holdings, the holding company was placed into liquidation; a report published 20 March 2026 put total liquidation debt at $3,067,439, including $2,125,533 owed to Boyne himself as a shareholder loan and $941,906 owed to SAFE-note investors including Icehouse Ventures and Archangel Ventures. Identified recoverable assets in the liquidation were just $10,777.

## Key decisions / turning points

- September 2025: Boyne identifies the sales slowdown as structural rather than temporary, setting up the wind-down rather than a further funding round.
- Late 2025: Boyne negotiates and executes the sale of the SmartSpace platform and its team to CreativeQ, preserving the product and customers outside the failing holding structure.
- Following the sale: Boyne, as director, puts SS Holdings into liquidation with no further trading business inside it, converting his own $2.1m shareholder loan into an unsecured liquidation claim alongside SAFE-note investors.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

This is a founder choosing an asset sale over a cash-call: rather than ask existing SAFE-note investors (Icehouse Ventures, Archangel Ventures) for a bridge round to ride out a sales slowdown, Boyne sold the operating platform to a buyer who could keep it running for customers, then let the now-empty holding company go to liquidation. It's a specific illustration of a pattern in small VC-backed startups — the founder's own capital (his $2.1m loan) sits behind, and is exposed the same way as, outside investor capital once the entity that owes it is wound up, regardless of the fact that he built and ran the company.
