## One-liner

Auckland-founded University of Auckland spin-off that developed Qi-standard wireless power systems and was acquired by Apple in October 2017 as the iPhone maker built out its wireless charging roadmap.

## What happened

PowerbyProxi was founded in 2007 by Fady Mishriki as a spin-out from the University of Auckland. The company specialised in resonant inductive wireless power transfer — the technology behind the Qi standard — and developed it for both consumer electronics and industrial/commercial applications. Its flagship product, the Proxi-Module platform, was a modular wireless power and data system capable of delivering up to 100 watts, adaptable for use in drones, robots, medical devices, and consumer gadgets.

Over a decade PowerbyProxi became one of the few companies globally with deep IP and engineering expertise in wireless power at scale. New Zealand-based venture capital firm Movac was an early backer. The company built industry partnerships and contributed to Wireless Power Consortium (WPC) standards development, giving it influence over the direction of Qi as a global specification.

In October 2017 Apple confirmed it had acquired PowerbyProxi. Apple SVP of hardware engineering Dan Riccio publicly stated Apple's intent: "We're thrilled to have this talented team join Apple to help bring truly effortless charging to more places and more customers around the world." The acquisition price was not disclosed, though investor information suggested the deal was in the upper tens of millions of dollars — a significant exit by New Zealand startup standards.

The acquisition came weeks after Apple launched the iPhone 8 and iPhone X with built-in Qi wireless charging — Apple's first time supporting the standard — and as the company was developing what became MagSafe for iPhone.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Deep-tech focus over consumer product**: PowerbyProxi deliberately built platform IP rather than consumer-facing products, making it acqui-hire-ready for large OEMs with hardware integration needs.
- **WPC standards involvement**: Active participation in Qi standard-setting gave PowerbyProxi credibility and visibility with exactly the kind of companies that would ultimately acquire it.
- **Patient capital from NZ VC**: Movac's long-hold approach supported ten years of IP development without pressure for a premature exit — a model rarely replicable in faster-moving ecosystems.
- **Timing of Apple's wireless charging push**: The acquisition landed at the moment Apple was committing to wireless charging across its entire product line, maximising PowerbyProxi's strategic value.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

PowerbyProxi demonstrates that deep-tech companies in small markets (New Zealand has ~5M population) can reach successful outcomes by becoming the global IP leader in a narrow, technically demanding niche. The company never tried to win in consumer hardware retail — it focused on standards, patents, and platform technology, which made it exactly what Apple needed when Apple decided to own the problem. For New Zealand's tech ecosystem, the Apple acquisition remains one of the most significant deep-tech exits and a model for Kiwi hardware founders.
