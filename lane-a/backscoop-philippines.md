## One-liner

Amanda Cua launched BackScoop from Manila in 2021, aged 19, on under US$30 and a mailing list she built by messaging her own Facebook friends one at a time — and turned it into the default daily read for Southeast Asian founders, VCs and operators, landing on Forbes 30 Under 30 Asia by 2024 while still running a team of three.

## What happened

Cua's university plans fell apart, and in August 2021 — at 19, from Manila, with less than US$30 of startup cost — she launched a newsletter about Southeast Asian startups and tech. The gap she had identified was specific: coverage of the region's tech scene was either too dry, too scattered across sources, or too focused on Western markets for Filipino and regional readers to care about. Her models were Morning Brew and Axios: short, fast, written like a person talking.

Her distribution strategy at the start was entirely manual. "I messaged all of my Facebook friends at the time, one by one, to invite them to subscribe. I had about 2,000 Facebook friends." She wrote and shipped weekly, spending four to six hours per issue, monitoring news sites and PR Newswire and working to a 4–5 p.m. deadline.

The first inflection was not content but channel. By late 2022 BackScoop had over 7,500 readers, and Cua was clear about what changed: "While we had a growing reader base, we didn't post anywhere on social media or have any social media presence. But when we started posting on BackScoop's social media pages, that changed everything — even if we already had a few thousand subscribers." The LinkedIn following eventually passed 30,000 and became a growth engine in its own right.

She deliberately delayed hiring, and was explicit that this was about voice rather than cost: "I didn't want to hire for a while because the way that you process the information, the way you share it, how it's read — it's very controlled by the writer." Her first hire came in January 2023 and her first writer in May 2023. As of the April 2024 interview the team was three people — Cua, one full-time writer and an intern — running a newsletter that had moved from weekly to three times a week.

Monetisation came late by design, roughly two years in, and the product stayed free to readers, funded by advertising inside the newsletter. Cua was candid that she was selling into an immature market: newsletter advertising is "still something very early, especially for newsletters" in Southeast Asia.

By 2024 the audience had passed 10,000 active subscribers on the way to 20,000 within three years, with Singapore the largest readership and Indonesia and the Philippines trading second and third — meaning a Manila-founded publication had become regional rather than national. The inbound flow reversed completely: rather than scraping news sites, Cua was receiving press releases directly from PR firms, venture capital firms and companies announcing news. She extended the franchise into a podcast ("One More Scoop"), a referral programme giving access to a Southeast Asian startup database, and in-person events in Singapore. She was named to Forbes 30 Under 30 Asia 2024 in Media, Marketing & Advertising, and to the Tatler Gen T list.

She has also been open about the cost of the climb: "I gave myself a hard time a lot of the time. I was beating myself up a lot," and on dealing with the region's senior figures, "You always forget that your newsletter or you are the reason that these people even want to meet you."

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Starting with near-zero capital and manual distribution.** Under US$30 and 2,000 individually-messaged Facebook friends. No capital meant no runway pressure and no obligation to grow faster than the audience would bear.
- **Treating social as a distribution layer rather than an afterthought.** By her own account the social channels "changed everything" even on top of an existing subscriber base — the newsletter was the product, but LinkedIn was the acquisition engine.
- **Refusing to hire early, specifically to protect editorial voice.** In a format where the writer *is* the product, Cua treated her own voice as the defensible asset and accepted a slower build to keep it intact for roughly 18 months.
- **Delaying monetisation about two years and keeping the product free.** She grew audience trust first, then sold advertising into a market that barely had a category for it — accepting a harder sale in exchange for not taxing early growth.
- **Letting the newsletter become the wedge for a portfolio.** Podcast, startup database and events all hang off the same audience, turning one newsletter into several surfaces without a second acquisition cost.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

BackScoop is a case for the smallest possible starting footprint in a market everyone else considers too small to serve properly. Southeast Asian tech was covered badly not because nobody could cover it but because the economics never justified a dedicated team — which is precisely why a single 19-year-old with US$30 and a Substack could take the position and hold it. The absence of capital was structurally useful: it forced manual, high-signal distribution and removed any pressure to grow past what the audience actually wanted.

The sharpest transferable decision is the deliberate hiring delay. Most founder advice pushes toward delegation, but Cua correctly identified that in a voice-driven medium the founder's processing and framing *is* the moat, and that handing it off too early would have destroyed the thing people were subscribing for. Knowing which function must stay with the founder — and for how long — is a judgement call that generalises well beyond media.

Finally, the reversal of information flow is the clean marker of when a media business has actually won. BackScoop went from Cua scraping PR Newswire at 4 p.m. to VCs and companies routing their announcements to her directly. For any business whose product is attention or curation, that flip — from chasing inputs to receiving them — is the real milestone, and it arrived before any large funding round did.
