## One-liner

Kerry Express, Thailand's largest private courier, listed in December 2020 at ฿28 a share on the back of a record ฿1.4 billion profit, then chose to defend its market share in a parcel price war against venture-funded Flash Express and J&T by matching prices it could not afford; four years, thirteen consecutive loss-making quarters and more than ฿14 billion of cumulative losses later, its Chinese parent SF Holding bought out minority shareholders at ฿1.50 a share and delisted the company on 15 October 2025.

## What happened

**The setup (2018–2020).** Kerry Express had grown since 2006 as the Thai express-parcel arm of Hong Kong's Kerry Logistics Network (KLN), part of Robert Kuok's Kerry group. Under CEO Alex Ng, who took charge in 2013, it built the country's best-known private parcel brand and, riding Lazada and Shopee's growth, became the largest private-sector courier in Thailand. Two things changed the ground beneath it. In 2018 Komsan Lee and Chinese co-founder Di Weijie founded Flash Express with ฿400 million of seed capital and a simple attack: a flat rate of about ฿25 per parcel against Kerry's roughly ฿60 for comparable service, plus 365-day pickup and delivery including weekends and holidays. Flash was handling about one million parcels a day by 2020. In 2019 Indonesia's J&T Express, backed by the Shopee ecosystem and heavy Chinese capital, entered Thailand with the same low-price playbook. Kerry, still profitable, went ahead with its listing anyway.

**The IPO (December 2020).** Kerry Express priced 300 million shares at ฿28, the top of its range, on 16 December 2020, raising about ฿8.4 billion with institutional books more than 23 times covered and cornerstone demand roughly 10 times covered. Proceeds were earmarked for network expansion, sorting automation to reach 3 million pieces a day by 2023, IT, debt repayment and working capital. On the first day of trading, 24 December 2020, the shares spiked to ฿73 and closed at ฿51.25, 83% above the offer price. Full-year 2020 delivered a record net profit of ฿1.4 billion on revenue of ฿18.92 billion. It would be the company's last profitable year of any consequence.

**The price war (2021–2022).** Rather than cede volume to Flash and J&T, management decided to match them. Kerry's own results release for 2021 describes an "aggressive pricing approach" and frames the collapse in margin as "an investment of short-term margin for long-term leadership." Parcel volume rose a record 30% and monthly active users 32%, yet revenue was flat at ฿18.8 billion and net profit fell 96.7% to ฿47 million, with cost of sales up about 10%. Flash, meanwhile, raised US$150 million from SCB10X and Buer Capital in June 2021 to become Thailand's first unicorn, then a further US$445 million or so in late 2022, and was moving two million parcels a day. J&T was preparing a US$2 billion-plus funding stack and an IPO. Kerry had gone to the public market once for ฿8.4 billion; its rivals could keep going back to private investors for more. By the first half of 2022 Kerry Express was losing ฿1.2 billion and had fallen to the number-two private courier. Full-year 2022 produced a net loss of ฿2.83 billion.

**Retrenchment (2023).** In the first week of January 2023 Ng launched a "Lean Programme": management pay cuts, a headcount freeze, closure of non-performing sites, a pause on non-core ventures, and capital spending on sorting machinery and automation with technology transferred from SF Holding in China and KLN in Hong Kong. Ng told the Nation the investments would "bring Kerry's running costs down immediately." They did not close the gap. Volumes fell 30% year on year in 2023 as e-commerce platforms redirected parcels and post-pandemic shoppers returned to stores, and the net loss widened 37% to ฿3.88 billion on revenue of ฿11.47 billion. The board omitted the dividend. Kerry's answer was to shift its mix toward higher-yield B2B and consumer-to-consumer parcels, which reached about 40% of volume and more than half of revenue by late 2023, in effect ceding the e-commerce volume game to Flash and J&T.

**Ownership passes to SF (2021–2024).** The rescue came from the top of the shareholding chain. SF Holding, China's largest express company, had bought 51.8% of KLN for HK$17.6 billion in September 2021, making it the indirect controller of Kerry Express. In February 2024 KLN distributed its 907.2 million KEX shares (a 52.1% stake) to its own shareholders as a special dividend in specie, and Flourish Harmony Holdings sold its 467.4 million shares (26.82%) to SF International Holding (Thailand), or SFTH. That crossed the mandatory-tender threshold, and SFTH offered ฿5.50 a share for the rest, valuing the offer for up to 73.2% of the company at about ฿7 billion. Kerry Express had to issue a clarification on 10 February 2024 that it had not "yet" been taken over. By the time the tender closed in March 2024 SFTH held 1.09 billion shares, or 62.66%, and Ng said management was "enthusiastic about SF Express becoming our major shareholder." In June 2024 the company was renamed KEX Express (Thailand) and launched a ฿9 billion rights issue of 2.81 billion new shares at ฿3.20, with SF committing to take up its allocation, to repay ฿3.24 billion of debt and provide ฿5.76 billion of working capital. Registered capital went from ฿890 million to about ฿2.3 billion, and SFTH's stake rose to 81.43%.

**The end of the listing (2024–2025).** The money bought time but not a turnaround. KEX lost ฿5.91 billion in 2024, its worst year, on revenue that fell again to ฿9.45 billion. On 12 December 2024 the company announced that Ng would step down on 31 January 2025 after twelve years, with Jiawei Zhang, a 20-year express-industry veteran who had joined the board in November 2024, becoming co-CEO immediately and CEO on 1 February 2025. Losses continued at ฿897 million in Q1 2025 and ฿1.1 billion in Q2 2025 on quarterly revenue of just ฿1.34 billion, roughly a quarter of the 2020 run rate. On 30 April 2025 the board approved SFTH's proposal to delist voluntarily, with a tender for the 651 million shares (18.57%) it did not own at ฿1.50, a 1.35% premium to the prevailing price and 94.6% below the IPO price. The stated reasons were thirteen straight quarters of net losses, liquidity strain, "aggressive pricing strategies by competitors," free float below the exchange minimum, and the constraints of listed status. Shareholders approved on 20 June 2025, the tender closed on 24 September 2025 leaving SFTH with 98.9%, the last trading day was 14 October 2025, and KEX left the Stock Exchange of Thailand on 15 October 2025. Zhang's post-delisting plan folds KEX into SF's 65-country network as a cross-border gateway, adds a same-day premium service, and leans on SF's fulfilment and warehouse software. Meanwhile, the Thai parcel market passed ฿100 billion in 2024, Thailand Post returned to profit, and Flash, after losing ฿2.1 billion in 2022, reported a ฿940 million profit on ฿24.7 billion of revenue in 2024.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Listing into a price war (Alex Ng and the KLN board, December 2020).** The IPO raised ฿8.4 billion at a valuation that assumed 2020's record margins would persist, at the very moment Flash and J&T had shown they would price parcels below cost to take share. Public shareholders bought the peak; private investors in the challengers were still funding losses. The mismatch in who was willing to fund a war of attrition decided the outcome before it started.

- **Choosing to match rather than segment (2021).** The 2021 results release is explicit: aggressive pricing, volume up 30%, profit down 96.7%, sold as a "short-term margin for long-term leadership" trade. Kerry's cost base was built for ฿60 parcels with a premium service level; matching ฿25 parcels meant every incremental order lost money. The company knew it was setting the price it was matching and called it leadership.

- **Cutting costs instead of exiting the fight (Lean Programme, January 2023).** Pay cuts, hiring freezes and automation addressed the cost side of a problem that was really about price and capital. Volumes fell 30% that year anyway, because platforms steer parcels to the cheapest carrier; a leaner Kerry was still the expensive option.

- **Pivoting to B2B and C2C (2023).** Deciding to let e-commerce volume go and rebuild around higher-yield segments was the strategically sound call, but it came two years and ฿6 billion of losses late, after the network had been scaled for a volume it was now giving up.

- **KLN's distribution in specie and SF's tender (February–March 2024).** KLN, itself controlled by SF since 2021, chose to hand its loss-making Thai subsidiary directly to shareholders rather than keep funding it, and SF chose to step in and pay ฿5.50 for control. This converted Kerry Express from a Kuok-family growth story into a Chinese operator's regional node.

- **The ฿9 billion rights issue at ฿3.20 (June 2024).** SF's decision to recapitalise rather than let the company fail kept it alive, but it also diluted minorities on the way down and set up the free-float problem that justified delisting a year later.

- **Delisting at ฿1.50 (SFTH proposal, 30 April 2025).** SF's own reasoning, that public-market constraints made it harder to run a loss-making price competitor, is the mirror image of the 2020 IPO logic. The listing that was supposed to fund the fight had become an obstacle to fighting it.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Kerry Express is the cleanest Southeast Asian example of what happens when an incumbent with a premium cost structure decides to match the prices of venture-funded challengers whose investors are explicitly paying for market share. The incumbent's advantages, brand, service quality, a listed currency, turn out to be worth little when the customer is a marketplace routing parcels by price, and its one-off IPO cannot compete with rivals who can raise a new round every year. The lesson founders and operators keep relearning in delivery, ride-hailing and quick commerce is that a price war is a capital war, and you should only enter one if you know who has more patient money. Kerry did not, and the public shareholders who paid ฿28 in December 2020 were bought out at ฿1.50 in September 2025.

It is also a case about sequencing. The right strategic answer, retreat from commodity e-commerce parcels into B2B, cold-chain, oversized freight and cross-border, was identified in 2021 in the form of partnerships with Betagro, Central Retail and Grab, and then again in 2023 as the B2B/C2C pivot. But each time it was pursued alongside, not instead of, matching the challengers on price, so the loss-making core swamped the profitable niches. The end state, a private subsidiary running Thailand as one node in SF's network, is roughly what Kerry Express could have been from 2021 with several billion baht less lost along the way.
