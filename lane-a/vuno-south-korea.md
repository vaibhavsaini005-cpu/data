## One-liner

Seoul-based VUNO pioneered AI-driven medical imaging diagnostics in South Korea — earning some of the country's earliest FDA and MFDS regulatory clearances for AI devices — and became the first Korean AI healthcare company to list on KOSDAQ in January 2021 (stock code 338220).

## What happened

VUNO was founded in 2014 by Beom-jin Lee, a KAIST-trained researcher, at a time when deep learning applied to medical images was primarily an academic exercise. The company bet early that convolutional neural networks could outperform radiologists on specific narrow tasks — starting with bone age assessment from hand X-rays, a tedious but high-volume task in pediatric endocrinology. That first product, VUNO Med-Bone Age, obtained FDA 510(k) clearance in 2018, making VUNO one of the first Korean companies to clear a healthcare AI device with the US regulator.

From there, VUNO expanded its product suite under the "VUNO Med" brand: chest X-ray triage (detecting findings like pneumonia, pleural effusion), ECG analysis, and EEG seizure detection. Each product addressed a genuine clinical bottleneck — a high-volume, protocol-driven task where AI could deliver consistent read times without radiologist fatigue. Korean hospitals began piloting and then deploying VUNO tools, with the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) granting Korean regulatory approval for several of the products.

In January 2021, VUNO completed an IPO on the KOSDAQ exchange (ticker: 338220), raising capital to fund further clinical validation and international expansion. The listing marked a milestone for the entire Korean AI healthcare sector: it proved a pure-play AI diagnostics company could survive the journey from lab to commercial product to public markets. Post-IPO, the company remained R&D-heavy and loss-making — typical for medical AI companies whose sales cycles involve lengthy hospital procurement and clinical evidence requirements — but continued growing revenue year-on-year and signing new hospital contracts domestically and in Southeast Asia.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Choosing a narrow, measurable starting task (bone age) rather than a broad "read everything" AI was critical. It let VUNO build a clinical validation dossier that regulators could actually evaluate, rather than making unverifiable general claims.
- Early investment in regulatory clearance (FDA + MFDS) rather than bypassing it as a "software tool" gave the company defensible credentialing that competitors without cleared products could not replicate quickly.
- The KOSDAQ IPO in 2021 came during a period of peak enthusiasm for AI in healthcare; the timing allowed VUNO to raise public capital before the sector-wide correction in AI health valuations that followed in 2022–2023.
- Maintaining focus on Korean hospital deployments as the primary revenue base, rather than prematurely burning cash on US expansion, preserved runway while building evidence for future international deals.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

VUNO's story illustrates a sustainable path for medical AI startups outside the US: anchor on a specific, well-defined clinical task, earn regulatory clearance in both your home market and the US, build a reference customer base domestically, then use the KOSDAQ listing as a bridge to longer-term international commercialization. The pattern stands in contrast to the many AI diagnostics companies globally that raised large pre-revenue rounds on broad claims ("AI reads all scans") and collapsed when clinical adoption proved slower than projected. VUNO's continued existence and growing revenue more than a decade after founding reflects the durability of the focused, regulatory-first approach to medical AI.
