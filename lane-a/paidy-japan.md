## One-liner

Japan's BNPL pioneer Paidy was acquired by PayPal for approximately $2.7 billion in October 2021, one of the largest fintech exits ever out of Japan.

## What happened

Founded in 2008 by Russel Cummer, Paidy addressed a persistent gap in Japanese consumer finance: most adults held bank accounts but lacked or avoided credit cards, making online purchases cumbersome. Paidy's two-click checkout let shoppers pay with just an email address and phone number, consolidating all purchases into a single monthly bill payable by bank transfer or at a convenience store.

By 2021 Paidy had built a network of millions of registered users and tens of thousands of merchant integrations — including major fashion, electronics, and subscription services. PayPal, looking to deepen its foothold in Japan (the world's third-largest e-commerce market), announced on 7 September 2021 that it would acquire Paidy for ¥300 billion (~$2.7 billion), principally in cash. The deal closed in October 2021.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **No-card UX from day one.** Rather than competing with credit card rails, Paidy designed around Japan's cash-heavy, bank-transfer-dominant culture — a localisation bet that competitors copying Western BNPL products missed.
- **Merchant-side distribution.** Integrating deeply with merchants (rather than relying solely on consumer pull) gave Paidy transaction volume and stickiness that made it attractive to PayPal as a commerce-enablement play, not just a lending book.
- **Timing the BNPL wave.** The acquisition closed just as global BNPL demand peaked and before the 2022–23 regulatory and funding squeeze hit the sector; Paidy exited at near-maximum valuation.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Paidy demonstrates that "copy Silicon Valley fintech" is not the only path in Japan. Deep localisation — matching payment UX to how ordinary consumers actually manage money — can build enough moat to attract a global acquirer. The deal also underlines that large incumbents (PayPal) will pay a premium for distribution in markets they can't crack organically.
