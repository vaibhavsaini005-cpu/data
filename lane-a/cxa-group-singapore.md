## One-liner

Rosaline Chow Koo founded CXA Group in Singapore in 2013 to rebuild corporate employee benefits around health data, raised about US$58 million from HSBC, B Capital, EDBI and Humanica, sold off its brokerage operations in 2021 and shifted to licensing its technology — and twelve years in, with no new round since 2020, called a shareholder vote to wind the company up voluntarily.

## What happened

CXA Group launched in 2013 as Connexions Asia, founded by Rosaline Chow Koo. The original product was a genuinely ambitious bundle: a single platform combining health insurance, wellness tools, HR support and data analytics, letting employees file claims and access wellness products while giving employers visibility into how their benefits spend was actually being used. The thesis was that corporate health and benefits budgets were being allocated blindly, and that a data layer over them would redirect spending toward prevention.

The company raised approximately US$58 million across twelve years, with an investor list that read as strong validation — HSBC, B Capital, EDBI (the Singapore government's investment arm) and Humanica. Its last round, a Series C, closed in 2020.

What followed was a sequence of strategic contractions rather than a single collapse. In 2021 CXA sold its brokerage operations in Singapore and Hong Kong to Pacific Prime — divesting the distribution business that had connected it to employers. Alongside that, the company shifted its model away from direct distribution and toward licensing its technology to other players. In effect it moved from being the company that owned the customer relationship to being a software supplier to companies that did.

No further funding followed the 2020 Series C. In September 2025 a shareholder meeting was called for September 25 to vote on voluntary liquidation under Singapore's Insolvency, Restructuring and Dissolution Act 2018, with liquidators to settle debts, distribute assets and dissolve the company on approval.

Koo's own framing of the ending was notably unembittered, and treated the technology's survival in other hands as the outcome that mattered: she was "happy to see that the CXA solution lives on throughout Asia through the deals completed" with Pacific Prime, HSBC Life Hong Kong and a China partner. She also cited a personal reason for stepping back — the need to care for elderly family members with healthcare issues.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Selling the Singapore and Hong Kong brokerage operations to Pacific Prime in 2021.** This removed the part of the business that owned the employer relationship. Whatever it did for the balance sheet, it converted CXA from a company with direct customers into one dependent on partners for reach.
- **Shifting from direct distribution to licensing the technology.** Licensing is a lower-revenue, lower-control model that works when you have many eager licensees and defensible IP. Making that move *after* divesting distribution meant the company had no fallback if licensing demand proved thin.
- **Not raising after the 2020 Series C.** Five years between the last round and the wind-up, across a period when insurtech funding broadly contracted, is the quiet constraint behind every subsequent decision. The divestment and the licensing pivot both look like responses to a closed funding window.
- **Choosing voluntary liquidation while the technology still had buyers.** Koo wound the corporate entity down in an orderly way, having already placed the product with Pacific Prime, HSBC Life Hong Kong and a China partner. The company failed; the software was successfully rehomed.
- **The founder's own availability as a factor.** Koo's stated need to care for family members is an unusually honest piece of the record, and a reminder that founder-dependent companies carry founder-life risk.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

CXA is a case study in the order of operations when retrenching. Both of its late moves — divesting brokerage and pivoting to licensing — are defensible individually. Taken in that sequence, they were close to fatal: the company gave up the channel that reached employers and *then* adopted a model that depended on other people's channels. Had the licensing business been proven first and the brokerage sold second, the same two decisions would have left the company in a far stronger position. When runway is short, sequencing retrenchment correctly matters more than picking the right destination.

The second lesson concerns the signalling value of a prestigious cap table. HSBC, B Capital and a sovereign investor (EDBI) is about as strong an insurtech investor set as Southeast Asia offers, and it did not prevent a twelve-year company from winding up without a final round. Investor quality validates the thesis at the time of investment; it does not commit anyone to the next cheque. A five-year gap after a Series C is the operative fact, not the names on the register.

Finally, this is a useful example of the distinction between a company failing and a product failing. The CXA platform is still running inside Pacific Prime, HSBC Life Hong Kong and a China partner. For founders, that reframes what a good ending can look like when the business is not going to make it independently: placing the technology where it will keep operating is a materially better outcome than a shutdown that strands it, even though the corporate entity is dissolved either way.
