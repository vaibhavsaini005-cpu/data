## One-liner

Krisada Kritayakirana and co-founders spent nearly two years designing and manufacturing a purpose-built electric tuk-tuk before launching MuvMi in Bangkok in 2018 to solve the specific "first/last mile" gap between mass transit stations and people's actual destinations — growing the fleet from 350 vehicles in 2023 toward 1,000+ today, carrying over 4 million riders, and expanding in February 2026 into an entirely new mode: electric taxi boats on Bangkok's canals.

## What happened

MuvMi's parent, Urban Mobility Tech, was founded in 2016 by Krisada Kritayakirana (CEO) along with Pipat Tangsiripaisan and Supapong Kitiwattanasak, with support from Thailand's National Innovation Agency. Rather than retrofitting an existing vehicle, the team spent close to two years developing and manufacturing a bespoke electric tuk-tuk body before the service launched in 2018 — a decision to own the hardware, not just the dispatch app. Krisada described the precise gap the company was built to fill: providing "environmentally friendly vehicles for city dwellers who struggle to find public transportation after departing skytrain, subway, and bus stations." That is the first/last-mile problem specifically, not general urban mobility — Bangkok's BTS Skytrain and MRT subway cover trunk routes well but leave riders stranded for the final stretch to their actual destination.

Growth tracked that narrow thesis closely. By early 2023 the fleet stood at 350 electric tuk-tuks across 12 downtown Bangkok neighborhoods, with a stated plan to nearly triple to 1,000 units by the end of that year and reach 4,000–5,000 within five years, backed by a budget of 600–700 million baht from venture capital and partners. The COVID-19 period had forced a detour rather than a shutdown: with ridership down, the company repurposed its tuk-tuk fleet for goods transport instead of passengers, preserving the asset base through the disruption rather than idling or selling it off. By 2023 the platform had carried more than 4 million riders over its five-year history, and by 2024 the fleet had grown past 600 vehicles still concentrated in the same 12 Bangkok neighborhoods — disciplined geographic density rather than premature geographic sprawl.

The company's most recent move, launching in February 2026, extends the same first/last-mile logic to a different transport mode entirely: a taxi boat service on Bangkok's Phadung Krung Kasem canal, booked through the existing MuvMi app. Bangkok's canal network is a transit corridor that cuts through traffic the way the tuk-tuks cannot, and bringing it into the same booking app turns MuvMi from an electric-tuk-tuk operator into a multi-modal micro-transit platform without requiring riders to learn a new system. The broader electric-tuk-tuk sector MuvMi operates in also attracted major capital in 2026, with the Asian Development Bank and Thai energy group Banpu signing a 2.4 billion baht deal to support electric tuktuk deployment in Thailand alongside battery production — signaling the category MuvMi pioneered is now attracting infrastructure-scale investment beyond MuvMi itself.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Building a bespoke electric vehicle rather than launching on existing hardware.** The two-year hardware development delayed MuvMi's launch but gave it a purpose-built vehicle and, per later patent coverage, proprietary vehicle and charging technology — a harder-to-replicate position than a ride-hailing app layered on commodity tuk-tuks.
- **Defining the problem as first/last-mile connection to mass transit, not general urban transport.** This kept MuvMi complementary to Bangkok's BTS/MRT network rather than competing with it, and gave the company a specific, provable use case (the walk from station to destination) rather than a diffuse "better transport" pitch.
- **Repurposing the fleet for goods transport during COVID-19 instead of mothballing it.** Finding an alternative revenue use for the same physical assets preserved the fleet and crew relationships through a demand shock that killed many pure-passenger mobility startups outright.
- **Scaling fleet density within the same 12 neighborhoods for years before expanding geography or mode.** Both the 2023 fleet-tripling plan and the 2024 fleet count stayed within the original service area; the real geographic and modal expansion (new cities, canal boats) came only after density and reliability were established at home.
- **Adding a new transport mode (canal boats) through the existing app rather than launching a separate product.** This leverages the installed user base and brand trust built over seven years of tuk-tuk service for an entirely different vehicle type.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

MuvMi is a disciplined example of solving a narrowly defined transit gap with purpose-built infrastructure, then expanding only after proving density in one area. Many mobility startups chase city count or vehicle count as the headline growth metric; MuvMi instead spent 2018 through at least 2024 deepening coverage of the same 12 Bangkok neighborhoods, which is a strategy that trades a flashier early growth narrative for defensible density and genuinely reliable service in the areas it actually serves.

The canal-boat expansion is the more transferable lesson: the company recognized that its actual asset is not "the electric tuk-tuk" but "the booking and dispatch layer solving first/last-mile mobility," and that asset generalizes to any vehicle type addressing the same underlying problem. For founders who have built a strong position in one vehicle, mode, or format, the MuvMi pattern argues for asking what the real reusable asset is — in this case, the app, the route-optimization logic, and the trust of an existing user base — before assuming growth requires either more of the same vehicle or an entirely new product.
