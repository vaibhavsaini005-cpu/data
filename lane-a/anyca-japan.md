## One-liner

DeNA's peer-to-peer car-sharing marketplace Anyca, Japan's largest of its kind with 500,000+ registered users, shut down on 31 May 2023 after eight years of failing to close the unit-economics gap against cheap, ubiquitous conventional rental.

## What happened

DeNA launched Anyca ("Any car") in October 2015 to tap the low car-utilisation problem in Japan — most privately owned vehicles sit idle more than 95% of the time. The platform let owners list their cars for neighbours and strangers to rent by the hour or day; a bespoke insurance partnership with Mitsui Sumitomo covered each booking, satisfying Japan's strict liability requirements for commercial vehicle use.

Anyca grew steadily to 500,000+ registered users and around 40,000 listed cars — comfortably the country's biggest P2P car marketplace. Yet profitability proved structurally difficult: Japan's dense rail network makes owning a car optional for most urban residents, suppressing casual renter demand; conventional rental from Times Car, Orix, and Nissan Rent-a-Car was cheap and geographically saturated, removing the price-gap that drives P2P adoption elsewhere; and COVID-19 eliminated leisure travel — Anyca's core use case — for two years.

DeNA announced on 28 February 2023 that the service would terminate on 31 May 2023, citing an inability to reach sustainable unit economics.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **2015**: DeNA enters car sharing backed by Mitsui Sumitomo's bespoke P2P insurance product — the enabling infrastructure, without which the service would have been legally unviable.
- **2020–2022**: Pandemic collapse in leisure travel eliminates the highest-value booking category. Unlike food delivery, there is no pandemic-compatible adjacent use to pivot into.
- **2023**: DeNA concludes the structural economics — insurance overhead per booking, low yield per car per day in a market with no rental-supply gap — cannot reach profitability at achievable Japan-only scale.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

P2P mobility markets work best where conventional rental is either expensive or geographically sparse. Japan had neither condition. The Anyca story echoes early Turo struggles in dense US metros: willing supply, genuine product, but no demand-side gap to fill. "Market exists" is not the same as "incumbent market is penetrable." Eight years and 500,000 users was not enough to rewrite the economics.
