## One-liner

Seoul fintech 8Percent had to overhaul its entire lending model and capital structure to survive Korea's 2020 P2P crackdown, emerging as one of only three firms licensed under the new law.

## What happened

8Percent, founded in 2014, built a peer-to-peer lending platform matching individual lenders with borrowers (particularly non-regular-income earners) using an in-house credit-scoring algorithm — an unregulated online P2P lending model common across Korea's fintech scene through the late 2010s.

That model became illegal to run as-is once Korea passed the Act on Online Investment-Linked Financing and User Protection, which took effect August 27, 2020, after a wave of fraud and defaults across the P2P sector. The law banned P2P lenders from re-lending money borrowed from commercial banks, required minimum paid-in capital, and mandated formal registration with the Financial Services Commission within a one-year grace period. The industry's headcount collapsed as a result — the number of marketplace lenders in Korea fell from 237 to 102 between August 2020 and May 2021 alone.

8Percent restructured its balance sheet and compliance function to meet the new capital and disclosure bar, and on June 10, 2021 became one of only three firms (alongside People Fund and Lendit) in the first group licensed by the FSC as an "online investment-linked financing" business — converting from an informal P2P matching site into a regulated lending institution. It went on to raise close to $40 million in October 2021 on the strength of that new licensed status.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Compliance-first restructuring**: Rather than exit the market like most of its 130+ shrinking peer set, 8Percent invested in meeting the FSC's capital and registration bar within the one-year window.
- **Licensing as a moat**: Being one of the first three licensed platforms let 8Percent raise fresh capital immediately after registration, at a moment when unlicensed rivals were shutting down.
- **Continued regulatory pressure**: By 2026, 8Percent was still operating under tightening household-debt rules that cut real-estate-backed loan originations, showing the pivot bought survival, not permanent stability.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

8Percent shows that a sudden regulatory reclassification can function as an extinction event for an entire fintech sub-sector — more than half of Korea's P2P lenders disappeared within nine months of the new law. Surviving required treating compliance itself as the core product pivot, not a side cost: the company effectively became a different, more heavily regulated type of lender to keep operating the same basic service. It's a useful counter-example to failure stories driven by product-market fit — here the market and product were fine, but the legal ground shifted under the entire category at once.
