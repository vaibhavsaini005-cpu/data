## One-liner

Flip started in 2015 as a university students' manual workaround to Indonesia's punishing interbank transfer fees, grew into a Bank Indonesia-licensed payment company with 10 million-plus users, raised a $55M Series B, and is now expanding into cross-border remittances — a textbook zero-to-regulated-fintech growth arc.

## What happened

In 2015, three University of Indonesia students — Rafi Putra Arriyan, Luqman Sungkar, and Ginanjar Ibnu Solikhin — noticed that transferring money between different Indonesian banks incurred Rp 6,500 (~$0.45) per transaction in interbank fees, a meaningful friction for everyday transactions. They built a simple workaround: Flip acted as an intermediary, netting opposing transfers within the same bank, eliminating the need for interbank transactions entirely. Initially the service ran manually via WhatsApp and spreadsheets.

The approach worked because Indonesia's banking system was then fragmented — a customer at Bank Mandiri paying a friend at BCA had to go through BI's interbank switching infrastructure and pay fees at every step. Flip routed around this by aggregating enough volume that most transfers could be settled internally. Bank Indonesia took notice — rather than shutting Flip down, regulators engaged and eventually granted Flip a full money transfer license in 2016, formalizing what had been a regulatory gray area into a supervised payment service.

By 2022 Flip had processed tens of millions of transactions and had 10+ million registered users. Sequoia Capital Southeast Asia led a $55M Series B in January 2022, joined by Insight Partners and existing investors including Sequoia Capital India. The company pivoted from domestic transfers (where BI's FAST payment system eventually made free transfers ubiquitous) to cross-border remittances — a still-fragmented, high-fee market. Flip's cross-border product targets Indonesia's ~5 million migrant workers sending remittances home.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **The manual-first approach (2015)**: Rather than building infrastructure first, the founders operated manually, validating product-market fit before engineering investment — a lean approach that preserved runway and forced deep customer understanding.
- **Proactive Bank Indonesia engagement (2016)**: Many informal fintechs in emerging markets avoid regulators; Flip engaged BI early, securing a formal license that became a competitive moat.
- **Domestic-to-cross-border pivot (2021–2022)**: When BI's SNAP and FAST standards lowered domestic transfer fees industry-wide (commoditizing Flip's original value proposition), Flip pivoted to international remittances — a larger addressable market with still-high fee structures.
- **$55M Series B (2022)**: Gave Flip capital to build cross-border infrastructure, regulatory relationships in destination countries (Singapore, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia), and a consumer brand.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Flip exemplifies the "regulatory arbitrage to regulated operator" journey common in Southeast Asian fintech. The founders started by exploiting a gap in the banking system — then, instead of hiding from regulators, converted that into a licensed business. The domestic pivot also shows strategic timing: recognizing that a commoditizing core product (interbank transfers) should push a company toward the next premium layer (cross-border) before the margin disappears entirely.
