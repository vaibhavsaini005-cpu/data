## One-liner

Hyflux, the Singapore water-treatment champion founded by Olivia Lum with S$20,000 in 1989, won the 2011 Tuaspring desalination tender with a water price roughly a third below the next bidder by betting it could subsidise cheap water with profits from a 411 MW gas power plant it had never operated; Singapore's wholesale electricity price then halved, Tuaspring bled S$81.9 million in 2017, and a S$2.9 billion debt pile collapsed into court protection (May 2018), two failed rescues, judicial management (November 2020) and liquidation (July 2021), wiping out about S$900 million held by some 34,000 retail investors and ending in the criminal trial of Lum and five ex-directors for non-disclosure.

## What happened

**The setup (1989–2010).** Olivia Lum, born in Kampar, Malaysia in 1961 and adopted into poverty, graduated in chemistry from the National University of Singapore in 1986, spent three years as a Glaxo chemist, and in 1989 sold her flat and car to found Hydrochem with S$20,000 and three staff. The company, renamed Hyflux, sold membrane filtration to Singaporean and Chinese industrial customers, listed on SESDAQ in January 2001 as the first water-treatment company on the exchange, moved to the SGX Mainboard in April 2003, and became the poster child of Singapore's "hydrohub" ambitions. It built SingSpring, Singapore's first desalination plant, in 2005, and by 2008 was earning net profit of S$59 million a year on revenue that would exceed S$500 million, with a market capitalisation above S$1.5 billion. Lum was Singapore's first self-made female billionaire on paper, was named Ernst & Young World Entrepreneur of the Year in 2011, the first woman to win it, and sat on national boards. Hyflux's retail bonds and preference shares were treated by ordinary Singaporeans as a blue-chip proxy for the national water story.

**The bet (2010–2011).** In 2010 the Public Utilities Board (PUB) tendered Singapore's second and much larger desalination plant at Tuas, 70 million gallons (318,500 cubic metres) a day, on a 25-year design-build-own-operate contract to 2038. Hyflux's bid was S$0.45 per cubic metre in the first year. The next-lowest bid, from Keppel Seghers, was S$0.67; the existing SingSpring price was S$0.78. Hyflux could undercut everyone because its bid was not really a water bid: it proposed to build an integrated water-and-power plant, a 411 MW combined-cycle gas turbine alongside the desalination trains, and to cover the shortfall on water by selling most of the electricity into Singapore's liberalised wholesale market. Hyflux had never operated a merchant power plant. The S$1.05 billion project was awarded in April 2011. Two days later, on 13 April 2011, Hyflux launched a S$200 million offer of 6% cumulative preference shares that was upsized to S$400 million on heavy retail demand. Prosecutors later alleged that the offer information statement did not tell investors that Tuaspring was an expansion into a new electricity business whose profitability depended on power sales.

**The slow leak (2013–2017).** The desalination half of Tuaspring came on line in 2013. The power plant, which was supposed to fund the whole structure, was delayed until August 2015 and only began commercial electricity sales in February 2016. By then the thesis had broken: a wave of new generating capacity had produced oversupply in Singapore's power market, and wholesale electricity prices fell by more than half between 2015 and 2017. Every megawatt-hour Tuaspring sold lost money, and the water contract, priced to lose money on its own, had no cushion. Governance analyst Mak Yuen Teen later documented that Hyflux's operating cash flow had been negative every year since FY2010, reaching minus S$422 million in FY2013, and that total liabilities more than tripled between FY2010 and FY2017. The company kept the balance sheet looking healthy by issuing instruments classified as equity: in May 2016 it sold S$500 million of 6% perpetual securities, upsized from S$300 million, to retail investors through DBS, POSB and OCBC ATMs with a S$2,000 minimum. About S$329 million of that retail money went to redeem S$475 million of 2014 perpetuals that had been sold only to institutions. Net profit fell from S$59 million in 2008 to S$3.8 million in 2016, and in 2017 the group reported a S$115 million loss, with Tuaspring alone posting an operating loss of S$81.9 million before interest and tax.

**The break (May 2018–May 2019).** On 21 May 2018 Hyflux suspended its shares and the next day applied for court protection under Section 211B of the Companies Act, freezing S$2.9 billion of liabilities including S$900 million of perpetual securities and preference shares held by roughly 34,000 mostly retail investors. It tried to sell Tuaspring, but the plant's negative value and PUB's contractual controls meant every bidder needed the regulator's blessing and none closed. On 19 October 2018 Hyflux signed a rescue with SM Investments, a consortium of Indonesia's Salim Group and Medco Group, for a S$400 million equity injection for a 60% stake plus a S$130 million shareholder loan. The scheme would have paid perpetual and preference holders a fraction of face value in cash and shares, and on 30 March 2019 hundreds of them rallied at Hong Lim Park in a rare public protest. On 4 April 2019 Hyflux terminated the SM Investments agreement, accusing the consortium of repudiating it; SM Investments blamed Hyflux for failing to satisfy conditions. On 17 April 2019 PUB served notice terminating the Tuaspring water purchase agreement, and on 18 May 2019 it took over the desalination plant for zero dollars, waiving the compensation it was owed because the plant's value was negative and Tuaspring could not pay, "to safeguard Singapore's water security." Maybank, Tuaspring's secured lender, took control of the stranded power plant.

**The long tail (2019–2022).** A second white knight, UAE utility Utico, signed in July 2019 for an 88% stake for S$400 million, valuing the equity at S$340 million and offering retail holders 50% of their first S$2,000 to S$3,000 plus a staggered cascade. It stalled for over a year on governance disputes and creditor objections; an unsecured working group and a steering committee representing 73% of senior unsecured debt voted against it. Bank lenders applied for judicial management in August 2020, a last-minute suitor called Pison Investments surfaced in October 2020, and on 19 November 2020 the High Court placed Hyflux under judicial managers Hamish Christie and Patrick Bance of Borrelli Walsh. Lum, who had resigned as CEO in 2020, was gone. After six final offers failed to produce a viable restructuring, the judicial managers applied for winding up in June 2021, and on 21 July 2021 the High Court ordered Hyflux liquidated. Unsecured creditors and the perpetual and preference holders were expected to recover cents on the dollar. The Tuaspring power plant was sold to YTL PowerSeraya in June 2022 for S$270 million, with proceeds going mainly to secured creditors.

**The reckoning (2022–2026).** On 17 November 2022 Lum, former CFO Cho Wee Peng and four former independent directors were charged under the Securities and Futures Act; Lum picked up three more Companies Act charges in May 2023. Former independent director Rajsekar Kuppuswami Mitta pleaded guilty in August 2023 and was fined S$90,000; Lee Joo Hai was arrested in Malaysia in March 2023. The remaining six, Lum, Cho, Teo Kiang Kok, Christopher Murugasu, Gay Chee Cheong and Lee, went to trial on 11 August 2025 in what Singapore media called the country's biggest corporate scandal. Prosecutors' core allegation is that the 2011 preference share offer and Hyflux's SGX filings failed to disclose that Tuaspring was "Hyflux's expansion into a new business of selling electricity" and that the project's profitability "was contingent on electricity sales revenue." Lum faces up to seven years' jail on the offer charge. Hearings were scheduled to run 56 days to 5 February 2026; as of this entry's date the outcome of the trial could not be verified from checkable public sources and is not asserted here.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Bidding S$0.45 per cubic metre and funding the gap with merchant power (Olivia Lum and the Hyflux board, 2010–2011).** This is the whole case. Hyflux won a 25-year water contract by cross-subsidising it with a business it had no experience in, exposed to a commodity price it did not control. The bid was structurally a leveraged long position on Singapore's wholesale electricity price. When PUB and analysts called it an "impossibly low" bid, they were describing the risk, not the skill.

- **Building the power plant as the profit centre but disclosing it as a support facility (2011 offer information statement, SGX filings).** Investors were told, according to the prosecution, that Tuaspring was primarily a desalination project with a power plant to run it. The economic reality was the reverse. Whether or not the criminal charges stick, the gap between the disclosed model and the real model is what turned a bad project into a legitimacy crisis.

- **Refinancing institutional debt with retail "equity" (May 2016).** Issuing S$500 million of 6% perpetuals through ATMs to redeem 2014 perpetuals that regulators had deemed suitable only for institutions moved the risk from professional holders to households, kept the instruments off the debt line, and bought about two years. Mak Yuen Teen's phrase was that "ordinary retail investors essentially bailed out the institutional investors." This decision is why the collapse became a national political event rather than a bank workout.

- **Delaying the power plant into a price collapse (2013–2016).** The desalination trains were on time in 2013, but the electricity that was supposed to pay for them did not flow until February 2016, straight into a market where prices had halved. A two-year slip on the revenue-bearing half of an integrated asset removed whatever margin of safety the original bid had.

- **Terminating the SM Investments deal (4 April 2019).** With retail holders in open revolt over a scheme that paid them a fraction of face value, Hyflux walked away from the only funded rescue on the table rather than force the vote. Thirteen days later PUB seized Tuaspring for nothing. The company never again had a bidder willing to buy the whole group, and every subsequent proposal, Utico's included, offered less.

- **PUB's decision to take the plant for zero and waive compensation (17 April–18 May 2019).** The regulator prioritised water security over creditor recovery, was legally entitled to do so under the water purchase agreement, and in doing so fixed the value of Hyflux's flagship asset at nil. For every future infrastructure concession bidder in Singapore, this set the precedent that the state will not backstop an aggressive bid.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Hyflux is the cleanest case in the region of winning a long-dated contract by pricing one product below cost and betting on an adjacent, uncontrolled market to make up the difference. Operators recognise the pattern from anchor-tenant retail, loss-leader logistics contracts and infrastructure bids that assume ancillary revenue: the headline win looks like competitive brilliance until the subsidising line moves against you, and a 25-year commitment leaves no way to reprice. The second pattern is capital structure as narrative management. Perpetual securities and preference shares let Hyflux report equity where lenders would have seen debt, and selling them to households through ATMs bought time at the cost of converting a corporate failure into a public-trust failure with criminal consequences for the board. Third, it shows how a national-champion halo, EY World Entrepreneur of the Year, seats on national boards, a founder story every Singaporean knew, suppresses the scrutiny that a first-time merchant power operator would otherwise have faced from its own directors, its bankers and its regulator. The lesson founders and operators should take is not "avoid infrastructure" but that a strategic bet whose payoff depends on a price you do not set must be disclosed as exactly that, sized so that it can be lost, and never funded with money from people who think they are buying the safe version of your company.
