## One-liner

South Korean online-class marketplace Class101 pivoted from per-class purchases to a subscription model in 2022, but revenue kept sliding into 2023 — triggering full capital impairment, unpaid rent, and three separate rounds of restructuring by early 2024.

## What happened

Class101 built a marketplace where independent creators sold pre-recorded video classes (crafts, illustration, cooking, and more) directly to consumers, raising a $26M Series B in September 2021 to fund growth. In 2022 the company undertook a major business-model pivot, moving roughly 300 staff through a six-month transition from one-off class purchases (priced around ₩200,000-300,000 each) to a subscription product, "Class101+", offering unlimited access to 6,000+ classes for about ₩19,000/month.

The pivot did not fix the underlying economics fast enough. For fiscal 2022, Class101 reported revenue of ₩65.6 billion, down 24% year-over-year, an operating loss of ₩29 billion (up roughly 70%), and a net loss of ₩29.5 billion, leaving the company in full capital impairment with negative equity of ₩14.5 billion. In February 2023, Class101 restructured its offline division and reassigned recruitment staff to unrelated roles. By July 2023 it was taking voluntary resignations from around 100 employees, on top of an earlier ~50-person reduction that February. By August 2023 the company had reportedly failed to pay three months of office rent. A third restructuring round followed in early 2024. The company survived and later launched "Creator Home," a SaaS layer for creators covering streaming, membership, merchandise and community, reportedly reaching its first annual profit in 2024 after sustained cost-cutting.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Subscription pivot (2022) was structurally sound but executed under financial pressure**: converting a 300-person org to a new pricing model in six months is a genuine operational achievement, but it didn't arrive early enough to prevent the 2022 revenue collapse.
- **Three separate restructuring rounds (Feb 2023, Jul 2023, early 2024)** rather than one decisive cut, suggests each round underestimated how much cost reduction was actually needed.
- **Capital impairment became public and severe** (missed rent payments by August 2023) before the company stabilized — a near-failure event that a weaker balance sheet or less patient investor base likely would not have survived.
- **Later diversification into a creator-tooling SaaS product (Creator Home, 2025)** moved Class101 further from a pure content marketplace toward creator infrastructure, a second pivot layered on top of the subscription pivot.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Class101 illustrates that a well-executed business-model pivot (marketplace → subscription) is necessary but not sufficient — it bought time rather than immediate recovery, and the company still had to survive a period of genuine insolvency risk (full capital impairment, unpaid rent) before cost discipline caught up with the new model. It's a useful counter-example to narratives that treat "pivoted to subscription" as an automatic fix: the mechanics of the pivot mattered less here than whether the underlying unit economics and cost base were resized to match a much smaller, lower-ARPU revenue line.
