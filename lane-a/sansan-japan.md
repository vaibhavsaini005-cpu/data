## One-liner

Japanese B2B SaaS pioneer that turned the uniquely Japanese meishi (business card) exchange ritual into a TSE-listed enterprise contact platform, then expanded into invoice automation and consumer networking.

## What happened

Sansan was founded in 2007 by Chikahiro Terada in Tokyo with a deceptively narrow premise: digitize Japan's ritual of physical business card exchange and connect it to enterprise contact management. Despite the niche entry point, the product addressed a genuine pain felt across every Japanese corporation — thousands of meishi cards accumulated per employee with no searchable database. Sansan scaled the product into a full CRM/SaaS suite and listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE Mothers) in June 2019, raising approximately ¥13.8 billion (~$128M) — one of Japan's most significant enterprise SaaS IPOs at the time. Post-IPO, Sansan expanded into adjacent markets with Bill One (paperless invoice management SaaS launched 2020) and maintained the consumer-facing Eight app for individual business networking.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Resisted early pressure to go global, building deep product-market fit in Japan before any international moves — cultural specificity was a moat, not a limitation
- Used meishi digitization as the wedge to capture enterprise contact graph data, then layered CRM features on top of data the company already held
- Launched Bill One during COVID-19 (2020) to capture sudden enterprise demand for paperless invoice processing, turning regulatory pressure (Japan's e-invoicing push) into growth
- Maintained a discipline toward profitability pre-IPO, avoiding the "growth at all costs" pattern that destabilized many contemporaries

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Sansan is the canonical example of finding a culturally specific wedge that outside observers would dismiss ("who builds a business card app?") and using it to enter a much larger enterprise software market. It also disproves the assumption that Japan's enterprise market cannot produce SaaS-native companies — deep regulatory and cultural alignment can substitute for the network effects that US SaaS firms rely on.
