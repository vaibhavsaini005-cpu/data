## One-liner

Toshiba, the 1875-founded conglomerate that invented flash memory and once employed more than 140,000 people, paid $5.4 billion for the US nuclear builder Westinghouse in 2006, hid the deterioration of that and other businesses behind 151.8 billion yen of overstated profits from 2008 to 2014, then absorbed a 712.5 billion yen (about $6.3 billion) nuclear write-down and a 965.7 billion yen loss in fiscal 2016 that left it with negative net worth; to survive it sold its memory-chip crown jewel to a Bain-led group for 2 trillion yen and issued 600 billion yen of shares to activist hedge funds, spent six years at war with those new owners, was caught colluding with the trade ministry to suppress their votes, saw two chief executives and a chairman forced out, had a break-up plan rejected by shareholders in March 2022, and was finally taken private by a Japan Industrial Partners consortium for about 2 trillion yen and delisted on 20 December 2023 after 74 years, while the memory business it sold, now Kioxia, became Japan's most valuable listed company in June 2026.

## What happened

**The setup (2006–2014).** Toshiba entered the 2010s as one of Japan's flagship conglomerates: laptops, televisions, elevators, medical scanners, power plants, and the NAND flash memory it had invented in the 1980s. On 17 October 2006 it completed the purchase of a 77% stake in Westinghouse Electric for $5.4 billion, a bet by then-president Atsutoshi Nishida that a global "nuclear renaissance" would make reactor construction a growth business. The 2011 Fukushima disaster ended that story in Japan and slowed it everywhere else, but Toshiba's reported results did not reflect the damage. As a third-party committee later found, from 2008 the company's presidents, Nishida, then Norio Sasaki, then Hisao Tanaka, set profit targets known internally as "challenges" that divisions could only meet by front-loading revenue on long-term contracts under the percentage-of-completion method, deferring loss provisions on infrastructure projects, and pushing costs into later periods in the digital products and semiconductor units. The committee described a culture in which "management decisions could not be challenged" and employees "were pressured into inappropriate accounting by postponing loss reports."

**The first crisis (2015).** In February 2015 the Securities and Exchange Surveillance Commission received a whistleblower's email. Toshiba announced an internal inquiry in May, and on 20 July 2015 the independent committee reported that operating profit had been overstated by 151.8 billion yen (about $1.2 billion) over seven years with the knowledge of top management; later tallies put the total at 156.2 billion yen. Tanaka resigned the next day calling it "the most damaging event for our brand in the company's 140-year history," and Sasaki and Nishida left their advisory posts with him; eight senior officials went in all. Regulators fined the company 7.37 billion yen. By December 2015 about $8 billion had been wiped from Toshiba's market value and it forecast a 550 billion yen loss for the fiscal year. The reforms that followed, a majority-independent board, a new audit structure, and Satoshi Tsunakawa as president from 2016, treated the problem as one of accounting discipline. The nuclear business, where the losses actually sat, was left in place.

**The second crisis (2016–2017).** The trigger was a deal Westinghouse did in December 2015: it took over CB&I Stone & Webster, the construction contractor building its four AP1000 reactors at Vogtle in Georgia and V.C. Summer in South Carolina, to end a dispute over cost overruns. Toshiba later admitted it had overestimated the value of what it bought; the projects were years late and billions over budget, and the acquisition turned Westinghouse from a supplier into the party liable for finishing them. On 14 February 2017 Toshiba postponed its third-quarter results, disclosed a 712.5 billion yen (about $6.3 billion) write-down of the nuclear business and a projected 390 billion yen corporate loss, and accepted the resignation of chairman Shigenori Shiga, who had run Westinghouse. It missed the audited filing again in March. On 29 March 2017 Westinghouse filed for Chapter 11 in New York with $9.8 billion of liabilities, and Toshiba raised its expected annual loss to about 1 trillion yen. The audited fiscal-2016 result, finally published in August 2017, was a net loss of 965.7 billion yen (about $8.7 billion) and negative net worth of 552.9 billion yen; the Tokyo Stock Exchange demoted the shares to its second section and gave the company until 31 March 2018 to restore positive equity or be delisted.

**The rescue and its price (2017–2018).** Toshiba had two assets big enough to close a 750 billion yen equity hole. The first was Toshiba Memory, its NAND flash business, the world's second-largest. On 20 September 2017 the board approved its sale to a consortium led by Bain Capital, with SK hynix, Apple, Dell and Seagate as investors, for 2 trillion yen (about $18 billion), a deal fought by joint-venture partner Western Digital through arbitration and delayed by antitrust review until it closed on 1 June 2018, leaving Toshiba with a 40.2% stake alongside Bain's vehicle at 49.9% and Hoya at 9.9%. Because the sale would not close in time, Toshiba's board on 19 November 2017 chose the second route: a 600 billion yen (about $5.4 billion) private placement of 2.28 billion new shares at 262.8 yen, a 10% discount, to more than 30 overseas investors through some 60 funds, including Effissimo Capital Management, Third Point, Oasis, Cerberus and Elliott. The placement diluted existing holders by about 35%, cost 26 billion yen in fees, lifted Effissimo to 11.34% as the largest shareholder, and handed roughly a third of the company to activist funds whose business model was to force change. Toshiba returned to positive equity by March 2018 and kept its listing, but the shareholder register it now had was the root of the next five years. Along the way it shed the PC business to Sharp in June 2018 for $36 million and the last of it in 2020; headcount fell from 141,256 in March 2018 to 116,224 by 2022.

**The governance war (2019–2022).** Nobuaki Kurumatani, a former banker and former head of CVC Capital's Japan business, became chief executive in 2018 with a mandate to repair relations with investors and rebuild the company around energy, infrastructure and digital services. Instead, relations broke. At the July 2020 annual meeting Kurumatani was re-elected with a thin majority and shareholder proposals from Effissimo were defeated; Effissimo suspected the count and the process, and in March 2021 shareholders voted, against the board's wishes, to commission an independent investigation. On 7 April 2021 CVC approached Toshiba with a roughly 2 trillion yen (about $20 billion) take-private proposal that would have kept incumbent management in place; the bid was widely read as a way to shield Kurumatani from his shareholders, and on 14 April 2021 he resigned, with Tsunakawa returning as chief executive. The investigators' report, published on 10 June 2021 after reviewing some 800,000 emails, found that Toshiba had worked with the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry to pressure activist shareholders including Effissimo and Harvard's endowment before the 2020 vote, invoking Japan's foreign investment law, and that the meeting "was not fairly managed." At the annual meeting on 25 June 2021 shareholders removed board chairman Osamu Nagayama and audit committee member Nobuyuki Kobayashi, a rare defeat for an incumbent board in Japan. Management's answer, announced on 12 November 2021, was to split Toshiba into three listed companies; investors led by 3D Investment Partners and Effissimo objected that a break-up avoided the sale that would maximise value. The plan was revised on 7 February 2022 to a two-way split plus the sale of the elevator and lighting units and the Kioxia stake, which provoked internal dissent that the remainder would be "low-margin businesses." Tsunakawa resigned on 1 March 2022 and Taro Shimada, a former Siemens executive who had joined in 2018, took over. On 24 March 2022 an extraordinary meeting rejected both the split and 3D's counter-proposal to solicit buyout offers, leaving the company with no approved strategy.

**The end of the public company (2023–2024).** Shimada's board ran a formal sale process. On 23 March 2023 it accepted an offer from a consortium led by Japan Industrial Partners, a domestic private-equity firm, joined by 17 Japanese companies and six banks including Rohm, Orix and Chubu Electric Power, valued at about 2 trillion yen (roughly $14 billion); the price was below what the activists had hoped for, but it offered a Japanese owner and an exit at a premium. The tender ran from August and closed on 20 September 2023 with 78.65% of shares accepted; Effissimo and the other funds sold. A November extraordinary meeting approved the squeeze-out, and on 20 December 2023 Toshiba was delisted, its last close at 4,590 yen. In May 2024 the new owners announced up to 4,000 domestic job cuts, about 6% of the Japanese workforce, through voluntary retirement of staff over 50, a move of head-office functions from Tokyo to Kawasaki, a 10% operating-margin target within three years and an intention to relist in about five. Shimada called it "a necessary thing to do to ensure the company survives for the next 100 years." The coda came from the business Toshiba had been forced to sell: Kioxia listed in December 2024 and in June 2026 became Japan's largest company by market capitalisation, and Bain's Yuji Sugimoto told Nikkei that "Kioxia of today wouldn't exist" under Toshiba's ownership.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Buying Westinghouse for $5.4 billion at the peak of the nuclear cycle (Atsutoshi Nishida, October 2006).** The original bet. It concentrated the group's growth thesis in a business with decade-long, fixed-price construction risk that Toshiba had never managed, and it sat on the balance sheet at a value that only made sense if the renaissance arrived.

- **Setting profit "challenges" instead of disclosing the shortfall (Nishida, Sasaki and Tanaka, 2008–2014).** The choice to manage the numbers rather than the businesses meant the board, auditors and shareholders learned of the problems seven years late and all at once. It also meant that the 2015 reforms fixed accounting while leaving the loss-making assets untouched, which is why a second, larger crisis followed within two years.

- **Letting Westinghouse absorb CB&I Stone & Webster (Westinghouse management under chairman Shigenori Shiga, December 2015).** Taking over the contractor converted a supplier's dispute into an owner's liability for two of the most over-budget construction projects in the United States. It was the single decision that turned a troubled subsidiary into a $6.3 billion write-down and a Chapter 11 filing.

- **Selling Toshiba Memory and issuing 600 billion yen of stock to hedge funds to keep the listing (Tsunakawa and the board, September–November 2017).** The company chose to survive as a listed entity at the cost of its best business and a third of its equity. The alternative, a court-supervised restructuring or a delisting, might have been cheaper for the company but not for management and lenders. Every subsequent conflict flowed from the shareholder base created that November.

- **Colluding with METI to pressure shareholders before the 2020 vote (Kurumatani's management, 2020).** The decision to treat activist owners as a threat to be managed with state help rather than investors to be answered ended Kurumatani's career, cost the chairman his seat and destroyed the board's ability to propose any strategy on its own authority.

- **Proposing a break-up rather than a sale (Tsunakawa's board, November 2021 to March 2022).** Management's last attempt to keep the group in its own hands. Its rejection on 24 March 2022 was the moment control passed to the shareholders, and within a year the company was sold.

- **Accepting a domestic private-equity buyer at a modest price (Shimada's board, March 2023).** Taking JIP's roughly 2 trillion yen rather than holding out for more bought Toshiba a Japanese owner, a clean register and the privacy to cut 4,000 jobs. It also fixed the final scorecard: shareholders who had bought at 262.8 yen in 2017 did well, and the company that remained was a fraction of the one that had bought Westinghouse.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Toshiba is the reference case for what happens when a company solves a solvency problem with the wrong kind of capital. The 2017 placement was fast and it worked, but it converted a governance-light Japanese conglomerate into a company one-third owned by funds whose only tool was the shareholder vote, without anyone on the board having decided that this was the ownership they wanted. Six years of stalemate, two forced resignations, an ousted chairman and a rejected strategy were the interest on that loan. Founders and operators will recognise the pattern at any scale: emergency money taken from investors whose objectives differ from management's does not end the emergency, it schedules the next one. The second lesson is that fixing disclosure is not the same as fixing the business. Toshiba's 2015 reforms were real, but the nuclear liabilities they exposed were left in place, and the CB&I deal that detonated them was done in the same month the new board took office. The third is the cost of a bad divestiture: Toshiba sold its highest-growth asset because it was the most saleable, not the least strategic, and the market has since valued that asset above the whole of the company that sold it. When a group is forced to sell, what goes first is what buyers want, and the remainder is by construction what nobody wanted.
