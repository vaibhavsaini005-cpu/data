## One-liner

South Korea's dominant e-commerce platform rebuilt from a Groupon clone into the country's vertically integrated Amazon equivalent, raising $4.55 billion in the largest U.S. listing by a Korean company in March 2021.

## What happened

Coupang was founded in June 2010 by Bom Suk Kim, a Harvard Business School dropout, initially as a Korean clone of Groupon — aggregating local restaurant and service deals with deep discounts. By 2012–2013, Kim recognized that flash deals were a poor structural moat and that South Korea's densely populated urban geography offered a genuine logistics advantage. Coupang rebuilt the business from the ground up as a vertically integrated e-commerce platform, constructing its own warehouse and last-mile delivery infrastructure rather than relying on third-party couriers. The centrepiece was "Rocket Delivery": items ordered by midnight, including fresh groceries, would arrive by 7 a.m. — a service level no incumbent could match. SoftBank's Vision Fund invested $1 billion in 2015 and a further $2 billion in 2018. Total pre-IPO funding reached approximately $3.4 billion. By 2020, Coupang reported $12 billion in annual revenue. On 11 March 2021, it listed on the NYSE at $35 per share (ticker: CPNG), opening at $63.50 and reaching an intraday market cap of approximately $84 billion — the largest listing by a Korean company outside Korea.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **2012 pivot away from daily deals**: Rather than optimising a fading Groupon-style model, Kim abandoned the original premise entirely and rebuilt around a logistics thesis — an unusually clean and early strategic break.
- **Own the last mile**: Coupang employed its own "Coupang Friends" delivery drivers rather than outsourcing, enabling the Rocket Delivery SLA at the cost of compressed margins — a sustained multi-year bet that scale would eventually justify the fixed cost.
- **Fresh food and grocery**: Adding perishables to the platform validated the speed-critical value proposition in the category where delivery timing matters most, and drove repeat purchase behaviour that discretionary goods alone cannot sustain.
- **NYSE listing over KOSPI**: Listing in New York rather than Seoul accessed deeper capital pools and global institutional investors at a time when the Korean exchange was perceived as undervaluing growth-stage tech.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Coupang demonstrates that dense urban markets with high smartphone penetration and concentrated delivery corridors can support next-day logistics standards that would be uneconomical in more dispersed geographies. The "own the customer relationship from warehouse to doorstep" model trades near-term margin for long-term defensibility — a playbook Amazon proved in the U.S. and Coupang executed in a single-country, hyper-dense context. It also shows that large SoftBank Vision Fund capital deployed into physical logistics infrastructure — warehouses, hired drivers, fleet — can build genuine competitive moats rather than simply subsidising unsustainable unit economics through growth marketing.
