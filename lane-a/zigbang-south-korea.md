## One-liner

Zigbang, South Korea's largest proptech platform and one-time $1.8 billion unicorn, saw its valuation collapse to roughly one-eighth of its peak after Korea's housing transaction volumes halved in 2022, a poorly-timed $77M acquisition of Samsung SDS's IoT unit saddled it with depreciation charges, and operating losses reached 37.1 billion won — forcing layoffs and an indefinitely deferred IPO.

## What happened

Founded in 2012, Zigbang built South Korea's leading apartment and villa rental listing platform, charging advertising fees to licensed real estate agents (junggaeop) who listed jeonse and monthly-rent (wolse) properties. Jeonse — a uniquely Korean tenancy structure where tenants pay a large lump-sum deposit held by the landlord rather than monthly rent — created specialised search and contract requirements that general listing platforms hadn't addressed. Zigbang dominated this niche, becoming the go-to resource for renters navigating Korea's opaque residential market.

By 2021 the company had raised at a 2.5 trillion won ($1.8B) valuation in a pre-IPO Series E, cementing unicorn status. In January 2022 Zigbang acquired Samsung SDS's smart home IoT division for roughly 100 billion won, planning to pivot from a pure listings marketplace toward a connected residential services platform — the thesis being that its data advantage on Korean residential properties could anchor smart home hardware subscriptions and property management services.

The bet collapsed. The Bank of Korea raised interest rates aggressively through 2022, and Korean housing transaction volumes halved from 1.01 million in 2021 to 508,790 — directly decimating Zigbang's agent-advertising revenue, which was nearly entirely transaction-volume-linked. The Samsung IoT acquisition added large depreciation charges on top of collapsing revenue. Operating losses climbed from 8.2 billion won in 2021 to 37.1 billion won in 2022, and net losses surged 296% to 51.5 billion won. In April 2023, Zigbang announced layoffs affecting approximately 10% of its workforce. The company's valuation fell to roughly one-eighth of its 2021 peak, and IPO plans were abandoned.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Acquisition at the top**: The Samsung SDS IoT acquisition was announced in January 2022, just as Korea's property market was beginning to freeze. Rather than diversifying revenue, the IoT unit required ongoing depreciation and couldn't generate standalone revenue before liquidity conditions tightened sharply.
- **Ad-fee model concentration**: Zigbang's near-total dependence on transaction-volume-linked agent fees left it with no revenue buffer during any property downturn — a structural vulnerability shared with rival Dabang, but magnified by Zigbang's unicorn-scale cost base and the debt service on the Samsung acquisition.
- **IPO ambition colliding with market reality**: The company had been positioning for a public listing through 2022, but losses and collapsed market conditions made pricing impossible. The delay destroyed the exit path for earlier-stage investors who had been in since the 2016-2018 vintage.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Zigbang illustrates the "platform company acquires adjacency at the top of a cycle" failure pattern in its clearest Korean form. The Samsung IoT bet made strategic sense in a bull market but became a financial anchor in a bear one. Beneath that, the case captures how Korea's jeonse-specific real estate market concentrated proptech risk in a way that non-Korean investors may not have fully priced: the entire sector's revenue rose and fell with a single, rate-sensitive residential transaction mechanism rather than with a diversified demand curve. Any proptech model solely funded by agent listing fees is, structurally, a leveraged bet on housing transaction volume — invisible when volumes are rising, catastrophic when they halve in a year.
