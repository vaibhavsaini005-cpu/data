## One-liner

James Rijanto and Donald Wono started Jamanow as a social-commerce platform giving housewives and working mothers an income opportunity, and two months into Indonesia's 2020 COVID-19 lockdown — watching demand for daily necessities spike — pivoted the whole company into Pasarnow, a fresh-grocery supply chain business, which went on to raise a $3.3 million seed round and grow to 1,000+ farmer and supplier partners.

## What happened

Jamanow's original idea, in Rijanto's own words, was "to make a startup with a social commerce business model" — using social selling to give housewives and working mothers a flexible income channel. It was a reasonable, well-populated category, and nothing in the record suggests the original product had failed outright.

What changed the founders' minds was an external shock they chose to read as a market signal rather than a temporary spike: about two months after Indonesia's COVID-19 lockdown began in 2020, Rijanto and Wono saw sustained, unusually strong demand for daily necessities — specifically fresh groceries — moving through whatever channels could deliver them reliably. Rather than treating that as noise to wait out, they treated it as validation of a much larger addressable problem than the one Jamanow was solving, and pivoted the company's entire model. Cindy Ozzie, who joined to lead the new direction, summarized the shift directly: "We are now Pasarnow, a supply chain company for products" — grocery supply chain, not social commerce.

The specific problem Pasarnow chose to attack was structural rather than merely logistical: Indonesia's fresh-produce supply chain is long, layered, and poorly suited to maintaining product freshness in transit, which drives both spoilage and high logistics costs. Rijanto framed the operational core of the business plainly: "Ensuring product freshness when it reaches customers is supremely challenging." Pasarnow's answer was a multi-channel platform connecting farmers and suppliers directly to households and food businesses, backed by physical infrastructure — "Frontline Mini Hubs," small warehouses placed in densely populated areas to shorten the last mile for perishables.

The pivot attracted real capital. In September 2021, Pasarnow closed a $3.3 million (roughly Rp 47 billion) seed round led by East Ventures, with SMDV, Skystar Capital, Amand Ventures, Prasetia Dwidharma and angel investors participating — Skystar's managing partner Abraham Hidayat noted the firm had been supporting the team "since their beginning," i.e., since before the pivot. By the time of the round, the company was operating across Greater Jakarta and Bandung with over 100 full-time employees, 200-plus daily workers and driver partners, and more than 1,000 farmers and suppliers in its network. The company has continued to raise since, with cumulative funding reported at $9.5 million as of mid-2025.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Reading a demand spike during a lockdown as a structural signal rather than a temporary anomaly.** Many founders would wait out an external shock before committing to a pivot; Rijanto and Wono acted on roughly two months of observed demand, converting a crisis-driven data point into a permanent strategic decision.
- **Abandoning a working, reasonably-sized market (social commerce for stay-at-home income) for a harder, more capital-intensive one (cold-chain-adjacent grocery logistics).** The pivot traded a lighter-asset business model for one requiring physical infrastructure (Frontline Mini Hubs), warehousing, and a driver network — a much higher operational bar to clear.
- **Building owned last-mile infrastructure (Frontline Mini Hubs) rather than relying purely on third-party logistics.** Given that freshness-in-transit was named as the core operational problem, building proprietary micro-warehousing in dense areas was the direct structural answer to that specific bottleneck.
- **Retaining investor continuity through the pivot.** Skystar Capital's own account that it had backed the team "since their beginning" suggests early investors moved with the founders from Jamanow's thesis to Pasarnow's rather than requiring the company to prove the new model from a cold start with new capital.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Pasarnow is a clear example of a pivot triggered by founders correctly distinguishing a structural shift in demand from a temporary one — and doing so quickly, inside a live crisis, rather than waiting for the dust to settle. The riskier and more interesting decision was not spotting the opportunity (plenty of founders noticed grocery demand rising in 2020) but committing the company's full resources to a category with fundamentally different economics — physical infrastructure, perishable-goods logistics, farmer relationships — than the social-commerce model the team had originally built toward.

The second lesson concerns investor continuity as a signal. When early backers stay through a pivot rather than requiring a fresh raise to validate the new direction, it suggests the investors were betting on the founding team's judgment as much as on the original business plan — which is itself a useful thing for founders to cultivate deliberately: investors who understand they are backing people, not only a static thesis, are the ones who can follow a well-reasoned pivot without forcing the company to restart its fundraising clock from zero.
