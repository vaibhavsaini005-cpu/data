## One-liner

TenX raised $80M in under seven minutes during the 2017 ICO mania on a promise of making crypto spendable at any merchant, then spent two years failing to deliver a working card product before losing its co-founder and dissolving into inactivity.

## What happened

TenX was founded in Singapore in 2016 by Julian Hosp — an Austrian physician turned crypto evangelist — and Toby Hoenisch. The pitch was clean and consumer-friendly: cryptocurrency holders couldn't spend their assets at regular merchants because no one accepted crypto, so TenX would bridge the gap with a physical Visa or Mastercard debit card that drew from a crypto wallet and converted to fiat at point of sale, invisibly.

In June 2017, at the absolute peak of the ICO boom, TenX launched a public token sale for PAY tokens. It raised approximately $80M worth of Ethereum in under seven minutes — a world record at the time and one of the ten largest ICOs in history. The scale of the raise generated enormous global press coverage and positioned TenX as one of the most promising crypto infrastructure plays of the era.

The product reality was far messier. Crypto-funded debit cards required banking intermediaries — Visa and Mastercard issuers who were deeply uncomfortable with regulatory exposure from crypto-sourced funds. Card issuance was possible only through niche issuers (primarily WaveCrest Holdings), and in December 2017 Visa terminated WaveCrest's issuing licence, instantly killing TenX cards along with those of every other crypto card company relying on the same intermediary. TenX scrambled to find replacement issuers but progress was slow.

The PAY token, which had peaked during the ICO frenzy, fell by more than 95% in the 2018 crypto bear market. In January 2019 Julian Hosp resigned from TenX, citing "irreconcilable differences" with the board, following months of public controversy. With the founder gone, the token worthless, and the product undelivered, the company effectively ceased meaningful operations. By 2020-2021, the website remained live but the team had dispersed and no active product existed.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **ICO-first, product second:** TenX raised before having confirmed banking partnerships or a working product. The capital gave the appearance of credibility but masked the absence of actual infrastructure.
- **WaveCrest dependency:** The entire card product was built on a single offshore issuer, and when Visa pulled WaveCrest's licence in December 2017, TenX had no backup.
- **Regulatory blind spot:** The crypto-to-fiat card model required co-operation from regulated card networks and issuing banks — entities with strong incentives to refuse. TenX treated this as a technical problem when it was primarily a regulatory and commercial one.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

TenX is a canonical ICO-era failure: a compelling consumer use case, extraordinary fundraising velocity, and a fatally underestimated dependency on regulated intermediaries who had no incentive to cooperate. It also illustrates the governance vacuum created by ICO-funded companies — with no conventional VC board oversight and no clear shareholder accountability, companies that raised hundreds of millions could drift for years with no corrective mechanism. The pattern recurred across dozens of 2017-era ICO projects: the absence of institutional discipline let bad assumptions survive long past the point where a conventional investor would have forced a pivot or shutdown.
