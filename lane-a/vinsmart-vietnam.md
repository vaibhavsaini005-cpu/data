## One-liner

Vingroup, Vietnam's largest private conglomerate, built a national smartphone champion from nothing in 2018 by buying a Spanish phone maker's IP, erecting a 125-million-unit factory and pricing Vsmart handsets below Chinese rivals; it reached number three in Vietnam with 16.7% share and a two-million-unit white-label order from AT&T, then on 9 May 2021 founder Pham Nhat Vuong's group shut the phone and TV business outright to pour every resource into its electric-car maker VinFast, re-tasking VinSmart's engineers to build in-car infotainment.

## What happened

**The setup (2018–2019).** In mid-2018 Vingroup, the property-to-hospitals conglomerate controlled by Vietnam's richest man, Pham Nhat Vuong, decided that "Made in Vietnam" technology was its next act. Having already committed to build cars under the VinFast brand, it founded VinSmart Research and Manufacture JSC in June 2018 and, in July, bought 51% of BQ, a struggling Spanish smartphone maker, to acquire designs and patents rather than start from a blank sheet. Six months later, on 14 December 2018 at Landmark 81 in Ho Chi Minh City, it launched four handsets, the Vsmart Joy 1, Joy 1+, Active 1 and Active 1+, assembled at the VinFast complex in Hai Phong with a first-stage capacity of five million units a year. Nguyen Viet Quang, Vingroup's vice chairman and CEO, framed the mission at launch: "together with VinFast automobile, Vsmart will promote the development of Viet Nam's industry and bring the Vietnamese brand to the world." Within months the ambition escalated. On 10 June 2019 Vingroup broke ground on a second, 15.2-hectare plant at Hoa Lac Hi-Tech Park outside Hanoi, planned in phases to 23 million, then 34 million, then 125 million devices a year, with Quang citing "massive orders from large partners from Europe and the US" as the reason for building a plant "25-times" the size of the first. Phase one opened on 23 November 2019, run by Le Thi Thu Thuy, Vingroup vice president and VinSmart CEO, with production lines from the US, Germany, Japan and Korea and partnerships with Qualcomm and Google.

**The rise (2019–2020).** VinSmart attacked the bottom of the market. Its best-sellers, the Joy and Star lines, sat in the VND 1–3 million (US$42–127) band where Samsung's Galaxy A series and Oppo, Xiaomi and Realme fought for first-time and replacement buyers. The Vsmart Joy 3 sold 12,000 units in its first 14 hours in early 2020. By April 2020, fifteen months after launch, GfK data put Vsmart at 16.7% of the Vietnamese smartphone market, third behind Samsung and Oppo and one of only three brands above 15%; the company said it had sold more than 1.2 million phones in 17 months, about 200,000 a month, across 12 models, with exports to Russia, Spain and Myanmar. In July 2020 it launched the Vsmart Aris 5G on Qualcomm's Snapdragon 765G, in September the Aris Pro with an under-display camera, and in December a 5G base station, all built in Vietnam. Vingroup's 2020 results showed manufacturing revenue (cars plus phones) more than doubling from VND 9.35 trillion to VND 19.56 trillion, even as the group's net profit fell 43% to VND 4.38 trillion and the manufacturing segment "had yet to recoup its investment." The biggest prize came in late 2020: a contract order of roughly two million smartphones from a US carrier, which Bloomberg identified as AT&T. Ten VinSmart models cleared FCC certification, and from February 2021 three of them were on sale at AT&T, Cricket and Walmart as the Fusion Z, Motivate and Maestro Plus, white-labelled with the carrier's brand at US$39–89 on promotion. VinSmart's general director Nguyen Thi Hong said 5G models would follow "through distribution channels." To fund all of this, in January 2021 Vingroup announced a VND 6.98 trillion (US$302 million) three-year bond programme, VND 5.1 trillion for VinFast and VND 1.86 trillion for VinSmart.

**The pressure (early 2021).** The domestic position that looked like a moat was already slipping. Vsmart's share fell from over 15% in 2020 to about 9–10% in the first quarter of 2021, dropping it to fourth. The reasons were structural rather than tactical. The under-VND 4 million segment where Vsmart lived was, in the words of BKAV's CEO Tran Viet Hai, a "cash burning game" against Chinese brands with global scale; retailer Mai Trieu Nguyen noted that Vietnamese brands built on ODM designs and imported components could not survive "when a company has to rely entirely on another manufacturer." The 2021 global chip shortage hit smaller buyers first: large brands locked up components while VinSmart faced multi-month delays. And VinSmart's revenue, though rising fast, came at thin margins that had to carry a 125-million-unit plant running at a fraction of capacity plus heavy R&D. Meanwhile VinFast, which sold about 30,000 petrol cars in 2020 and 6,849 in the first quarter of 2021, was preparing to launch electric vehicles in Vietnam in late 2021 and abroad in 2022, and Vingroup was weighing a US$2 billion US IPO or SPAC to pay for it. Capital, engineers and management attention could go to one bet or the other.

**The decision and the outcome (9 May 2021 onward).** On Sunday 9 May 2021 Vingroup announced that VinSmart would stop researching and manufacturing smartphones and televisions. Quang's statement was blunt: "The production of smartphones or smart TVs no longer brings breakthroughs and creates unique values for users," whereas "development of smart cars, smart homes and smart cities will bring more benefits." The group said the move was to "mobilise all resources" for VinFast and called it "a strategic step towards making VinFast one of the world's leading firms in electric and smart vehicles." VinSmart, which had shipped 19 phone models and five TV models in under three years, would finish existing product lifecycles, keep honouring warranties and software updates, hand part of the Hoa Lac factory to contract manufacturing for partners, and redirect its engineers to roughly 150 infotainment features for VinFast cars plus electric motors, electronic components and batteries. Just months after Vietnam's first phones reached US carrier shelves, the brand was gone; Counterpoint's Q2 2021 data showed Chinese brands taking half the Vietnamese market, with Samsung at 37% and Xiaomi at 17% absorbing the space Vsmart vacated. VinFast, the beneficiary of the pivot, went on to list on Nasdaq via a SPAC in August 2023, a story with its own well-documented troubles that sit outside this entry.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Buying BQ's IP and launching in six months (Pham Nhat Vuong / Nguyen Viet Quang, July–December 2018).** The acquisition-not-invention route got products to market at conglomerate speed and made "Vietnam's first real phone brand" credible. It also meant the core designs were Spanish and the components Chinese, the dependency VnExpress's analysts later identified as fatal for every Vietnamese brand.

- **Building for 125 million units a year on the strength of expected export orders (June 2019).** Quang justified a plant 25 times the size of the first with anticipated orders from Europe and the US. The orders, when they came, were a couple of million white-label units at sub-$100 prices; the plant was a fixed cost sized for a global ODM business VinSmart never became.

- **Competing on price at the bottom of the market (2019–2020).** Choosing the VND 1–3 million segment bought share fast and made the 16.7% headline possible, but it put VinSmart in direct competition with Xiaomi, Realme and Oppo on the one dimension where a sub-scale newcomer cannot win, and it left almost no margin to fund differentiation.

- **Taking the AT&T white-label order (late 2020).** The deal was Vingroup's proof that a Vietnamese maker could sell in the US, and it was celebrated as such. Strategically it confirmed the opposite of the brand thesis: the phones sold as "AT&T Fusion Z," not Vsmart, at ODM economics.

- **Shutting the business rather than selling or scaling it back (Vingroup board, 9 May 2021).** The group did not look for a buyer, spin the unit out or keep a smaller line alive. It killed the consumer brand at its moment of maximum visibility to remove any competition for capital and engineering talent with VinFast, and it rebranded the loss of a business as a reallocation of "resources." This is the decision that makes the case a pivot rather than a failure: the assets (engineers, factory, supplier relationships) were redeployed, not written off.

- **Keeping the software team and warranties running (May 2021).** A small but telling choice: Vingroup committed to OS updates and repairs for existing Vsmart owners indefinitely, preserving the group's consumer reputation in Vietnam, where it sells homes, schools, hospitals and now cars to the same households.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

VinSmart is the reference case for a conglomerate-funded "national champion" in a scale industry: capital, factories and share can be bought quickly, but the economics of low-end smartphones belong to whoever has global volume and captive supply chains, and no amount of domestic goodwill changes that. Every Vietnamese phone brand before it (Q-mobile, Mobiistar) and alongside it (Bphone) hit the same wall; VinSmart simply hit it faster and with more money. Founders building hardware brands in mid-sized markets should read the 16.7% share and the two-million-unit US order as what they were, a demonstration that a local brand can exist, not evidence that it can make money.

The second lesson is about how a diversified group behaves when two moonshots compete for the same balance sheet. Vingroup was simultaneously closing its Adayroi e-commerce site, selling its VinMart supermarkets and shutting VinSmart, each time stating the money was going to VinFast. That is a coherent capital-allocation logic, and it explains why the exit was a clean shutdown rather than a slow fade. The cost is that the "Made in Vietnam" narrative that justified VinSmart's existence was abandoned the moment it stopped serving the group's priority, and partners, employees and early customers who had bought into that narrative had no say in the decision.
