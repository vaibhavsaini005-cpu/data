## One-liner

Co-founders Varun Saraf and Rishabh Singhvi abandoned WhyQ's nine-year run of consumer hawker-delivery models and pushed the company into B2B corporate dining, a pivot that took it to baseline profitability in Singapore in Q2 2025 and kept it there through Q1 2026.

## What happened

WhyQ launched in 2016 on a simple consumer pitch: pay a little more and skip Singapore's notoriously long hawker-stall queues by having the food delivered to CBD office workers instead. That model went through repeated resets — a COVID-era shift into residential delivery that blew up unit economics as rider costs outran order volumes, a scrapped no-minimum-order pricing policy, a supplier-aggregation app, and a Malaysia expansion (WhyQ EBiz, Kira Kira) that left no visible trace of success. A May 2025 Vulcan Post investigation captured the company mid-churn: unreliable third-party riders, a 2021 wave of CASE complaints over unrefunded orders, Glassdoor claims of delayed pay, and a co-founder's account of the residential-delivery timeline that didn't match the company's own records. By mid-2026, though, a follow-up Vulcan Post piece found the company had settled on a different model entirely — consolidated B2B corporate meal programs for 50 to 1,000 employees per office, capitalizing on companies using meal perks to pull staff back after pandemic-era remote work. Singhvi credited the shift directly: "The pivot to B2B... solved our entire unit economics puzzle." WhyQ hit baseline profitability in Singapore in Q2 2025 and had sustained it through Q1 2026, reporting an annualized revenue run rate of roughly S$5 million, 2,500+ corporate meals delivered daily, and 100% retention among its corporate accounts — a much smaller partner base (500 merchants, down from 2,200 hawker stalls) built for a fundamentally different customer.

## Key decisions / turning points

- 2016: Saraf and Singhvi launch WhyQ around consumer hawker-food delivery for CBD workers.
- COVID-19 period: pivot to residential delivery under lockdown; order volumes outstrip the delivery-rider economics that could support them.
- 2024 (per the May 2025 reporting, "over the past year"): the founders make a hard pivot away from consumer delivery entirely and rebuild the business around B2B corporate dining programs.
- Q2 2025: WhyQ reaches baseline profitability in Singapore for the first time — sustained for at least three subsequent quarters through Q1 2026, per the founders' own account.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

WhyQ is a rare case of a founder team getting credit for a pivot that worked only after two independent journalists had already documented years of pivots that didn't — unreliable delivery ops, an abandoned pricing model, a failed regional expansion, and labor complaints. The lesson isn't that the founders' telling of their own turnaround should be taken uncritically (the same outlet's earlier reporting caught inconsistencies in how they described their own history), but that a consumer-facing model with structurally bad unit economics (subsidized delivery to individual diners) and a B2B version of the same core asset (hawker/vendor relationships, now sold as bulk meal logistics to employers) can be genuinely different businesses — one that loses money by design and one that doesn't have to.
