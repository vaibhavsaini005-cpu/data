## One-liner

CEO Lee Seung-min grew K-beauty brand Amuse from 200 million won in revenue to over 52 billion won, leading to Shinsegae International's 100%, 71.3 billion won ($51.6-52.6M) acquisition of the company from Naver's Snow Corp. in August 2024.

## What happened

Lee Seung-min joined Amuse in 2019 as marketing director, after building branding expertise at Chanel and L'Oréal, when the brand's annual revenue was around 200 million won. Under his leadership as CEO, Amuse — known for viral, Gen Z-oriented products like its "Jang Won-young tint" — grew revenue more than 250x to over 52 billion won ($38-40M) by 2024, with particular strength in North America, Japan, and Southeast Asia. On August 5, 2024, Shinsegae International announced it was acquiring 100% of Amuse's shares for 71.3 billion won (reported as US$51.6-52.6M) from majority shareholder Snow Corp., a Naver subsidiary that had held a 77.6% stake. Shinsegae International CEO William Kim said the deal was "expected to provide significant momentum to expand our global business." Lee Seung-min joined Shinsegae Group as part of the deal and now also heads the company's Cosmetics Division 2, managing Amuse alongside other Shinsegae beauty brands.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Lee Seung-min's decision to leave global incumbents (Chanel, L'Oréal) for an early-stage, 200-million-won-revenue Korean brand** in 2019, betting on Gen Z-driven viral growth over an established corporate beauty career.
- **Shinsegae International's decision (under CEO William Kim) to acquire 100% of Amuse from Snow/Naver** rather than build a competing Gen Z beauty brand internally, paying a premium for an already-scaled, internationally distributed label.
- **Retaining Lee Seung-min post-acquisition** rather than replacing him, expanding his mandate to multiple Shinsegae beauty brands instead of treating the deal as a simple asset purchase.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Amuse shows a large Korean conglomerate (Shinsegae) using an outright acquisition, rather than in-house incubation, to buy its way into Gen Z-driven K-beauty distribution and export momentum — and explicitly retaining and promoting the operating executive who built that growth rather than absorbing the brand into existing management. For founders/operators, it's also a case where joining a small brand as an employee (not a founder) at an early stage, and driving the growth that leads to acquisition, converted into a bigger role inside the acquirer rather than an exit from operating entirely.
