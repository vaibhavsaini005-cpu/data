## One-liner

Thai Airways, the loss-making national carrier carrying about 240 billion baht of debt and a politically appointed board, was pushed by Prime Minister Prayut Chan-o-cha's cabinet in May 2020 to refuse a 54 billion baht state loan guarantee, sell down the Finance Ministry's stake to 47.9% so that it stopped being a state enterprise, and enter Thailand's largest ever court-supervised rehabilitation; five years later it had cut headcount from nearly 30,000 to about 14,000, fleet types from 12 to 4, debt from roughly 400 billion to 80 billion baht, converted about 55 billion baht of debt into equity, turned shareholders' equity from minus 127.2 billion baht to plus 55.2 billion, exited rehabilitation on 16 June 2025 and relisted on 4 August 2025 at 10.50 baht, 134% above its 4.48 baht capital-raise price.

## What happened

**The setup (2017–early 2020).** Thai Airways International (THAI) was founded in 1960 and majority-owned by the Ministry of Finance, which held 51.03%. As a state enterprise it carried the obligations that come with that status: unionised staff, procurement rules, a board that in 2020 included three Air Chief Marshals among its members, and routes and aircraft chosen for reasons that were not always commercial. Two Airbus A340-500s bought in the early 2000s for a Bangkok–New York non-stop that never paid were parked and could not be sold; a 2005 Rolls-Royce bribery case over engine sales to THAI, exposed by UK investigators in 2017, had produced no Thai prosecutions three years later. The airline operated 12 aircraft types across a fleet of about 100, an expensive mix that mirrored the politics of each purchase. Results deteriorated before the pandemic: a net loss of 2.07 billion baht in 2017, 11.57 billion in 2018 and 12.02 billion in 2019, with a 6.8 billion baht first-half 2019 loss triggering a state audit. By early 2020 total liabilities were about 240–245 billion baht (roughly US$7.7 billion) and the airline employed more than 22,000 people directly, close to 30,000 including subsidiaries.

**The decision point (May 2020).** COVID-19 grounded the international network, and THAI projected an 18 billion baht loss for the first half of 2020 alone. The Finance Ministry proposed a 54 billion baht loan guarantee to keep the airline afloat. On 19 May 2020 the cabinet under Prime Minister Prayut Chan-o-cha rejected that route and approved a filing for court-supervised business rehabilitation instead, reasoning that fresh public money without structural change would be lost. Three days later, on 22 May 2020, the Finance Ministry sold 3.17% of THAI to the state-backed Vayupak Fund 1, taking its holding from 51.03% to 47.86%. With the state below 50%, THAI ceased to be a state enterprise: its unions lost the protections of state-enterprise labour law, its procurement and pay rules changed, and the board could be reconstituted on commercial grounds. On 26 May 2020 THAI filed its petition with the Central Bankruptcy Court, and on 14 September 2020 the court accepted it and appointed plan preparers, EY Corporate Advisory Services with six THAI directors. Tilleke & Gibbins described it as the largest business restructuring in Thai history. The airline reported a 2020 net loss of 141.18 billion baht on revenue of 48.64 billion, and ended the year with shareholders' equity of minus 127.2 billion baht.

**The plan (2021–2022).** THAI submitted its rehabilitation plan on 2 March 2021, covering about 410 billion baht of claims from some 13,000 creditors. The Central Bankruptcy Court approved the plan and its amendments on 15 June 2021 and named five plan administrators: Piyasvasti Amranand, a former THAI president brought back to run the turnaround, Pornchai Thiravet, Siri Jirapongphan, Kraisorn Barameeauychai and Chansin Treenuchagron, who also served as acting CEO through the early restructuring. The operating cuts were immediate and deep. The fleet was shrunk from 103 aircraft in 2020 to about 70 by 2023, and the number of aircraft types was cut from 12 toward 4, which the airline said saved roughly 8.5 billion baht a year in operating cost. Staff were offered early retirement and mutual separation; the workforce fell from nearly 30,000 to around 14,000. Loss-making routes, catering and ground-service arrangements were renegotiated. In 2021 THAI booked a net profit of 56.5 billion baht, almost entirely from debt restructuring gains and asset sales rather than flying, then a 5.09 billion baht loss in 2022 as operations restarted. Two plan administrators, Kraisorn and Siri, resigned on 21 October 2022, and in 2023 Chai Eamsiri, a THAI finance veteran, became CEO.

**The recovery (2023–2024).** Reopened borders and a tourism rebound arrived with a much lower cost base. In 2023 THAI posted revenue of 165.49 billion baht and net profit of 28.12 billion. In 2024 revenue excluding one-offs rose 16.7% to 187.99 billion baht and operating profit before finance costs rose 3.2% to 41.52 billion, with EBITDA after aircraft leases of 41.84 billion. Chai told reporters THAI had recorded the highest EBIT margin growth of any airline in the world in 2024. The balance sheet was fixed in the fourth quarter. On 18 November 2024 the airline opened its debt-to-equity conversion: a mandatory tranche of up to 14.86 billion new shares (37.83 billion baht), a voluntary tranche of up to 4.91 billion shares (12.5 billion baht) and a deferred-interest tranche of up to 1.90 billion shares (4.85 billion baht), all at about 2.55 baht a share, roughly 55.18 billion baht in total, open to bondholders, financial institutions, the Finance Ministry and state-enterprise creditors. That was followed by a private placement and rights-style capital increase at 4.48 baht per share, registered on 20 December 2024. Because existing creditors converted at 2.5452 baht while new money came in at 4.48 baht, accounting rules required THAI to book a debt-restructuring loss of about 45.27 billion baht, and with impairments and foreign-exchange items the reported 2024 net loss was 26.90 billion baht, a paper loss on a year of record operating profit.

**The exit (2025).** With equity positive, THAI petitioned the court in the second quarter of 2025 to end rehabilitation, showing it had met the plan's four conditions: a new board formed, twelve-month EBITDA after aircraft leases of 40.3 billion baht against a 20 billion baht target, debt repaid on schedule without default, and the registered capital increase with debt-to-equity conversion completed. Shareholders' equity stood at 55.2 billion baht at 31 March 2025, against minus 127.2 billion at the end of 2020, and debt had fallen from about 400 billion baht to about 80 billion, scheduled for repayment through 2036 with 10 billion baht due in 2025. On 16 June 2025 the Central Bankruptcy Court ordered the rehabilitation terminated. The Stock Exchange of Thailand cleared the shares, suspended since 2021, to resume trading on 4 August 2025. They opened at 10.50 baht, 134.4% above the 4.48 baht capital-increase price, valuing the airline at close to 300 billion baht. THAI's forward plan is growth from a rebuilt base: 79 aircraft in mid-2025 rising to 103 in 2026 and 116 in 2027, with 45 Boeing 787-9s on firm order for delivery from mid-2027 and options for 35 more, and a long-term target of about 150 aircraft by 2033.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Refusing the 54 billion baht guarantee and choosing the bankruptcy court (Prayut cabinet, 19 May 2020).** The single most important choice. A guarantee would have kept every structural problem in place: the board, the unions' state-enterprise protections, the 12-type fleet and the political procurement. Court supervision gave management legal cover to do what no CEO of a state carrier had been able to do voluntarily.

- **Selling 3.17% to Vayupak to drop below 50% (Finance Ministry, 22 May 2020).** A tiny transaction with enormous effect. Taking the state's stake from 51.03% to 47.86% removed state-enterprise status and with it the labour and procurement regime that had made the airline unmanageable. It is a textbook example of changing a company's legal category as the lever for changing its behaviour.

- **Bringing back Piyasvasti Amranand and reconstituting the board as plan administrators (court order, 15 June 2021).** Putting a former president with a reform record at the head of a small administrator group, answerable to creditors and the court rather than to ministers, replaced political oversight with creditor oversight for the duration of the plan.

- **Cutting fleet types from 12 to 4 and headcount by more than half (plan administrators, 2021–2022).** The fleet simplification alone saved about 8.5 billion baht a year in maintenance, training and spares, and the separation programmes removed some 15,000 positions. These were the cuts the airline's political owners had never been able to make, and they set the cost base that turned the 2023 tourism rebound into a 28 billion baht profit.

- **Converting about 55 billion baht of debt to equity at 2.55 baht and raising new money at 4.48 baht (November–December 2024).** This is what turned equity positive and satisfied the exit conditions. The price gap forced a 45 billion baht accounting loss in 2024, which management chose to accept and explain rather than avoid, because the alternative was staying under court supervision.

- **Committing to 45 Boeing 787-9s before leaving rehabilitation (2024–2025).** THAI chose to lock in growth capacity while still under the plan, signalling to the market that the turnaround was about scale on a lower cost base rather than permanent shrinkage. Whether the order proves right will only be known after deliveries begin in 2027.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Thai Airways is the clearest recent case in the region of a state-owned incumbent being saved by being deliberately made less state-owned. The operators' lesson is that in a company whose problems are structural, the constraint is rarely cash; it is the set of rules that stop management from touching the cost base. Prayut's government understood that a bailout would fund the problem and a court process would dissolve it, and used a 3.17% share sale to change which laws applied to the airline. The second pattern is the value of a supervised, time-boxed restructuring with hard exit conditions. Because the plan defined success as measurable outcomes, positive equity, an EBITDA floor, repayments without default, the administrators had a mandate that survived leadership changes and creditor disputes. Third, the case shows that headline financial results can invert during a turnaround: THAI reported a 56 billion baht profit in its worst operating year and a 27 billion baht loss in its best, and only readers who separated restructuring accounting from operations understood which year was the good one. Founders and operators in any sector will recognise the shape: the recovery came from cutting the variety that politics had added, not from new revenue, and the growth bet was made only after the cost base had been proven at a smaller scale.
