## One-liner

Wellington-founded Xero turned desktop accounting software into a cloud-native product, growing from a NZ$55 million NZX debut into an ASX 100 company serving nearly 5 million customers across 180 countries.

## What happened

Rod Drury and Hamish Edwards founded Xero on July 6, 2006 in Wellington, New Zealand, betting that small-business accounting would move entirely off desktop software (dominated at the time by MYOB and Intuit's QuickBooks) and onto the cloud, with live bank feeds and real-time books instead of periodic reconciliation. Xero listed on the New Zealand Stock Exchange in June 2007 at a NZ$55 million market capitalisation, raising NZ$15 million in its IPO — an unusually early public listing for a startup still building out its core product.

Growth outside New Zealand initially lagged, so Xero pursued a dual listing on the Australian Securities Exchange in November 2012 to access deeper capital markets and accelerate expansion into Australia, the UK, and the US; by the time of that listing the company was valued at almost half a billion Australian dollars. In 2018, Xero consolidated its listing solely on the ASX and delisted from the NZX, a move that coincided with its inclusion in the S&P/ASX 100 index.

By the mid-2020s Xero had built an ecosystem of over 1,000 third-party app integrations and was used by more than 4.9 million subscribers globally, making it one of the largest technology companies to originate from New Zealand.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Betting on cloud-native from day one**: Building Xero as a pure cloud SaaS product rather than a hosted version of desktop software gave it a structural advantage over incumbents as bank-feed integrations and real-time bookkeeping became the market standard.
- **Going public early (2007) to fund a capital-intensive land grab**: Listing on the NZX just a year after founding gave Xero capital to out-invest competitors in customer acquisition before profitability, a path most SaaS startups reach via late-stage private funding instead.
- **Consolidating onto the ASX (2018)**: Moving fully to the larger, more liquid Australian market to chase index inclusion and institutional investor access, rather than staying dual-listed indefinitely.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Xero is the clearest counter-example to the idea that a New Zealand-based company must either relocate to the US or stay small: it scaled a horizontal SaaS category (accounting) globally while keeping its listing and much of its operations anchored in Australasia, using two sequential public listings as growth-capital vehicles rather than a single late exit event. It's also a useful data point for the "reverse timing" pattern — going public unusually early, before the product or market was proven at scale, and using the public markets' capital access as the engine for that proof rather than the reward for having already achieved it.
