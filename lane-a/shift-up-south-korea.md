## One-liner

Shift Up founder and CEO Kim Hyung-tae postponed the studio's IPO by four months to let its console debut Stellar Blade prove the company wasn't a one-game mobile gacha business, then listed on the KOSPI on July 11, 2024 to a market cap of roughly $3 billion.

## What happened

Kim Hyung-tae, a former NCSoft art director on Blade & Soul, founded Shift Up in December 2013 and built it around mobile gacha titles, culminating in Goddess of Victory: Nikke, which by early 2024 generated about 97% of the studio's revenue. Shift Up filed to list on the Korea Exchange (KOSPI) on March 5, 2024, targeting a valuation of roughly $2.3 billion, but Korean regulators flagged the company's near-total dependence on a single title as a listing risk.

Rather than list on the original timeline, Kim pushed the IPO back to July 2024 and used the delay to launch Stellar Blade — a PS5-exclusive action game built on Unreal Engine 4 that Shift Up had spent five years developing, a deliberate departure from the Unity-based mobile work the studio was known for. Stellar Blade shipped in April 2024 and became a commercial and critical hit, giving Shift Up a second major revenue line by the time it returned to the listing process. Kim also kept Tencent on as a 35% shareholder through the IPO rather than pursuing full independence, trading some ownership control for continued distribution and development support. Shift Up's IPO priced at 60,000 won per share, raised about 435 billion won (~$315 million), and closed its first trading day up nearly 50%, valuing the company at close to 4.12 trillion won (~$3 billion); Kim retained a 39% stake.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Delaying the IPO by four months (March → July 2024)** rather than listing on the original timeline despite regulator concerns, converting a flagged weakness (single-title dependence) into a resolved one before going public.
- **Spending five years and a full platform switch on Stellar Blade** — moving from Unity-based mobile gacha to a PS5-exclusive Unreal Engine 4 console title — to diversify revenue ahead of the listing rather than optimizing the existing hit.
- **Keeping Tencent as a large minority shareholder (35%) instead of buying it out or diluting it pre-IPO**, prioritizing continued publishing and infrastructure support over maximizing founder control.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Shift Up shows a founder treating a regulator's objection as a scheduling problem, not a strategic one: instead of contesting the single-title-dependence concern or delisting the risk with disclosures, Kim used the delay itself to manufacture the missing proof point (a second hit, in a different genre and platform, aimed at a different audience). That reframes "IPO readiness" as something a founder can actively build in the gap between filing and listing, rather than a fixed condition to be judged against at a single point in time — a useful pattern for any founder whose growth story depends on convincing gatekeepers that a current strength (a hit product) isn't also a hidden concentration risk.
