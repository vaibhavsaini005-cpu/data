## One-liner

Tokyo founder Shogo Hayashi pivoted his company from a struggling consumer AI education service to nocall.ai, a generative-AI phone-operator product, after ChatGPT's emergence convinced him voice automation was the better vehicle for his original mission — and raised an ¥800 million Series A to scale it.

## What happened

Shogo Hayashi, who had previously helped found several schools in Japan including the country's first boarding school for elementary students, founded nocall in 2020 to build a consumer-facing AI education service. By his own account, the business struggled to find traction: "Initially, we started with an AI education service for consumers. However, as we worked on it, we struggled to find traction as a business and continued to search for the right path." The turning point came with the emergence of ChatGPT and generative AI more broadly, which gave Hayashi a specific new thesis: "I had an intuition that by implementing generative AI in society, we could fundamentally change the means that everyone has been using."

Hayashi made what he calls "a major pivot" to nocall.ai, a service where AI agents handle real business phone calls — listening to a caller's issue in context and, in some cases, proceeding directly to negotiations or bookings — for sectors including automotive, financial services, recruiting and IT. He was explicit that the underlying mission hadn't changed, only the mechanism: "While the original goal of 'creating a system where people can thrive' remains the same, we are now trying to realize it through the technology of generative AI." The pivoted company raised approximately ¥800 million (about $5.4 million) in a Series A round co-led by JAFCO and Archetype Ventures, announced around September 11-16, 2026, bringing cumulative funding to over ¥1 billion. The capital is earmarked to expand the automotive and financial-services verticals, invest in telephony infrastructure, grow enterprise sales in Japan, and eventually build an English-language product for Southeast Asia.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Abandoning a struggling AI-education product rather than iterating within the same category**: Hayashi treated the underlying mission ("creating a system where people can thrive") as fixed while treating the specific product and market as fully replaceable.
- **Personally taking over product and development work after the pivot**: "I began to get deeply involved in development and the product myself" — Hayashi shifted from a more removed founder role to hands-on building once he committed to the new direction.
- **Choosing voice/phone automation specifically, rather than another generative-AI text or chat product**: a deliberate bet that phone calls remain a more intuitive interface than text-based UIs for the business use cases nocall targets (appointment confirmations, payment collection, follow-ups).

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Hayashi's account is a clean, self-narrated example of a founder separating mission from mechanism during a pivot: rather than treating the failure of the AI-education product as a failure of the underlying goal, he explicitly reframed it as a wrong choice of technology and channel, then rebuilt around a new one (generative AI voice agents) that a specific external event (ChatGPT's emergence) made newly viable. For founders stuck in a low-traction product, this is a concrete template — keep the mission statement, discard the product thesis, and look for a new technological unlock that serves the same underlying goal through a different mechanism.
