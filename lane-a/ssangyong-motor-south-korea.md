## One-liner

SsangYong Motor, the Mahindra-controlled Korean SUV maker, filed for bankruptcy in December 2020 after missing $135 million in creditor repayments, then entered court receivership in April 2021 when Mahindra refused to inject further capital and a rescue buyer couldn't be lined up in time.

## What happened

SsangYong Motor, a South Korean SUV and pickup manufacturer that Indian conglomerate Mahindra & Mahindra (M&M) acquired a 75% stake in back in 2011, filed for bankruptcy on December 21, 2020 after failing to meet 150 billion won (about $135 million) in debt repayments owed to creditors led by state-run Korea Development Bank (KDB). Creditors refused to reschedule the deadline, forcing the filing. Under Korea's Autonomous Restructuring Support (ARS) scheme, SsangYong was initially given a grace period — with Mahindra continuing to manage it outside full court intervention — to find a solution before a hard deadline.

Mahindra, which had already told the market more than a year earlier that it would not invest further funds into the loss-making subsidiary, spent the grace period trying to broker a sale, including talks with US vehicle importer HAAH Automotive Holdings. When a binding letter of intent from HAAH failed to materialize before the court-imposed March 31, 2021 deadline, the Seoul Bankruptcy Court placed SsangYong into formal court receivership on April 15, 2021 — the second time in a decade the company had gone through receivership (the first was in 2009). Vehicle sales fell 22% year-on-year in 2021 to 84,106 units amid the receivership uncertainty, pandemic disruption, and a global automotive chip shortage. The company eventually emerged from receivership via a 2022 sale to Korean conglomerate KG Group's consortium (later rebranded KG Mobility).

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Mahindra's 2011 majority acquisition without a durable turnaround plan**: Buying 75% of a chronically loss-making Korean automaker gave Mahindra global manufacturing reach, but it never solved SsangYong's underlying cost structure or product-cycle problems, and a decade later Mahindra walked away rather than keep funding losses.
- **Public pre-announcement of "no further funding"**: By signaling over a year in advance that it wouldn't recapitalize SsangYong, Mahindra effectively started the countdown to insolvency and forced creditors and the Korean court system to take over the restructuring process on their own timeline.
- **A failed rescue deal at the deadline**: Pinning survival on a single external buyer (HAAH Automotive) meeting a hard court deadline, rather than lining up a backup, meant the entire company tipped into receivership the moment one deal slipped past March 31, 2021.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

SsangYong is a case study in the risk of cross-border manufacturing rescues without a real path to profitability: a foreign parent (Mahindra) can prop up a national industrial asset for years, but once it withdraws support, local courts and creditors — not the parent — end up controlling the company's fate. It's also a reminder that automotive manufacturing failures unfold on a much longer, more legally structured timeline (repeated receivership, multi-year sale processes) than typical startup shutdowns, but the underlying trigger — a controlling shareholder no longer willing to fund losses — is the same pattern seen across sectors.
