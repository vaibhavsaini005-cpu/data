## One-liner

Zentoshin, an Osaka credit-card payment processor that advanced sales proceeds to roughly 200,000 small merchants before the card companies paid out, became Japan's largest corporate bankruptcy of 2026 with about ¥125.9 billion in liabilities — killed not by its product but by a 2024 fraud scandal that cut off the bank financing its entire cash-advance model depended on.

## What happened

Zentoshin's business was a cash-flow service dressed as payment processing: it signed up restaurants and retail shops as card merchants and then paid them their sales proceeds *early*, ahead of settlement from the credit card companies, taking a fee for bridging the gap. As of 2018 it had around 200,000 member stores, many of them thin-margin, high-volume food businesses that had come to treat Zentoshin's early payment as working capital rather than a convenience.

That model has a structural dependency most of its merchants never saw: to front money it does not yet have, the processor must itself be continuously financed. Zentoshin was funded by Japanese regional banks, which made its access to credit — not its merchant demand — the real load-bearing element of the business.

Two shocks hit that dependency in sequence. First, the pandemic damaged the restaurant sector that made up much of its merchant base. Then in 2024, former employees were arrested on suspicion of signing up merchants who had failed credit screening by using other people's names — reportedly including dining businesses tied to rip-off practices. Food journalist Rikiya Yamaji summarised the consequence bluntly: "That caused the company itself to lose credibility."

Credibility, in a financing-dependent business, converts directly into cash. With lenders pulling back, Zentoshin could no longer fund the advances that were its reason for existing. The Osaka District Court approved the start of bankruptcy proceedings around July 6–8, 2026. Reported liabilities differ by source and filing stage — ¥115.1 billion in initial reports, about ¥125.9 billion in later court-stage figures (roughly $710–776 million) — but either number made it Japan's biggest corporate failure of the year.

The damage propagated in both directions at once. Merchants lost proceeds already earned: multiple restaurant operators reported several million yen outstanding across their outlets, and a court-appointed bankruptcy lawyer's notice warned that "even if the credit terminal appears to operate, the service cannot be used under any circumstances." Upstream, at least five listed regional banks and banking groups — including Towa Bank and San ju San Financial Group — announced writedowns.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Building a financing-dependent business and treating merchant growth as the growth metric.** Scaling to ~200,000 member stores increased the volume of advances Zentoshin had to fund, which deepened rather than reduced its reliance on bank credit. Scale made the company more fragile to a financing shock, not less.
- **Letting merchants through who had failed credit screening, via third-party names.** Whatever this did for short-term volume, it attacked the one asset the business could not operate without — lender trust. The 2024 arrests, not any competitive or product failure, are the proximate cause of the collapse.
- **Allowing merchants to become dependent on early payment without disclosing the counterparty risk.** Restaurants treated Zentoshin as infrastructure. When it stopped, they discovered they had been extending unsecured credit to a leveraged intermediary.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Zentoshin is a clean case of a company whose fatal single point of failure sat in its funding structure rather than its market. Demand was never the problem — 200,000 merchants wanted the product on the day it died. But any business that pays out before it gets paid is really a lending business, and a lending business runs on its lenders' confidence. That makes a compliance or integrity failure existential in a way it simply is not for a company funded by customer revenue: a fraud scandal at a normal SaaS vendor is a bad quarter, while at Zentoshin it severed the input the entire model metabolised.

The second, broader lesson is about concentration risk flowing through an intermediary. Zentoshin's failure simultaneously hit thin-margin merchants who had treated it as a utility and the regional banks that had treated it as a borrower — the same collapse landed on both the customers and the creditors. Founders building any kind of float, factoring, or "get paid early" product should assume their real risk register is a financing register, and that their customers are silently taking on credit exposure they have not priced.
