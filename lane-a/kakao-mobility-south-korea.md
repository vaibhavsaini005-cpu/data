## One-liner

Facing a public and political backlash over its taxi-hailing fees, Kakao Mobility scrapped its "Smart Call" priority-dispatch surcharge and cut driver commissions, trading near-term revenue for regulatory survival.

## What happened

Kakao Mobility's Kakao T taxi app generated meaningful revenue from "Smart Call," a service launched April 10, 2018 that let riders pay roughly 1,000 won to be matched first with drivers who had the highest dispatch-acceptance rates — effectively a pay-to-jump-the-queue fee. As Kakao Mobility scaled into designated-driver services and other adjacent verticals, lawmakers, small taxi operators, and the public increasingly accused the company of using its dominant platform position to squeeze extra fees out of both riders and drivers.

The backlash intensified through 2021 as Kakao Group came under broader scrutiny over "platform monopoly" practices across its businesses. On September 14, 2021, Kakao Mobility Chairman Kim Beom-su announced the company would completely abolish Smart Call, and it also cut commission fees on its designated-driver service by 20%. The changes were explicitly framed as a "co-existing business model" pivot away from extraction-style monetization. The concessions arrived alongside an indefinite delay of Kakao Mobility's planned IPO, which spooked existing investors TPG and Carlyle given the hit to the company's earnings outlook and valuation.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Political pressure forced the hand**: The decision followed direct criticism from Kakao's own chairman amid a national conversation about platform-company monopoly power, not a bottom-up product rethink.
- **Revenue sacrificed for legitimacy**: Smart Call was a proven cash generator; killing it and cutting driver commissions was a deliberate trade of near-term monetization for regulatory and public goodwill.
- **IPO fallout**: The pivot directly delayed Kakao Mobility's public listing, showing investors that a reversed monetization strategy has real valuation consequences, not just PR ones.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Kakao Mobility illustrates the tension dominant platforms face between monetizing marketplace power and avoiding regulatory/political blowback in markets where taxi driver associations and antitrust regulators have real leverage. Unlike a product-market-fit pivot, this was a defensive pivot — walking back a working revenue feature under external pressure — and it cost the company both near-term earnings and IPO timing, a pattern later echoed by further antitrust fines against Kakao Mobility in 2023 and 2024.
