## One-liner

South Korean premium grocery startup Kurly hit a $3.3 billion unicorn valuation in December 2021, then scrapped its IPO in January 2023 as its valuation collapsed 78% to ~$669 million — before quietly posting its first-ever EBITDA profit in December 2023 and signaling a possible IPO revival.

## What happened

Kurly (operating as Market Kurly) was founded in 2015 by Kim Seung-bum (Sophie Kim) in Seoul and pioneered "dawn delivery" (새벽배송, Saebalk) — an order-by-midnight, delivered-by-7am cold chain service for premium groceries. The model resonated with time-poor urban Korean households willing to pay a premium for fresh, curated produce and imported goods. The company grew rapidly through the pandemic years. In December 2021, it raised $210 million in a pre-IPO round at a $3.3 billion valuation, making it one of Korea's highest-valued consumer startups.

Kurly received preliminary KOSPI listing approval in August 2022, with plans to go public before February 2023. But the rate-driven tech selloff erased most of its listed peers' valuations. The company, still carrying cumulative operating losses and facing questions from the Korea Exchange about its ownership structure (founder Kim held a small stake relative to institutional holders), announced in January 2023 that it was withdrawing its IPO application. By then, secondary market indicators put its implied valuation at roughly $669 million — a 78% decline from the $3.3 billion peak eighteen months earlier.

Kurly then pivoted to operational tightening: reducing headcount, narrowing SKU count to focus on high-margin private-label and premium imports, and renegotiating logistics contracts. In December 2023, the company reported its first-ever positive EBITDA, a reversal that analysts cited as making a renewed IPO attempt viable in 2024–2025.

## Key decisions / turning points

- The December 2021 $3.3 billion pre-IPO raise at peak market pricing locked Kurly into investor return expectations that the subsequent bear market made impossible to meet at IPO.
- The ownership structure — founder holding a small equity stake while institutional investors held most of the float — was flagged by the Korea Exchange as a governance concern, complicating the listing process independently of market conditions.
- The January 2023 IPO withdrawal was a deliberate choice to avoid a down-round listing that would have been visible to the market and damaging to brand and employee morale.
- The shift to SKU rationalization and private-label focus in 2023 was the operational pivot that made the December 2023 EBITDA turnaround possible.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Kurly illustrates the "unicorn valuation trap": raising at a peak multiple creates a reference price that becomes an anchor both internally (employee expectations) and externally (public market optics). When markets turn, the company must either list at a visible 78% discount or withdraw — both outcomes carry reputational cost. The more interesting pattern is the recovery arc: by abandoning growth-at-all-costs and focusing on unit economics, Kurly achieved EBITDA profitability faster than most predicted. The lesson is that premature IPO timelines set by pre-IPO investor appetite, rather than operational readiness, tend to produce exactly this scenario — and that the exit from it requires a painful but achievable unit-economics reset.
