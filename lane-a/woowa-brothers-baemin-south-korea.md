## One-liner

South Korea's dominant food delivery app, Baemin, grew from a zero-revenue side project into a $4 billion acquisition by Delivery Hero — the largest tech M&A deal in Korean startup history at the time.

## What happened

Kim Bong-jin launched Baedal Minjok ("배달의민족," meaning "Nation of Delivery") in 2011 after noticing that Korea's massive telephone-based food delivery culture had no digital equivalent. The app aggregated restaurants and let users order the same way they always had — but through a smartphone.

Early growth was rapid but so was competition: rival Yogiyo (backed by Rocket Internet) fought aggressively for the same market. The two companies spent heavily on marketing, most notably Baemin's distinctive retro-typography campaigns and subway poster ads that became a cultural phenomenon in Seoul.

The turning point came in 2018 when Goldman Sachs co-led a ~$400 million funding round — a signal of Baemin's market-leading position. By late 2019 Baemin commanded roughly 55% of South Korea's food delivery GMV.

In December 2019 Delivery Hero — which already owned Yogiyo — agreed to acquire Woowa Brothers for approximately $4 billion in a cash-and-stock deal, creating an effective duopoly. The Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) opened an antitrust review. After nearly two years of scrutiny, the KFTC approved the deal in December 2021 on the condition that Delivery Hero divest one of the two platforms. Delivery Hero retained Baemin and in 2023 sold Yogiyo to GS Retail for roughly $710 million — a fraction of what it paid for Woowa.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Branding as culture:** Baemin's retro-font identity campaigns made the brand iconic in Seoul, driving word-of-mouth and app installs without heavy performance marketing.
- **Resisting the merger to grow independently first:** Rather than consolidating early, Baemin fought the competitive war and arrived at the negotiating table as the dominant player, which determined the price.
- **Goldman Sachs 2018 round:** The $400M at a multi-billion valuation validated the business and made an exit to a global player more feasible.
- **KFTC condition:** The regulator's divestiture requirement ultimately meant Delivery Hero had to sell the weaker asset (Yogiyo) rather than Baemin, confirming Baemin's superior unit economics.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Baemin illustrates that in winner-take-most delivery markets, brand equity and GMV share matter more than short-term profitability when negotiating an exit. The company also shows how a local startup can outmaneuver a well-funded incumbent (Rocket Internet's Yogiyo) through cultural relevance rather than purely financial firepower. The KFTC antitrust outcome is now a frequently cited precedent for how Korean regulators handle platform consolidation.
