## One-liner
Lozi started in 2014 as a restaurant-discovery and review app, then pivoted into building its own delivery fleet — Loship — after realizing its users wanted to order, not just browse.

## What happened
Lozi launched in Vietnam in 2014 letting users find dining spots by ratings and reviews. After raising early funding in 2015 from Vietnam Silicon Valley, Golden Gate Ventures, and DesignOne Japan, the team saw rising demand from both customers (who wanted convenient ordering, not just discovery) and merchants (who needed on-demand delivery support). In late 2017, Lozi built Loship, a proprietary one-hour delivery fleet, shifting the company's center of gravity from a C2C content/review app to a logistics and on-demand commerce operator. The pivot deepened with a 2019 investment from South Korea's Smilegate Investment and Hana Financial Group, which funded nationwide expansion. By the time of this reporting, Loship had grown into a multi-service platform spanning food (Loship), groceries (Lomart), ride-hailing (Lo-xe), medicine (Lomed), laundry (Lozat), package delivery (Lo-send), beauty (LoBeauty), flowers (Lo-hoa) and B2B supply (Lo-supply) across major Vietnamese cities; the original Lozi app was later discontinued as the Loship brand took over.

## Key decisions / turning points
- 2015: raised seed-stage funding as a review/discovery app, before any delivery infrastructure existed.
- Late 2017: built an in-house delivery fleet (Loship) instead of relying on third-party logistics, betting that owning last-mile delivery was the real value driver, not the review content.
- 2019: took investment from South Korean financial and VC backers specifically to fund nationwide scale-up of the delivery business, cementing Loship (not Lozi) as the core product.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)
A content/discovery layer is often just the cheapest way to acquire an audience before the real, harder-to-build business (in this case, logistics infrastructure) reveals itself as the actual value the users wanted — the pivot succeeded because the team was willing to let the original product (and eventually its name) be fully subsumed by what the data showed users actually needed.
