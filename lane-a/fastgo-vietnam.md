## One-liner

Vietnam's "made-in-Vietnam" answer to Grab and Go-Viet lost the ride-hailing
war, but the same founder and the same holding group rebuilt the business
inside a fintech subsidiary that ended up bigger than the app ever was.

## What happened

FastGo launched in Hanoi in June 2018, backed by NextTech Group (the
Vietnamese internet conglomerate behind e-commerce and payments ventures
going back to the mid-2000s) and funded in part by VinaCapital. It positioned
itself as a domestic, zero-commission alternative to Grab and Go-Viet, and
grew fast on paper — nearly 15,000 driver-partners across Ho Chi Minh City
and Hanoi within its first two months.

The growth ran into a wall almost immediately: in December 2018, Vietnam's
Ministry of Transport and Ministry of Industry and Trade jointly ruled that
FastGo was not qualified to operate. It had not completed the pilot-program
registration required under Decision 24 (the government's framework for
app-based ride-hailing) and hadn't finished e-commerce platform registration
either — meaning it legally couldn't recruit drivers or carry passengers
until both were resolved. FastGo kept operating through the ambiguity, but
the regulatory drag combined with Grab and Go-Viet's much deeper subsidy
war chests meant driver growth stalled: only about 20,000 new drivers added
over the following eight months of 2019. From 2020 the app faded, and by
May 2021 it had officially stopped updating.

While FastGo the consumer app was dying, its founder and CEO, Nguyễn Hữu
Tuất, was simultaneously running NextPay — NextTech's fintech arm, formed
by merging its earlier mobile-POS business (mPOS) with payments company
Vimo. Tuất redirected his attention and the group's resources there. NextPay
scaled to 34 provinces and cities with more than 80,000 payment-acceptance
points, capturing roughly 12% of Vietnam's national POS terminal market —
a substantial share for a wholly domestic player going up against banks and
foreign processors.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Launching into a subsidy war it couldn't win.** FastGo tried to compete
  on being "Vietnamese-owned" rather than on capital depth, in a category
  where Grab and Go-Viet were burning venture money on driver and rider
  incentives. That positioning didn't convert into durable share once the
  regulatory delay ate its early momentum.
- **Nguyễn Hữu Tuất's decision to run FastGo and NextPay in parallel, then
  let NextPay win the resource allocation.** Rather than shutting down and
  restarting, the group let FastGo wind down slowly (last app update May
  2021) while NextPay — a B2B, infrastructure-layer bet on small-merchant
  payments — kept scaling underneath it. The pivot wasn't announced as a
  single event; it shows up only in hindsight, as one business fading while
  the other absorbed the founder's time and the parent group's capital.
- **Choosing infrastructure (POS/payments) over consumer subsidy
  competition.** NextPay's 12% national POS share suggests the underlying
  group had real distribution and merchant-relationship assets — the kind
  that transfer better to a B2B fintech model than to a subsidized consumer
  app fighting Southeast Asia's best-funded ride-hailing players.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

FastGo is a case of a founder correctly reading that they'd lost a capital
war, without needing to admit defeat publicly — the wind-down was gradual
and the team's real assets (merchant network, payments licenses, group
balance sheet) were redeployed into an adjacent business with structurally
better unit economics (B2B fees vs. subsidized consumer rides). Operators in
capital-intensive, subsidy-driven categories against foreign-funded
competitors should watch for this pattern: the exit doesn't have to be a
shutdown announcement if there's an adjacent line of business already inside
the same holding structure that can absorb the team and the market
relationships.
