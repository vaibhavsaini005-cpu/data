## One-liner

Hanjin Shipping, South Korea's largest container line and the world's seventh largest, doubled its debt to about US$8 billion by ordering and chartering big ships into the post-2008 freight collapse, was run for eight years by a chairwoman who admitted she lacked shipping expertise, and on 31 August 2016 filed for court receivership after state lender KDB refused to bridge the gap between the 550 billion won the Cho family offered and the 700 billion won creditors demanded; 98 ships carrying roughly US$14 billion of cargo were stranded worldwide, the company was declared bankrupt on 17 February 2017, and its asset sales recovered about 2% of US$10.5 billion owed, ending 40 years of Korean presence in a sector the state had treated as strategic.

## What happened

**The setup (1945–2008).** Cho Choong-hoon founded Hanjin in November 1945 with a single truck in the port of Incheon. Over six decades it became one of Korea's largest chaebol, spanning Korean Air, Hanjin Shipping and logistics. Hanjin Shipping grew into the country's flagship container carrier, moving more than 100 million tons of cargo and about 3.7 million TEU a year, operating some 20 container terminals with 22.4 million TEU of combined annual capacity, and in 2010 becoming the first line to put a 10,000 TEU class vessel on the Asia–Europe trade. Its Busan terminal employed around 11,000 people and Busan handled roughly 70% of Korea's container traffic. When founder Cho Choong-hoon died in 2002, the empire was split among his sons: the eldest, Cho Yang-ho, took Korean Air and the group chairmanship; the third son, Cho Soo-ho, took Hanjin Shipping.

**The succession problem (2006–2014).** Cho Soo-ho died of lung cancer in 2006. His widow, Choi Eun-young, a former flight attendant with no operating background, became chairwoman in 2007. In the years that followed she openly conceded that she lacked expertise in the business, yet the company made its most consequential capital decisions under her: from 2008 onward Hanjin ordered about 250,000 TEU of new ships and locked in long-term charters at boom-era rates. Seven large vessels delivered between 2010 and 2013 doubled the company's debt to about US$8 billion. The bet was that scale would restore margins after the global financial crisis. Instead, every major line was ordering bigger ships at once, capacity outran demand, and freight rates stayed depressed for most of the decade. By 2016 Hanjin owned only 44% of the fleet it operated; the other 56% was chartered, much of it at rates far above spot, which meant the cost base could not fall with revenue. Choi handed control to her brother-in-law Cho Yang-ho in April 2014, and Korean Air bought 33.2% of Hanjin Shipping in June 2014 to provide liquidity. By then, as later accounts put it, the company was already in very bad shape.

**The squeeze (April–August 2016).** In April 2016 Hanjin applied to its creditors, led by the state-owned Korea Development Bank (KDB), for a voluntary debt restructuring to avoid formal insolvency. The company sold what it could, raising 267.7 billion won from stakes in Vietnamese terminals, bulk carriers, trademark rights and overseas office property, and tried to renegotiate charter rates with shipowners, largely without success. It had 1.2 trillion won of loans maturing within a year and, by the CSIS account, less than US$200 million of cash against about US$6 billion of debt and more than US$1 billion of immediate rollover needs. Its domestic rival Hyundai Merchant Marine (HMM), in no better shape at the start of 2016, took the opposite path: it sold assets, won charter concessions from its shipowners, joined a cooperation with the Maersk–MSC 2M alliance, and handed most of its equity to creditors including KDB. Hanjin's owners did not. On 25–26 August 2016 Hanjin Group submitted its self-rescue plan: 550 billion won, of which 400 billion won would come through capital increases and 100 billion won was conditional. Creditors said they needed at least 700 billion won, with roughly 600 billion won required before year-end, and a KDB official called the offer "very disappointing." On 30 August creditors formally withdrew support. The same day, a creditor in Singapore arrested the vessel Hanjin Rome, an early sign of what would happen to every ship in the fleet once protection lapsed.

**The stranding (31 August–October 2016).** On 31 August 2016 Hanjin filed for receivership at the Seoul Central District Court, which placed the company under court management on 1 September. On 2 September it filed in the US Bankruptcy Court in Newark for recognition that would let its ships dock without arrest. The operational consequences were immediate and global: 98 ships (37 owned and about 62 chartered), roughly 623,000 TEU of capacity, about 3% of world container capacity and around 2,500 seafarers were at sea or anchored outside ports, because terminals, tug operators and fuel suppliers would not serve a carrier that could not pay and creditors could seize any vessel that berthed. Estimates of stranded cargo ranged from US$14 billion to US$17.5 billion, hitting retailers stocking for Black Friday and Christmas. Consignees paid two or three times over to retrieve their own containers. Korean Air and KDB put in about US$100 million of emergency funds to get cargo unloaded, and the Federation of Korea Maritime Industries estimated the damage to Korea's shipping industry at US$15 billion. President Park Geun-hye framed the decision as deliberate: "Lazy thinking that the government will have no choice but to help shippers if they run into problems has ended up hurting trading companies." The government helped exporters recover cargo but did not rescue the company. A bankruptcy trustee report later noted that within about three months competitors had absorbed Hanjin's services.

**The dismantling (October 2016–February 2017).** With no reorganisation possible, the court sold the business in pieces. On 21 November 2016 Korea Line (part of SM Group) agreed to buy the Asia–US route's operating rights, seven overseas subsidiaries, business information and about 570 employees for 37 billion won (US$31.4 million); SM Group used it to launch SM Line, which bought five former Hanjin vessels in February 2017 and the Gwangyang and Incheon terminals in March 2017. On 20 January 2017 a New Jersey bankruptcy judge approved the sale of Hanjin's 54% stake in Total Terminals International, the Long Beach terminal, to MSC's Terminal Investment Ltd for US$78 million plus US$54 million of debt relief, with HMM taking 20% of TTI and the equipment lessor HTEC for US$15.6 million; HMM also took over Hanjin's Tokyo and Kaohsiung terminals. On 17 February 2017 the Seoul Central District Court declared Hanjin Shipping bankrupt, and the US Chapter 11 case was converted to Chapter 7 liquidation. The last ship, the Hanjin Rome, was sold by the Singapore court and left for scrapping in Chittagong on 2 March 2017. By August 2017 the trustee had collected only about US$220 million from asset sales, around 2% of the US$10.5 billion owed to creditors.

**The aftermath (2017–2022).** In December 2017 the Seoul Central District Court sentenced Choi Eun-young to one and a half years in prison and fined her about US$1.09 million for selling her and her daughters' shares in April 2016, days before the restructuring application, avoiding roughly US$1 million of losses. HMM, the survivor, was effectively nationalised through KDB, and Korea's share of the global container market fell from about 5% to 3%. Writing in 2022, Korean insolvency lawyer Hyun Kim noted that a country which moves 99.7% of its trade by sea was left with a single global carrier, and that HMM's record profits in the 2021 freight boom were what Hanjin might have earned had it survived.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Ordering 250,000 TEU of new ships and chartering the rest at peak rates (Choi Eun-young's board, 2008–2013).** Committing to a fleet whose fixed costs assumed boom-era freight rates, in a sector where every competitor was doing the same, is the original decision. It doubled debt to about US$8 billion and, because 56% of the fleet was chartered-in, made the cost base impossible to shrink when rates fell. The scale bet only works if you are the last one making it.

- **Keeping an unqualified family successor in the chair for eight years (Hanjin Group, 2006–2014).** Chaebol succession norms put a chairwoman who admitted she lacked expertise in charge through the most capital-intensive stretch in the company's history. CSIS singled out the episode as the reason Koreans began questioning whether family control guarantees good management.

- **Choosing not to give up equity when HMM did (Cho Yang-ho and Hanjin Group, spring–summer 2016).** HMM's owners handed most of the company to creditors, won charter cuts and secured alliance cover; they survived. Hanjin's owners offered 550 billion won against a 700 billion won ask and kept control. The 150 billion won gap, less than US$150 million, is the price at which a US$10.5 billion company was allowed to die.

- **KDB and the Park government refusing to bridge the gap (30 August 2016).** The state's choice to let its flagship carrier fail, rather than absorb losses from a chaebol family it judged was not paying its share, broke the assumption that Korea's big companies would always be rescued. It was a policy decision as much as a credit one, and FreightWaves reported speculation that Cho Yang-ho's refusal to donate to foundations tied to President Park did not help.

- **Filing without a contingency for ships at sea (31 August 2016).** The receivership was filed with no funded plan to keep ships moving, so protection in Korea meant paralysis everywhere else. The stranding of roughly US$14 billion of other people's cargo is what turned a corporate insolvency into a global supply-chain event and destroyed any residual customer trust that could have supported a reorganisation.

- **The court selling the network in pieces rather than as a going concern (October 2016–February 2017).** Once customers had fled, the only saleable assets were terminals and route rights. Korea Line paid 37 billion won for the core Asia–US business; the Long Beach terminal fetched US$78 million. Total recovery of about 2% of claims shows how little a shipping line is worth once its cargo is gone.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Hanjin is the reference case for capacity bets in a commodity service business: when the product is undifferentiated, ordering more of it during a boom is a wager that you can outlast everyone else's identical wager, and a chartered cost base removes the option of shrinking when you lose. Operators in freight, cloud capacity, ride-hailing supply or any leased-fleet model will recognise the shape. The second pattern is owner behaviour in a rescue. Creditors judge restructurings by whether the people who made the mistakes are willing to pay for them, and HMM versus Hanjin is a controlled experiment: same country, same lender, same year, same industry, with the survivor the one whose owners gave up equity first. Third, the case shows that insolvency protection is jurisdictional but operations are not. A service business with assets constantly crossing borders needs its liquidity bridge arranged before it files, not after, or the filing itself destroys the asset it was meant to protect. Finally, it is a lesson about implicit guarantees: Hanjin's owners, customers and lenders all priced in a state backstop that the state, when tested, declined to provide, and the resulting shock reshaped how Korea thinks about both bailouts and family succession at its conglomerates.
