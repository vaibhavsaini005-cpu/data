## One-liner

Once South Korea's largest ridesharing platform with over 1 million users and 22 billion won ($16M) from Naver and SK, Poolus shut down in March 2024 after a decade-long war of attrition with taxi-industry regulation.

## What happened

Poolus launched as a real-time carpool-matching app and quickly became South Korea's leading ridesharing platform, drawing investment from major backers including Naver and SK Group that totaled roughly 22 billion won ($16M) over its life. It built a user base of more than 1 million riders at its peak.

The company's core problem was never product-market fit — it was regulation. South Korea's Passenger Transport Service Act restricts private carpooling to narrow commute-hour windows, a rule aimed at protecting the country's politically powerful taxi industry. Poolus (and rivals like Kakao's carpool service) spent years in standoffs with taxi unions and lawmakers, repeatedly scaling back hours and features to stay compliant, which hollowed out the convenience that made ridesharing viable in the first place. By the Korea Herald and Korea Times' 2018 coverage, Poolus was already described as "falling apart" amid these financial and regulatory pressures. The company limped on for several more years but never regained momentum, and formally discontinued its ridesharing business in March 2024.

Poolus was one of 68 previously-funded Korean startups that shut down in the first half of 2024 alone — double the closures of the same period in 2023 — with platform businesses like Poolus accounting for 57% of that total, per BusinessKorea's August 2024 reporting.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Betting on a regulated-adjacent model**: Poolus built its entire product around carpool-matching in a market where full ridesharing (Uber-style) was already effectively banned, forcing it to operate inside a narrow, legally-defined commute window from day one.
- **Years of incremental regulatory retreat**: Rather than one decisive pivot, Poolus made a series of small concessions to taxi-industry pressure (restricting hours, limiting drivers) that each individually seemed survivable but collectively eroded the product's usefulness.
- **No successful pivot away from ridesharing**: Unlike some peers that diversified into logistics or B2B mobility, Poolus stayed in its core lane and simply ran out of runway after the funding environment tightened industry-wide in 2023-2024.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Poolus is a textbook case of a well-funded startup losing not to competitors or poor execution but to a regulatory environment explicitly designed to protect an incumbent industry. Founders entering markets with strong incumbent political protection (taxis, in this case) need a realistic model of how much of their product's value proposition survives after inevitable regulatory concessions — Poolus's "carpool, but only during commute hours, with restrictions" ended up not different enough from the status quo to justify its cost structure. It's also a reminder that startups can carry sizable, credible institutional backing (Naver, SK) and still fail slowly over years rather than collapsing suddenly.
