## One-liner

Japan's largest retailer spent a year fending off the biggest foreign takeover bid ever made for a Japanese company, and did it by executing, under duress, the breakup an activist had demanded and management had rejected two years earlier: it sold its founding supermarket business to Bain Capital for ¥814.7 billion, installed its first foreign CEO, promised a ¥2 trillion buyback and a US listing of 7-Eleven, and was left standing alone when Couche-Tard walked away in July 2025, with the US IPO since pushed to fiscal 2027.

## What happened

**The setup (2005 to 2023).** Seven & i Holdings was formed in 2005 as a holding company above 7-Eleven Japan, the US operator 7-Eleven Inc. (SEI), the Ito-Yokado general-merchandise superstores that founder Masatoshi Ito had built from the 1950s, York-Benimaru supermarkets, Denny's Japan, Loft, Akachan Honpo, Seven Bank and a long tail of specialty retailers. The convenience-store business generated nearly all the profit. Ito-Yokado, the business that gave the group its name and its founding family, had lost money for years. Foreign shareholders saw a conglomerate discount: a world-class convenience-store franchise with a low-return domestic retail estate strapped to it. In April 2023 San Francisco activist ValueAct Capital, holding 4.4%, wrote to the board demanding a spin-off of 7-Eleven and a sale of Ito-Yokado, and nominated four directors. At the 25 May 2023 annual meeting, President Ryuichi Isaka and every management nominee were re-elected and ValueAct's slate was rejected, but reported approval for key incumbents fell from above 90% a year earlier to the 65 to 76% range. Management had won the vote and lost the argument.

**The bid (July to October 2024).** On 25 July 2024 Canada's Alimentation Couche-Tard, operator of Circle K, delivered a confidential, non-binding proposal to buy the whole group. Seven & i disclosed it in August and formed a special committee of independent directors chaired by Stephen Dacus, an American who had run Walmart Japan and Fast Retailing's Uniqlo USA and sat on the board as lead independent outside director. On 6 September 2024 Dacus rejected the offer in an open letter: $14.86 per share in cash, about $39 billion, which "grossly undervalues" the company, provided no plan for the US antitrust divestitures a combination of the two largest US convenience-store chains would require, and underestimated 7-Eleven's role in everyday Japanese life. Couche-Tard came back in early October with roughly $47 billion, about ¥7 trillion, a 20% increase.

**The defensive restructuring (10 October 2024).** With the higher offer on the table, Isaka announced the plan ValueAct had asked for. Thirty-one non-core businesses, including Ito-Yokado, York-Benimaru, Denny's Japan, Loft and Akachan Honpo, would be carved into an intermediate holding company, York Holdings, for which the group would seek strategic partners or an IPO. The parent would rename itself 7-Eleven Corporation. The same day it cut full-year operating-profit guidance on weak sales. The message to shareholders was that a standalone, convenience-only Seven & i could deliver the value Couche-Tard was offering to pay for.

**The family's counter-bid and its collapse (November 2024 to February 2025).** On 13 November 2024 Junro Ito, the founder's son, a vice president of Seven & i and owner through Ito-Kogyo of about 8% of the stock, submitted a non-binding proposal to take the company private for up to ¥9 trillion, which would have been the largest management buyout ever attempted. The shares closed up more than 11%. The financing plan relied on Japan's megabanks, private equity and trading house Itochu, which was expected to put in around ¥1 trillion. On 26 February 2025 Itochu withdrew, citing limited synergies and the obvious conflict that it owns FamilyMart, 7-Eleven's direct competitor. On 28 February Ito-Kogyo told the special committee it could not raise the money. The shares fell sharply and Couche-Tard was once again the only bidder.

**The standalone plan (6 March 2025).** A week later the board announced the package that defines the pivot. Dacus would replace Isaka as President and CEO after the May 2025 annual meeting, the first non-Japanese chief executive in the group's history, with Isaka moved to senior advisor. York Holdings would be sold to Bain Capital at an enterprise value of ¥814.7 billion, about $5.4 billion, with Seven & i rolling 35% of its equity and the founding family a further stake so that Bain held 60%. SEI, the North American business, would be listed on a major US exchange in the second half of 2026, with Seven & i keeping a majority. The group would cut its Seven Bank stake below 40% to deconsolidate it and commit about ¥2 trillion, roughly $13 billion, to share buybacks through fiscal 2030, funded by the Bain proceeds and the IPO. The committee said it was still discussing an antitrust divestiture package with Couche-Tard, with no assured timeline.

**The end of the bid (March to July 2025).** Couche-Tard's version is that after signing an NDA and a December 2024 term sheet, which included a reverse termination fee of about $1.2 billion, it received only 14 files on the US business in ten weeks, found buyers for the divested stores by 31 March 2025, and then met a "calculated campaign of obfuscation and delay". It also offered an alternative structure: buy all non-Japan operations and 40% of the Japanese business, leaving Seven & i shareholders 60% of the parent. Seven & i countered on 1 July 2025 in Tokyo. On 16 July 2025 Couche-Tard withdrew its ¥2,600-per-share cash proposal, a 47.6% premium to the unaffected price, citing "no sincere or constructive engagement". Seven & i's reply the next day said it was "disappointed" and "not surprised", disputed the "numerous inaccurate statements" and pointed at changed exchange rates, financing markets and "extraordinary antitrust hurdles". The shares fell about 7% on the news.

**Living with the plan (September 2025 to April 2026).** The York Holdings sale to Bain closed on 1 September 2025, with Bain reported to be planning a relisting in about three years. On 9 April 2026, alongside results for the year to February 2026 that showed overseas segment revenue down about 7% in the quarter and same-store sales in the US slipping, Seven & i pushed the SEI listing to fiscal 2027 "at the earliest", blaming market volatility and softer North American consumer spending. The buyback commitment stands; the exit valuation of the US business that was supposed to prove the standalone thesis has been deferred.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Rejecting ValueAct's breakup in 2023, then executing it in 2024 (Isaka and the board).** The board won the May 2023 vote at the cost of a collapse in its approval ratings, and eighteen months later announced the York Holdings carve-out and Ito-Yokado exit that the activist had asked for. The substance was the same; what changed was that a $47 billion bid made the price of keeping the conglomerate structure explicit. The lesson management drew, and eventually acted on, was that the discount was real.

- **Handing the process to an independent, foreign special committee chair (Dacus, August 2024).** Putting an American Walmart and Uniqlo veteran in front of the bid, rather than the founder's family or the incumbent CEO, gave the rejection credibility with foreign shareholders and with Japan's 2023 M&A guidelines, which require boards to take bona fide offers seriously. It also created the succession path: the same person ended up running the company.

- **Betting on the founding family's buyout as a white knight (November 2024).** The ¥9 trillion Ito-Kogyo proposal was a way to keep the company Japanese and private. It failed on a structural conflict that was visible from the start: the trading house that owns FamilyMart was never a natural anchor investor for 7-Eleven. When it collapsed, the company had spent three months and lost the option of a quiet, friendly exit.

- **Selling the founding business to private equity and replacing the CEO (6 March 2025).** This is the pivot itself. A ¥814.7 billion sale of Ito-Yokado and thirty other units to Bain, with a 35% rollover, ended sixty years of the group being defined by its superstores. Naming Dacus CEO signalled to the market that the standalone plan would be run by the person who had written the rejection letters.

- **Choosing a US IPO and a ¥2 trillion buyback over Couche-Tard's cash (March to July 2025).** The board chose to realise the value of SEI itself rather than sell it. The mechanism, a listing and buybacks funded by it, ties shareholder returns to a US capital-markets window the company does not control. The April 2026 delay to fiscal 2027 is the first consequence of that choice.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Seven & i is the reference case for **an incumbent that agrees with an activist's diagnosis only after a hostile bidder prices it**. ValueAct in 2023, Couche-Tard in 2024 and the board's own March 2025 plan all described the same company: a world-class convenience-store franchise carrying low-return legacy retail. The board rejected the breakup when the cost of saying no was a proxy vote, and adopted it when the cost was a $47 billion takeover. Operators will recognise the pattern from any founder-led or family-influenced group where the legacy business is the founding one. The structural change was inevitable; the sequencing decided who captured the value and who got to run the company afterwards.

Second, it shows **how the takeover defence became the strategy**. Every element of the standalone plan, the York carve-out, the Bain sale, the US IPO, the buyback and the foreign CEO, was announced in the window between a bid and a deadline. Seven & i is now committed to delivering a valuation through a US listing that has already slipped a year, without the cash a sale would have paid. A defence that works by promising the bidder's value on your own timetable has to keep that promise in public.

Third, the failed family buyout is a clean example of **a white-knight structure that fails on a conflict everyone could see**. Itochu owning FamilyMart made it the wrong anchor for a 7-Eleven buyout, and the ¥8 to 9 trillion size left no room for a partner to be squeamish. Founders who look to a friendly consortium to block an outside bid should test whether the friendliest party can actually close before the market learns that they can't.
