## One-liner

Singapore's TradeGecko was acquired by Intuit in 2020, rebranded as QuickBooks Commerce, then progressively wound down by 2023 — an exit that transformed from a success story into a cautionary study of product-fit misalignment inside an acquirer.

## What happened

TradeGecko was founded in Singapore in 2012, building cloud-based inventory and order management software targeted at small and medium businesses. By the time of its acquisition it served customers in more than 100 countries, having raised roughly $17M in venture funding. In August 2020 Intuit announced it would acquire TradeGecko with the transaction closing in September 2020, positioning it as the commerce layer that would plug into QuickBooks Online's accounting stack.

Intuit rebranded the product as QuickBooks Commerce, selling it as an omnichannel inventory add-on for existing QuickBooks customers. But the integration strategy quickly revealed a structural problem: TradeGecko's standalone international customer base was incompatible with Intuit's US-centric QuickBooks Online ecosystem. By June 2021 Intuit announced it would sunset the standalone version. Standalone QuickBooks Commerce (legacy TradeGecko accounts) was shut down on July 10, 2022. Remaining Commerce functionality was available only with a QuickBooks Online subscription — and only for US customers — with full discontinuation completed by August 31, 2023.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Acquirer scope mismatch**: TradeGecko's strength was serving international SMBs globally; Intuit's QuickBooks Online had limited international footprint, leaving TradeGecko's existing customer base orphaned by the integration strategy.
- **Rebrand to QuickBooks Commerce**: stripping the standalone identity and folding into the QuickBooks brand confused non-US customers who had no path to the acquirer's core product.
- **Failure to rebuild the inventory module natively**: Intuit appeared to acquire TradeGecko to fast-track a commerce capability rather than build it, but ultimately rebuilt natively anyway — the acquisition bought speed but not a sustainable product strategy.
- **Customer mass exodus**: the sunset created a public exodus that benefited direct competitors (Unleashed, Cin7, Ordoro), who documented the process in detail — a rare case where a shutdown became others' customer-acquisition campaign.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

TradeGecko is a textbook example of the "acqui-capability" pattern: an acquirer buys a product not for its existing customers but for its IP and team to accelerate building something adjacent. For Singapore's startup ecosystem it illustrated that an exit to a US mega-corp is not a guaranteed durable outcome — product-market fit within the acquirer matters as much as the acquisition price. The story also underscores how international-first SaaS products struggle to retain strategic value inside US-anchored acquirers whose go-to-market infrastructure is domestic by default.
