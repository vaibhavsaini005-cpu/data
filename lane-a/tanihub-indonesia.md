## One-liner

Indonesian B2B agritech platform TaniHub shut down its warehousing and B2C operations in February 2022 after failing to close a Series B round during the global funding downturn, then ceased all operations by April 2023.

## What happened

TaniHub was founded in 2016 by Wilson Yanaprasetya and Pamitra Wineka in Bandung, Indonesia. It built an end-to-end supply chain platform connecting smallholder farmers directly to commercial buyers (restaurants, hotels, food processors), eliminating traditional middlemen and claiming to deliver fresher produce at better farmgate prices. The company raised approximately $65.5 million in total — a $10 million Series A in 2019 from Openspace Ventures, MDI Ventures, and others, plus bridge rounds — and operated warehouse hubs in Bandung, Surabaya, and Bali.

In early 2022, as the global venture pullback gathered pace, TaniHub was unable to close a planned Series B. The company announced in February 2022 that it was shutting down its B2C retail arm (Tani Store) and closing warehouse operations, citing "market timing." The B2B marketplace operation briefly continued but by April 2023 TaniHub had wound down entirely.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Premature vertical integration**: TaniHub expanded into last-mile warehousing and cold-chain logistics, increasing capex significantly at a time when unit economics in Indonesian agritech were still unproven. This made the business capital-intensive at the wrong moment.
- **B2C retail distraction**: The consumer-facing Tani Store required marketing spend and inventory risk that diverted focus from the B2B marketplace, where network effects were more defensible.
- **Timing the Series B into a downturn**: Like several SEA startups that tried to raise in Q4 2021–Q1 2022, TaniHub found institutional appetite had dried up while its burn rate remained high.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

TaniHub is representative of a cohort of SEA agritech startups (alongside Vietnamese Greenfeed models and Philippine agri-players) that over-invested in physical logistics infrastructure assuming growth-stage capital would remain available. The business model was sound in theory — smallholder fragmentation in Indonesian agriculture is a genuine market failure — but the path to profitability required sustained capital that evaporated faster than product-market-fit could be proven. The lesson: marketplaces with embedded logistics need to phase capex after demand, not before.
