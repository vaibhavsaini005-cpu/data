## One-liner

South Korea's homegrown OTT challenger raised $89M and hit an ~$800M valuation in 2021, then was forced to retreat from every international market within two years as Netflix outspent it on Korean originals and domestic conglomerates locked up broadcast libraries.

## What happened

Watcha was founded in 2011 by Park Tae-hoon in Seoul, initially as a recommendation engine called Watcha Pedia — users rated what they had watched and received personalised suggestions. In 2016 the company pivoted into subscription streaming with Watcha Play (later renamed Watcha), building a licensed library of Korean dramas, films, and international titles.

By 2021 Watcha had approximately 2 million monthly active subscribers in South Korea and had expanded into Japan. In December 2021, it raised an $89M Series D from investors including UTC Investment and Kakao Entertainment, reaching a reported valuation of around 1 trillion KRW (roughly $800M USD). On the back of that capital, Watcha launched in the US, targeting Korean diaspora audiences and K-content enthusiasts.

The timing was structurally bad. Netflix had responded to the global K-content boom by pouring hundreds of millions of dollars into Korean originals — "Squid Game," "Hellbound," "All of Us Are Dead" — making it the dominant global curator of Korean content. Domestically, Wavve (backed by the KBS/MBC/SBS public broadcasters) and TVING (backed by CJ ENM and JTBC) held deep libraries of broadcast content that Watcha could not license. Watcha was caught between better-funded global players above and better-connected local conglomerates below.

By late 2022, Watcha was in visible financial distress. The company laid off staff, suspended its US streaming service, and pulled out of Taiwan. In early 2023 it suspended all remaining international operations and accepted an emergency bridge loan from IMM Private Equity to stay solvent. Reports circulated about potential acquisition by a larger conglomerate. By mid-2023 Watcha had contracted to a Korea-and-Japan-only service, significantly smaller than its 2021 peak.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Licensed content over originals:** Watcha built on third-party licensed content rather than producing its own, leaving it with a thin moat — competitors could simply outbid it for the same titles.
- **International expansion while cash-constrained:** Launching in the US against Netflix's Korean-originals budget, with no original content to differentiate, was tactically incoherent.
- **Emergency financing:** Accepting a survival bridge from IMM in early 2023 marked the official end of the growth narrative — the company entered preservation mode at exactly the moment it needed to compete hardest.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Watcha illustrates a failure mode specific to mid-sized OTT entrants: entering streaming as a content aggregator in an era when deep-pocketed incumbents are differentiating on originals. The platform assumed that curation and recommendation quality could substitute for content exclusivity, but in practice subscribers followed the shows, not the recommendation engine. The pattern — regional challenger outflanked by a global platform's local content investment — has repeated across Southeast Asia and Europe, making Watcha a useful archetype for any market where Netflix is competing with Korean, Turkish, or local content as the primary weapon.
