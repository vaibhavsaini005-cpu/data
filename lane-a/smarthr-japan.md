## One-liner

SmartHR became Japan's leading cloud HR and payroll platform, reaching a $1.7 billion valuation in 2021 by solving a distinctly Japanese compliance pain point: the country's notoriously paper-heavy social insurance and employment administration.

## What happened

SmartHR was founded in 2013 by Naoya Miyata (originally as Kufu Company) in Tokyo and launched its core product in 2015 — a cloud SaaS tool that automates the filing of employee social insurance, tax withholding, and employment paperwork with Japanese government agencies. Japan's paper-first administrative culture meant even large companies were still mailing physical forms to local government offices; SmartHR converted that workflow to API calls.

The company grew steadily through the late 2010s serving SMEs, expanded upmarket to enterprise HR management (goal-setting, performance reviews, organisational analytics) through 2019–2022, and raised a $140 million Series D in October 2021 led by Sequoia Capital's US fund — at the time the largest-ever venture round for a Japanese B2B SaaS company. By early 2023 it had exceeded 60,000 corporate customers, making it the dominant player in Japanese cloud HRM.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Deep compliance moat**: SmartHR embedded itself into Japanese labour law workflows (specifically shakai hoken — social insurance filings), creating switching costs that generic HR tools from abroad couldn't easily replicate without Japanese regulatory expertise.
- **Bottom-up then enterprise**: Early growth was via self-serve SME adoption; SmartHR then built an enterprise tier with compensation management, performance analytics, and API integrations — a classic PLG-to-enterprise motion applied to a very un-Americanised market.
- **US institutional backing**: The Sequoia Series D brought not just capital but validation that Japanese B2B SaaS could scale to international VC standards; this attracted follow-on talent and enterprise deals.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

SmartHR is a template for how to build a durable SaaS franchise in a regulation-dense market: start with the compliance workflow nobody else wants to touch (because it requires deep local knowledge), accumulate a defensible customer base, then layer higher-value product modules on top. It is also a counterpoint to the narrative that Japanese startups can't attract global capital — the key was not just growth but category leadership and gross margin visibility.
