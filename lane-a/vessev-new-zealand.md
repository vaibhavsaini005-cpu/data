## One-liner

Auckland-based electric hydrofoil boat maker Vessev, led by co-founder and CEO Eric Laakmann, raised $32.6 million (US$19M) in Series A and follow-on financing on August 21, 2026, to fund a push into the US market.

## What happened

Vessev designs and manufactures electric hydrofoiling passenger vessels in Auckland, New Zealand, with its first commercial vessel entering service with ferry operator Fullers in Auckland in 2025. On August 21, 2026, the company announced it had raised $32.6 million (US$19 million) in Series A and follow-on financing, led by Blackbird Ventures — an early backer of Canva and one of Australia/New Zealand's leading venture firms — with new investors GD1 and Rypples joining existing backers K1W1, Icehouse Ventures, Shasta Ventures, and NZVC. Co-founder and CEO Eric Laakmann framed the vessels' core advantage as enabling "more point-to-point transfers" without relying on larger vessels. The capital is earmarked for in-house serial production of Vessev's VS-9 line, an expanded global go-to-market team, onboard software and telemetry development, larger vessel development, and US manufacturing plans.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Proving the technology commercially in a home market first** (the 2025 Fullers Auckland ferry deployment) before raising a large round to fund international expansion, rather than raising ahead of a working commercial reference customer.
- **Laakmann's bet on point-to-point hydrofoil transit as the wedge** — smaller, faster electric vessels serving direct routes — rather than competing on vessel size or route consolidation against incumbent ferry operators.
- **Committing raised capital to US manufacturing** rather than exporting from New Zealand, signaling an intent to localize production for the US market rather than scale a single home-country factory.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Vessev is a case of a deep-tech hardware startup using a real, revenue-generating commercial deployment (a working ferry route) as proof before scaling internationally — a more capital-disciplined path than many hardware startups take. It's also a data point on New Zealand's marine/deep-tech sector attracting serious late-stage venture capital (Blackbird) for hardware, not just software, when the underlying technology has an operating commercial reference customer.
