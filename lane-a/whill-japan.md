## One-liner

WHILL, the Tokyo-founded personal mobility startup that started out selling a single premium power wheelchair to consumers, used a 2018 $45M raise and a 2022 Toyota-backed round to pivot into operating autonomous wheelchair fleets as a service inside airports — turning a hardware sales business into a recurring mobility-as-a-service (MaaS) one.

## What happened

WHILL was founded in 2012 by Satoshi Sugie and co-founders after Sugie heard a wheelchair user in Japan say he'd stopped going to a convenience store two blocks from his house because the trip itself had become too much of a hassle — the company's founding story, repeated in its own materials and in interviews, is built around that one sentence. WHILL launched its first product, the Model A power chair, in 2014: a premium, design-forward electric wheelchair aimed at consumers, sold directly rather than through the traditional medical-device/insurance-reimbursement channel most competitors relied on. Model A was expensive and bulky, and consumer sales alone were not enough to build a large business on — WHILL followed it in 2017 with the more portable, foldable Model C, which became the company's volume product and the base for what came next.

Starting around 2018-2019, WHILL began repositioning itself away from being purely a device manufacturer selling chairs one at a time, and toward being an operator of mobility services inside large venues — airports, stadiums, shopping centers — where the buyer became the venue operator (paying for a fleet-as-a-service contract), not the individual rider. The September 2018 $45M Series C, led by SBI Investment and Daiwa entities and joined by Endeavor Catalyst, explicitly funded this shift: WHILL's own release described building "the technology and infrastructure to efficiently and safely move people through large and crowded venues such as airports, sports venues, shopping centers, and public sidewalks," alongside continued expansion of the consumer product line and a first push into Europe. WHILL ran autonomous-driving pilots at Haneda and Narita in Japan and at Heathrow, then in the US at LaGuardia, and by late 2019 TechCrunch reported WHILL's autonomous chairs entering commercial or pilot service at additional North American airports, with a permanent installation at Tokyo's Haneda Airport. The MaaS bet was reinforced again in May 2022, when Toyota's growth-stage venture arm Woven Capital led a new round explicitly to help WHILL "scale production globally" and shift more of its resources "to the service side of its business," following successful autonomous-service trials at San Jose International and additional US and Canadian airports.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Model A → Model C (2014 → 2017): premium bulky chair to portable everyday chair.** The Model C's compact, foldable design and tight turning radius (via patented front omni-wheels) made WHILL's device usable in the tight spaces of consumer daily life, not just as a showpiece — this is what made a fleet-deployment model even feasible later, since a fleet of chairs needs to be easy to store, move, and turn around in crowded terminals.
- **Selling direct-to-consumer, not through medical/insurance channels (from founding).** This kept WHILL outside the reimbursement-driven, slower-moving durable-medical-equipment sales cycle that constrains most wheelchair makers, but it also meant WHILL's total addressable consumer market was capped by what individuals would pay out of pocket — a real constraint that made the venue-operator pivot commercially necessary, not just opportunistic.
- **The 2018 Series C's explicit MaaS reallocation.** Rather than just plowing new capital into more consumer manufacturing and marketing, WHILL used the raise to build autonomous-driving and fleet-operations capability aimed at a completely different buyer (a venue, not a rider) — a genuine business-model pivot funded by a single strategic decision at the board/fundraising level, not an incremental feature add.
- **Picking airports as the proving ground, starting with Haneda/Narita/Heathrow and expanding to LaGuardia, San Jose and others (2018–2022).** Airports gave WHILL a controlled, high-need environment (20M+ passengers a year need wheelchair assistance at US airports alone, per WHILL's own figures) to prove autonomous fleet operations were safe and reliable before trying to sell the model to lower-controlled venues like malls or stadiums.
- **Bringing in Toyota's Woven Capital in 2022** tied WHILL's MaaS ambitions to an investor with its own direct interest in autonomous mobility infrastructure, not just a financial backer — a signal the pivot had moved from "unproven bet" to a strategy sophisticated mobility-industry capital was willing to back a second time, four years after the first MaaS-focused raise.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

WHILL's story is a pattern other hardware founders should recognize: a well-designed, well-funded consumer product can be necessary but not sufficient — Model A proved WHILL could build something people wanted, but the business only became durable once WHILL stopped treating "sell one chair to one rider" as the only revenue model and started selling fleet contracts to venue operators who had both the budget and the operational need to pay for mobility as infrastructure. The pivot didn't require abandoning the original hardware — it required finding a second, structurally different buyer for the same underlying technology, funded deliberately (twice, four years apart, by increasingly strategic investors) rather than discovered by accident.
