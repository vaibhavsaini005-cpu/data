## One-liner

Auror, founded in Auckland in 2013, built the world's leading retail crime intelligence platform — used by 90% of New Zealand's major retailers and 32,000+ stores globally — and raised $82M in a 2024 Series C led by Axon (the Taser maker) at a $500M+ valuation.

## What happened

Auror (originally launched as Eyedentify) was founded in 2013 by Phil Thomson and Tom Batterbury out of the University of Auckland's Velocity startup programme. The core product is a shared intelligence platform: when a retail loss prevention team logs an offender at one store, the system automatically flags that pattern for other retailers and police in the same area, creating a network effect that improves detection rates for all participants.

The company gained market traction by solving a specific coordination failure — retailers rarely shared offender data with competitors or police in a structured, searchable way. By 2023, Auror was used by 90% of New Zealand enterprise retailers, had expanded across Australia and was entering North America and the UK. In December 2021 the company raised NZ$30M led by Movac. In late 2024, Auror closed an $82M Series C led by Axon Enterprise (Nasdaq: AXON) and W23 (backed by Woolworths), valuing the company at over $500M. Revenue had grown 60% year-on-year at the time of the raise.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Shared network model**: Rather than selling siloed software to individual retailers, Auror built a shared intelligence layer. The network effect meant each new retailer improved the system for all existing users — a rare compounding moat in vertical SaaS.
- **Police integration**: Connecting retailers directly to law enforcement (over 2,000 agencies) reduced the friction of reporting incidents and dramatically improved closure rates, making the ROI case easy to sell to procurement teams.
- **Axon as lead investor (2024)**: Choosing a strategic lead — Axon makes body cameras, Tasers, and digital evidence software for police — brought deep channel access into North American law enforcement relationships while validating the product roadmap toward AI-assisted video evidence.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Auror illustrates how a small-country startup can build a global vertical SaaS business by owning a coordination problem no single participant has the incentive to solve alone. Starting in a market small enough to reach 90% penetration quickly (New Zealand) proved the model before expanding — a distribution-first, network-first approach that is replicable in any fragmented industry where information asymmetry creates crime or fraud.
