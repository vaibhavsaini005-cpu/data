## One-liner

Southeast Asia's biggest studio-backed streaming joint venture filed for liquidation in March 2020, overwhelmed by Netflix's regional expansion and a structural misalignment between its three corporate parents.

## What happened

HOOQ Digital launched in January 2015 as a regional video-on-demand joint venture between Singtel (majority stake), Sony Pictures Television, and Warner Bros. Entertainment. It operated across Singapore, the Philippines, Thailand, Indonesia, and India, offering a catalogue of 10,000+ movies and TV series including major Hollywood titles, plus local language originals. At peak it claimed around 80 million registered users across its markets.

Despite the headline user numbers, HOOQ never reached commercial sustainability. Netflix launched across Southeast Asia in January 2016 and within a year had reshaped consumer expectations around catalogue depth, original content, and global brand trust. HOOQ's three corporate parents — each of which was simultaneously licensing content to Netflix under separate agreements — never resolved the conflict of interest at the heart of the venture. HOOQ could not secure exclusive or first-window rights to the Sony and Warner libraries it ostensibly owned as a joint venture partner, because those studios were getting higher licensing fees from Netflix globally.

By 2019 regional streaming was crowding further with Disney+, Amazon Prime Video, and well-funded local entrants (Viu, WeTV). HOOQ was unable to raise additional external capital. On 27 March 2020 it filed for voluntary liquidation in Singapore. Operations ceased on 30 April 2020.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **JV structure created a content conflict from day one**: Sony and Warner Bros. were HOOQ's co-parents and simultaneously Netflix's largest content suppliers. HOOQ could never obtain meaningful exclusivity over the content that was supposed to differentiate it.
- **Late pivot to local originals**: HOOQ invested in Philippine and Thai original productions in 2018–2019 — the correct strategic direction — but several years behind Netflix's head start on local content investment in the region.
- **Pricing and UX never closed the gap**: HOOQ launched free-with-ads and shifted to subscription, but sustained a reputation for inferior UX and slower content refresh rates compared to Netflix.
- **COVID-19 was the accelerant, not the cause**: The March 2020 filing coincided with pandemic lockdowns, but HOOQ confirmed that emergency financing talks had already failed before COVID arrived.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

HOOQ is a canonical example of the **incumbent JV trap in media tech**: a well-funded joint venture where the founders' core interests are fundamentally misaligned with the venture's success. For the three parent companies, HOOQ was a defensive hedge against Netflix — not a priority growth vehicle. The lesson for the region: streaming services backed by studios that also license globally to competitors cannot win on content differentiation. The Southeast Asian streaming services that survived (Viu, iQIYI, WeTV) were either single-parent focused or had exclusive access to a distinct content library (Korean drama via iQIYI, Chinese content via WeTV).
