## One-liner

An 84-year-old family-run Japanese supplier, one of the world's largest airbag makers, chose a propellant its own engineers and every major rival had rejected because it was far cheaper, then concealed test failures for over a decade; the result was the largest recall in automotive history, at least 17 deaths, a $1 billion US criminal plea, and the biggest bankruptcy of a Japanese manufacturer, with the good assets sold to a Chinese-owned rival for $1.6 billion.

## What happened

**The setup (1988 to early 2000s).** Takata began making airbag inflators in 1988 using sodium azide, the industry's original propellant, and moved in the 1990s to tetrazole, a safer but costlier compound. Takata's chemists then developed an inflator around phase-stabilised ammonium nitrate (PSAN), a fertiliser and industrial explosive. The New York Times' 2016 investigation reconstructed the moment the bet was placed: in the late 1990s General Motors received an "unexpected and enticing" offer from Takata for cheaper airbags built around the new compound. GM asked its incumbent supplier Autoliv to match it. Autoliv's senior scientist Linda Rink and head chemist Robert Taylor analysed the design and refused, saying they could not use a "dangerously volatile compound"; in Autoliv's own destructive testing, per team member Chris Hock, "when we lit it off, it totally destroyed the fixture." Ammonium nitrate was reported to be about ten times cheaper than tetrazole and Takata's modules as much as 30% cheaper. Inside Takata, engineer Mark Lillie raised the same objection in the late 1990s and was overruled. Takata's Moses Lake, Washington inflator plant had already suffered explosions in 1997 that crippled production and forced it to buy inflators from competitors.

**Why the chemistry mattered.** Ammonium nitrate is hygroscopic and changes crystal phase with temperature cycling. Over years in a hot, humid cabin the propellant wafers absorb moisture and break down into a more porous form that burns far faster than designed. When the inflator fires, pressure spikes beyond what the steel canister can hold and it ruptures, turning the housing into shrapnel aimed at the driver or passenger. Other airbag makers had ruled the compound out for exactly this reason. Takata bet that its "phase stabilisation" additive solved the problem. In production, with humidity ingress over 8 to 15 years of vehicle life, it did not.

**The concealment (2000 to 2015).** The first documented rupture came in May 2004 in a 2002 model-year vehicle in Alabama; Takata was notified in 2005. The US Department of Justice later established that from around 2000 Takata knew certain PSAN inflators were failing specification and rupturing in testing, and that it kept winning business by submitting falsified test reports to automakers. The indictment of three Takata executives, Shinichi Tanaka, Hideo Nakajima and Tsuneo Chikaraishi, described deleting, altering and manipulating ballistic and effluent-gas test data, including removing records of ruptures, with the conduct running through roughly 2015.

**The unravelling (2008 to 2017).** Honda began small recalls in 2008. Recalls widened year after year as ruptures and deaths accumulated, and by 2014 the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) had opened a formal investigation. On 3 November 2015 NHTSA imposed the largest civil penalty in its history: up to $200 million ($70 million payable, the rest held in abeyance), plus a consent order requiring Takata to stop taking new contracts for PSAN inflators in the US and to phase out non-desiccated PSAN inflators entirely by the end of 2018. The same day Honda, Takata's largest customer, announced it would no longer use Takata's PSAN inflators in new models. On 27 February 2017 Takata pleaded guilty in Detroit to one count of wire fraud and was sentenced to $1 billion in criminal penalties: a $25 million fine, a $125 million fund for injured victims, and $850 million in restitution to automakers who had borne the recall costs. It also had to retain an independent compliance monitor for three years.

**The collapse (June 2017 to April 2018).** With global recalls covering roughly 100 million inflators, recall costs estimated near $10 billion, at least 17 deaths linked worldwide, and automakers holding claims against it, Takata could not fund the liability. On 25 June 2017 its US arm TK Holdings filed for Chapter 11 in Delaware listing $10 to $50 billion in liabilities, and on 26 June the parent filed for protection with the Tokyo District Court, with total liabilities reported at about ¥1.7 trillion ($15 billion). Chairman and CEO Shigehisa Takada, grandson of the founder, bowed to the cameras, said he would resign once the restructuring was set, and acknowledged the family's control would end. The shares, down 95% since January 2014, were delisted on 27 July 2017. Key Safety Systems, a Michigan supplier owned by China's Ningbo Joyson Electronic, agreed to buy substantially all of Takata's global assets, including seatbelts, non-PSAN airbags, steering wheels and child seats, for $1.588 billion, funded by Joyson, PAG and SDIC. The deal closed on 11 April 2018 and the combined business was renamed Joyson Safety Systems, with more than 50,000 employees in 25 countries. The PSAN inflator business and the recall liabilities stayed behind in a rump Takata entity that exists only to produce replacement inflators and wind down claims.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **The propellant bet (late 1990s, Takata leadership over engineering objections).** Choosing ammonium nitrate was a deliberate cost-leadership strategy that let Takata undercut Autoliv and TRW by up to 30% per module and win share at GM, Honda and others. The company's own engineer Mark Lillie and its main competitor's chemists had both said the compound was unsafe for the application. The decision-makers accepted a known chemical-stability risk in exchange for price advantage, and the risk did not show up for a decade because degradation takes years of humidity exposure.

- **Choosing concealment over disclosure (from about 2000).** When early testing showed ruptures, the DOJ found that Takata falsified data rather than notify customers. This converted an engineering mistake into fraud. It is the single choice that turned a survivable recall into corporate death: a supplier that had disclosed in 2004 would have faced a large but bounded recall of a few model years; by the time ruptures were public the defective design was in tens of millions of vehicles across more than a dozen automakers.

- **Refusing to name a root cause (2014 to May 2015).** Takata resisted NHTSA's demand for a national recall and for years maintained it could not identify the defect, leaving automakers to lead regional recalls. Only in May 2015 did it admit the inflators were defective, which is what unlocked the consent order and, later, the criminal case. The delay expanded the recall population and the eventual liability.

- **Honda's exit (November 2015).** Takata's largest customer and shareholder dropping PSAN inflators removed the revenue that might have funded a settlement, and signalled to other automakers that they could do the same.

- **Structured sale rather than rescue (2017 to 2018).** The Chapter 11 and Tokyo filings were designed around a pre-agreed buyer. Selling the viable seatbelt and non-PSAN airbag businesses to KSS while ring-fencing PSAN and recall liabilities preserved 50,000 jobs and continuity of supply to automakers, but wiped out the founding family's equity and left victims and automakers as unsecured creditors of a shell.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Takata is the canonical case of a **cost-driven materials or architecture choice with a latent, slow-acting failure mode**. The pattern recurs wherever a cheaper input wins share before its long-tail behaviour is observable: the advantage compounds for years, the installed base grows, and by the time the defect surfaces the liability is proportional to everything shipped, not to current revenue. Founders and operators recognise the shape in cheaper battery chemistries, lower-grade cloud security defaults, or a pricing model that is only unprofitable after churn cohorts mature.

The second lesson is about **disclosure economics**. Takata's engineering error was survivable; its data manipulation was not. A supplier's entire business is trust with a small number of OEM customers. Once the DOJ established fraud, every automaker had both the legal grounds and the commercial motive to pull volume and press claims, and the company's cash flow disappeared at the same moment its liabilities crystallised. The order of events matters: the criminal plea in February 2017 preceded the bankruptcy in June by four months.

Third, it illustrates **how a distressed asset sale is structured** when good businesses are trapped inside a toxic liability: pre-negotiate the buyer, split the assets from the claims, and use the bankruptcy court to make the split stick. Joyson Safety Systems is today a going concern; Takata's victims were left with a $125 million fund.
