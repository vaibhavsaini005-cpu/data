## One-liner

Japan's brashest internet conglomerate was delisted from the Tokyo Stock Exchange and its celebrity founder jailed after a landmark accounting-fraud scandal wiped out 90% of its share price in four months.

## What happened

Livedoor, led by flamboyant founder Takafumi Horie ("Horiemon"), grew through the early-2000s into one of Japan's most aggressive internet conglomerates — a portal business bolted onto a fast-moving string of acquisitions funded by serial stock splits. On January 16, 2006, Tokyo District Public Prosecutors raided Livedoor's Roppongi Hills headquarters, Horie's home, and the homes of other executives on suspicion of securities and accounting fraud. Horie was arrested on January 23, 2006.

Investigators found Livedoor had inflated reported profits by roughly ¥5.35 billion ($45 million) for the fiscal year ending September 2004, partly by disguising a subsidiary's acquisition of a company it already controlled as revenue, and by using its own inflated stock as acquisition currency in circular deals with entities it effectively controlled. The raid triggered panic selling so heavy the Tokyo Stock Exchange's systems nearly overloaded, forcing an unprecedented full trading halt. Livedoor shares lost about 90% of their value over the following four months, and the company was delisted from the Tokyo Stock Exchange on April 14, 2006. Horie was convicted of securities law violations and sentenced to 2.5 years in prison on March 16, 2007.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Stock-split-funded acquisition spree**: Livedoor used aggressive, repeated stock splits to inflate share liquidity and fund a rapid string of acquisitions, a structure that only worked as long as the share price kept climbing.
- **Circular accounting to mask losses**: Rather than report a real loss, executives used investment-partnership structures and self-dealing acquisitions to keep reported profits positive.
- **Prosecutorial raid as market event**: The January 2006 raid itself — not a later court verdict — was what destroyed Livedoor's value, since investor panic collapsed the stock before any fraud was formally proven.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Livedoor is Japan's clearest 2000s case study in growth-by-financial-engineering: a real, large, revenue-generating internet business whose acquisition machine depended on an ever-inflating stock price rather than underlying cash flow. Once regulators intervened, the entire structure unwound in days, not quarters — a reminder that businesses built on circular stock-based M&A are exposed to a single point of failure (loss of market confidence) that ordinary operating businesses aren't. The scandal also reshaped Japanese securities enforcement, prompting tighter disclosure rules for internet and media conglomerates in subsequent years.
