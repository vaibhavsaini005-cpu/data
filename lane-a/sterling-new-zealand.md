## One-liner

Wellington AI startup Sterling, co-founded by CEO Nik Wakelin and Ludwig Wendzich just ten months earlier, raised a $3.8 million seed round led by Blackbird to expand its "autopilot" AI accounting product from New Zealand into Australia and the United States.

## What happened

Nik Wakelin and Ludwig Wendzich founded Sterling in 2025 to let finance and accounting teams deploy AI workflows that run continuously in the background, integrating with existing tools and logging every action taken, rather than requiring staff to prompt a chatbot for each task. Wakelin described the product's specific design goal as full automation of routine work rather than assistance: "It sits in your finance team, in your inbox and all your systems, picks up things as they arrive and works out where everything goes and files it for you," adding that "by the time your finance team turns up in the morning and has their coffee, everything's done and prepped for them, ready to review and tick off."

The company raised a $3.8 million seed round announced around August 1, 2026, led by trans-Tasman venture firm Blackbird (taking an 18% stake), with additional backing from Rowan Simpson (10%) and Pushpay co-founder Eliot Crowther (5%), alongside other alumni from Xero, Vend, Pushpay and Trade Me. The capital was earmarked specifically to expand Sterling beyond New Zealand into Australia and the United States.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Building for full task completion ("autopilot") rather than a conversational AI assistant**: Wakelin's framing explicitly rejects the chatbot model in favor of software that finishes tasks unprompted and logs its own actions for review.
- **Raising a large seed round (by NZ standards) just 10 months after founding**, backed heavily by operator-investors from prior successful NZ software companies (Xero, Vend, Pushpay, Trade Me) rather than only institutional VCs.
- **Committing the seed capital explicitly to international expansion (Australia, US) rather than NZ-market deepening**: Wakelin and Wendzich treated the domestic market as a proving ground, not the primary target market, from very early on.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Sterling shows a fast-moving pattern increasingly common for AI-native NZ startups: reach a credible product within under a year, then raise a seed round explicitly staffed and priced for immediate international expansion rather than treating international growth as a later-stage milestone. Assembling investors who were themselves operators at NZ's prior software success stories (Xero, Vend, Pushpay, Trade Me) also gives a young company access to institutional memory about scaling a NZ-founded software company internationally, which is a distinct advantage over purely financial seed investors.
