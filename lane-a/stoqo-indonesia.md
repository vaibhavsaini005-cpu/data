## One-liner

Stoqo, a Jakarta-based B2B platform that supplied fresh ingredients to small restaurants and food stalls, shut down in April 2020 after COVID-19 restaurant closures wiped out its customer base almost overnight.

## What happened

Stoqo Teknologi Indonesia was founded in 2017 by Aswin Andrison, a former McKinsey & Co. associate, and Angky William, a former Amazon software developer. The company built an online ordering and logistics platform that let small food outlets across Greater Jakarta — warungs and independent restaurants — buy fresh ingredients (everything from chili and eggs to coffee powder) from a single supplier instead of sourcing piecemeal from wet markets, promising more consistent pricing and delivery.

The model had real traction: CEO Andrison said the business grew sevenfold in 2019, reaching tens of thousands of food-outlet customers and around 250 employees, backed by investors including Accel Partners, Alpha JWC Ventures, Monk's Hill Ventures, and Insignia Ventures Partners. That growth reversed within weeks once Indonesia imposed large-scale social restrictions in response to COVID-19 in early 2020: restaurants and food stalls, Stoqo's entire customer base, closed or cut ordering drastically, and the company ceased operations in late April 2020.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Betting entirely on offline food-outlet demand**: Stoqo's supply chain was built around physical restaurants and stalls staying open and ordering regularly — a structurally sound bet in normal conditions that left zero buffer when that demand category was the first to be shut down by pandemic restrictions.
- **No pivot window**: unlike other Southeast Asian startups that had months to redirect operations toward delivery or e-commerce as COVID hit, Stoqo's B2B logistics model had no adjacent consumer-facing product to shift into quickly, and it wound down within roughly six weeks of the first serious lockdown measures.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Stoqo is a clean case of a company with genuine product-market fit (sevenfold growth, blue-chip investors) failing not from a flawed business model but from a single-customer-segment concentration risk that a macro shock (pandemic restrictions on restaurants) turned fatal overnight. It's a reminder that "the model was working" and "the company survives" are separate questions when a startup's entire revenue base sits on one narrow, externally-fragile category of customer.
