## One-liner

Liquid (formerly Quoine), Japan's first FSA-licensed cryptocurrency exchange, survived a $90 million hack only to be acquired by FTX and then wiped out when FTX collapsed in November 2022 — a rare double catastrophe that left customers locked out for months.

## What happened

Mike Kayamori and Mario Gomez Lozada co-founded Quoine in Tokyo in 2014. Quoine's early advantage was regulatory: it became one of the first 30 exchanges registered with Japan's Financial Services Agency (FSA) after the agency began licensing crypto exchanges following the 2014 Mt. Gox collapse. This regulatory legitimacy made Quoine trusted by Japanese retail and institutional clients at a time when most global competitors operated in a legal grey zone.

The company rebranded to Liquid Group around 2019–2020, operating Liquid Japan (FSA-licensed) and Liquid Global (international) as parallel entities. Liquid raised significant venture funding and expanded to Southeast Asian markets, positioning itself as a premium, compliant global exchange.

The first catastrophe struck on August 19, 2021: hackers compromised Liquid's warm wallets and drained approximately $97 million worth of cryptocurrency (ETH, BTC, TRX, and XRP). It was one of the largest exchange hacks of that year. FTX, then at the peak of its influence, provided an emergency $120 million loan to Liquid within days — effectively becoming Liquid's financial backstop.

The relationship deepened: in February 2022, FTX announced it would acquire Liquid Group outright. The deal closed on April 4, 2022. The acquisition was framed as FTX's accelerated path to FSA licensing in Japan. Liquid Japan was rebranded FTX Japan.

Then, on November 11, 2022, FTX filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Liquid Global immediately halted all fiat and cryptocurrency withdrawals. FTX Japan (the former Liquid Japan entity, which held FSA-licensed customer assets separately) eventually resumed withdrawals under regulatory supervision and was sold to BB Technologies (a SoftBank subsidiary) in 2023. However, Liquid's international operations effectively ceased to exist, and the Liquid brand was extinguished.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Early FSA registration (2017):** Regulatory compliance gave Liquid a genuine moat at a time when competitors ignored licensing; it also made Liquid an acquisition target precisely for its licence.
- **Accepting FTX's rescue loan (August 2021):** The $120 million loan from FTX saved Liquid after the hack but created a dependency relationship that made the subsequent acquisition almost inevitable.
- **Selling to FTX (April 2022):** At the time the deal looked rational — FTX was the world's second-largest exchange and the acquisition brought financial stability. In hindsight, Liquid traded a recoverable position for one that was existentially catastrophic.
- **Japan's segregated-asset rule:** Because Liquid Japan (FTX Japan) held customer assets separately per FSA requirements, Japanese customers ultimately recovered their funds. International Liquid customers did not fare as well.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Liquid's collapse illustrates the contagion risk of "acqui-rescue" in crypto: a distressed-but-viable company accepting a rescue from a financially opaque acquirer can end up far worse off than it started. The hack in August 2021 was survivable; the decision to become structurally dependent on FTX was not. Japan's FSA-mandated asset segregation, often criticised as burdensome compliance overhead, turned out to be the mechanism that protected Japanese customers — a rare case where stricter regulation clearly produced a better outcome for retail investors than looser international equivalents.
