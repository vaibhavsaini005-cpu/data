## One-liner

Kiwi-Coin, New Zealand's longest-running Bitcoin exchange, traded for eleven years and still had to shut down on January 1, 2026 — not because of a hack, a fraud, or a loss of customers, but because the country's banks had spent years refusing to keep a compliant crypto business banked at all.

## What happened

Kiwi-Coin launched in 2014 as a straightforward bitcoin-to-NZD exchange, run by director Lev Sidorenko. It survived long enough to become New Zealand's longest-serving cryptocurrency exchange, outlasting Cryptopia (which collapsed after a hack and liquidity crisis), BitPrime, and Dasset — each of which failed for its own distinct reason.

Kiwi-Coin's ending was different, and Sidorenko was explicit about the cause in an email to customers on October 17, 2025: "Since 2020, the Virtual Asset Service Provider industry in New Zealand has faced significant barriers to doing business due to the inability to obtain, or maintain, access to banking services. Unfortunately, Kiwi-Coin has been caught in the middle of what is widely known as 'de-risking' by the New Zealand registered banks." De-risking is the practice of banks closing or refusing accounts for an entire category of business — not because any specific customer broke a rule, but because the category itself is judged not worth the compliance cost and reputational risk of serving.

The company announced trading would halt on December 15, 2025, with full closure on January 1, 2026. Kiwi-Coin was not an isolated case: BitNZ closed in 2017, Vimba in 2020, and NZBCX in 2021, all citing the same banking access problem rather than product failure, fraud, or lack of customer demand.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Operating a fully compliant business inside a category banks had decided to exit wholesale.** Kiwi-Coin's failure mode was not a mistake it made — it was a sector-level decision made by banks that no individual compliant operator could out-execute.
- **Surviving eleven years through the first wave of de-risking (2017-2021) that killed three competitors.** Kiwi-Coin outlasted BitNZ, Vimba and NZBCX by continuing to find banking relationships after each round of closures — buying time rather than solving the underlying problem.
- **Choosing an orderly, announced wind-down (October notice, December halt, January closure) rather than an abrupt shutdown.** Sidorenko gave customers roughly ten weeks of notice, converting a forced failure into a managed one.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Kiwi-Coin is the cleanest available example of a business killed by an input it could not purchase at any price: banking access. No amount of product quality, compliance spend, or customer trust could substitute for a bank willing to hold the company's account, and once every New Zealand bank concluded the same thing, there was no commercial tactic left to try. For founders in any category subject to de-risking — crypto, cannabis-adjacent, remittance, certain gambling-adjacent businesses — the operative lesson is that banking relationships are not a vendor contract like any other; they are a single point of failure that regulatory sentiment, not customer behavior, controls. A business plan that depends on one bank's continued willingness to serve the category is not fully de-risked until it has an alternative banking path already proven, not merely assumed.

The second lesson is about how an entire wave of failures can look like one company's story when it is actually the same cause recurring. BitNZ, Vimba, NZBCX and now Kiwi-Coin did not fail independently — they are four data points on the same underlying trend line. Reading a single shutdown in isolation would misdiagnose it as company-specific; reading the sequence makes clear that this was a structural, sector-wide problem that eventually killed every participant regardless of individual execution quality.
