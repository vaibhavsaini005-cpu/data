## One-liner

A Philippine mobile-wallet and crypto-exchange startup serving the country's largely unbanked population became Indonesian ride-hailing giant Go-Jek's entry vehicle into the Philippines, selling a majority stake for a reported $72M in January 2019.

## What happened

Coins.ph built a mobile wallet and blockchain-based remittance and bill-payment platform aimed at the Philippines' large unbanked and underbanked population, layering in cryptocurrency buy/sell functionality as one of the country's earliest licensed crypto exchanges. By the time of its exit, the platform had built up roughly five million registered users transacting cash-in/cash-out, remittances, bill payments and crypto trades through a network of physical partner outlets as well as its app.

On January 18, 2019, TechCrunch reported that Indonesian ride-hailing and payments company Go-Jek had acquired a majority stake in Coins.ph, with industry sources putting the price at $72M — a figure neither company officially confirmed. Go-Jek CEO Nadiem Makarim framed the deal explicitly around market entry: with no ride-hailing or payments footprint of its own in the Philippines, buying an established, licensed fintech platform let Go-Jek skip years of regulatory and trust-building work in a new market. The strategic plan, per Go-Jek's own statements, was to integrate Coins.ph with its Indonesian GoPay wallet product and use the combined base to expand into lending and insurance products across the Philippines.

Unlike many regional fintech exits driven by distress, Coins.ph's sale was a growth-stage acquisition: the company kept operating (and later relaunching services) under its own brand with Go-Jek's backing, rather than being wound down or folded invisibly into the parent.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Building trust and licensing before scale**: Coins.ph's early investment in becoming a licensed, compliant crypto and remittance operator in a heavily regulated market is what made it a credible acquisition target rather than a risky one for a foreign acquirer.
- **Positioning as market-entry infrastructure, not just a product**: Coins.ph's five million users and existing regulatory licenses were worth more to Go-Jek as a shortcut into a new country than as a standalone growth story — a different kind of "success" than an IPO or independent scale-up.
- **Acquirer's regional-superapp strategy**: the deal only made sense in the context of Go-Jek's broader Southeast Asian expansion — a reminder that a startup's most valuable exit path can be as an entry vehicle for a larger regional platform, not a direct competitor.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Coins.ph shows a viable playbook for fintech startups in emerging Southeast Asian markets: build deep regulatory and trust infrastructure serving an underbanked population, and the resulting license + user base becomes acquisition bait for regional platforms that need fast, de-risked market entry more than they need to build from scratch. It's a useful counter-example to the more common failure/pivot stories in this dataset — proof that "sell majority control to a regional super-app" can be a clean, well-documented success outcome rather than a face-saving euphemism for distress.
