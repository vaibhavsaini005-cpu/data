## One-liner

AIDIN Robotics, founded in 2019 by a Sungkyunkwan University mechanical engineering professor and his former student, built six-axis force and torque sensors — the components that let a robot feel what it is touching — and spent seven years climbing a ladder of increasingly strategic investors, ending with 16 billion won from HD Hyundai Robotics and Samsung Venture Investment in September 2026 to put its sensors into shipyard robots and humanoids.

## What happened

Choi Hyouk-ryeol, a professor of mechanical engineering at Sungkyunkwan University, co-founded AIDIN Robotics in 2019 with his former student Lee Yoon-haeng, who became CEO. The company picked a deliberately narrow position in the robotics stack: it designs and manufactures its own sensor solutions that precisely detect touch and force, rather than building robots, arms, or software platforms. Its core product is a six-axis force and torque sensor — the part that tells a robot how hard it is pressing and in which direction, which is what separates a machine that can polish a curved steel surface from one that can only move to coordinates.

AIDIN built a real export business around that component, shipping six-axis force and torque sensors to ten countries including Germany, France, Spain, China and Japan. Being a component supplier to foreign robot makers, rather than a domestic robot brand, gave the company revenue and design-win validation that was independent of Korea's own robotics market.

The funding history shows a clear escalation in the *type* of investor rather than just the amount. A 4.5 billion won Series A in 2022 came from conventional Korean venture firms — DSC Investment, BNK Venture Capital, Kolon Investment and Future Play. The 15 billion won Series B in 2024 shifted decisively toward strategics and corporates: Korea Investment Partners, POSCO Technology Investment, CJ Logistics, Samsung Next and GS Ventures. Each of those later names was a potential customer or deployment partner, not only a source of capital.

That pattern paid off directly. A humanoid robot co-developed by AIDIN Robotics and CJ Logistics — its Series B investor — demonstrated packaging capability and was selected for South Korea's state-backed logistics humanoid project in 2025, converting an investor relationship into a government-validated deployment.

In September 2026 the ladder reached its logical top. HD Hyundai Robotics, part of the country's largest shipbuilding group, invested 13 billion won for an undisclosed equity stake, with Samsung Venture Investment putting in a further 3 billion won announced the same day. The stated plan is specific rather than exploratory: combine AIDIN's force and tactile sensors and control technology with HD Hyundai's robot platforms to build a five-fingered robotic hand for shipbuilding and heavy industry, plus surface-treatment automation for polishing and grinding — starting in HD Hyundai's own shipyards before expanding to global markets including U.S. shipyards.

The round landed inside a broader Korean surge in "physical AI." The same month, robotics firms dominated the Korean IPO market, with Bigwave Robotics drawing nearly 5 trillion won in retail subscription deposits and Hyundai Motor Group committing roughly 8 trillion won ($5.4 billion) to an AI and software research complex outside Seoul.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Choosing a component, not a robot.** AIDIN sold the sense of touch to everyone building robots instead of competing with them. That made every robot maker a potential customer rather than a rival, and it is why a shipbuilding group, a logistics group and a steel group could all invest without conflict.
- **Exporting the sensor to ten countries before scaling domestically.** International design wins gave the company external proof of quality, which is far harder for a strategic investor to discount than domestic revenue arranged through local relationships.
- **Taking Series B money from prospective customers (CJ Logistics, POSCO, GS, Samsung Next) rather than maximising valuation from financial investors.** The CJ Logistics relationship produced the co-developed logistics humanoid that won state backing in 2025 — a capability milestone that a purely financial round would not have generated.
- **Letting the 2026 strategic round be scoped to a named application in the investor's own facilities.** The HD Hyundai deal is not a generic partnership; it starts with polishing and grinding in HD Hyundai's shipyards, which means the first deployment has a committed internal buyer.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

AIDIN is a textbook case of using the capital stack as a distribution strategy. The amounts matter less than the sequence: financial VCs first to build the product, then corporate strategics who are themselves the end market, then the single largest industrial player in the country to anchor deployment. By 2026 the company's investor list and its customer list had substantially merged — which is the strongest possible position for a component supplier, because it removes the usual hard problem of selling an unproven part into a conservative industrial buyer.

The deeper lesson is about picking the narrow layer. Robotics is crowded with companies trying to build whole robots, where they must out-execute Hyundai, Samsung and every Chinese manufacturer at once. AIDIN instead identified a specific, hard, physically-grounded component — force and tactile sensing — that nearly every serious robot needs and few can make well, and became the default supplier for it. A professor-and-student founding team is well suited to exactly this shape of company: deep narrow technical advantage, long time horizon, no need to win a consumer market.

For founders, the transferable test is whether your investors could plausibly become your customers. If they can, structure the rounds so they do.
