## One-liner

Auckland-based Tradify built a job-management SaaS platform for trade contractors (electricians, plumbers, builders, painters) that quietly grew from a New Zealand product into a profitable international business serving 25,000+ tradespeople across New Zealand, Australia, the UK, and Ireland — without a splashy fundraise or acqui-hire exit.

## What happened

Tradify was founded in 2012 in Auckland by Jacob Carter, an entrepreneur who observed that small trade businesses — electricians, plumbers, painters, HVAC technicians — were managing their jobs through a combination of paper notebooks, WhatsApp messages, and spreadsheets. The core insight was that tradespeople are skilled with their hands but time-poor and admin-averse; a mobile-first app that handled quoting, scheduling, time tracking, and invoicing in a few taps could meaningfully improve their margins.

The product launched and gained traction slowly but organically in New Zealand, where the construction and trades sector is substantial relative to the country's 5 million population. Carter bootstrapped the business through early years, building product based on tight feedback loops with actual tradespeople, then raised NZ$2M from Movac — New Zealand's largest institutional venture capital firm — in 2018. A follow-on Movac investment of NZ$6M came in 2021 to fund international expansion, primarily into Australia and the UK.

By the early 2020s Tradify had grown to over 25,000 active users. The product handled end-to-end job management: customer management, quoting, supplier purchase orders, time sheets, and Xero/QuickBooks integration for accounting sync. Unlike many SaaS companies that sprint toward growth at the expense of unit economics, Tradify operated with a strong focus on customer retention and word-of-mouth within trades communities — referral is a dominant acquisition channel in a sector where contractors talk to each other on job sites. The company remained private and did not pursue an IPO or large strategic exit through the reporting period.

## Key decisions / turning points

- Choosing to go deep on a single vertical (trades) rather than broadening to all field service businesses kept the product tightly matched to a specific customer's workflow and vocabulary — "your apprentice is assigned to the Henderson job" is more compelling than a generic scheduling UI.
- New Zealand's small but high-density professional trade community was an ideal proving ground: enough customers to reach break-even, but tight enough that reputation and referral drove acquisition at near-zero cost.
- Mobile-first design was non-negotiable; tradespeople cannot spend time at a desktop, and a product that worked well on-site on an iPhone was the product, not a nice-to-have.
- International expansion followed the English-language adjacency sequence — NZ → AU → UK → Ireland — which minimized localization work while accessing much larger addressable markets.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Tradify's story is a counterpoint to the venture-funded "grow at all costs" narrative. The company grew to a meaningful international SaaS business on disciplined, product-led growth from a country of 5 million — without a large Series B, an acquisition, or a flashy pivot. For New Zealand's startup ecosystem, it represents the "boring but durable" archetype: a founder who understood a specific customer's pain better than any outsider could, built exactly the right tool for it, and scaled internationally by following linguistic and regulatory familiarity rather than chasing the US market prematurely. Tradify also illustrates the resilience of vertical SaaS in recession-resistant industries — tradespeople need to quote and invoice regardless of macro conditions, making churn far lower than consumer or broad-market SaaS.
