## One-liner

Founder and president Kazutaka Yonekura took AI firm Alt public on the Tokyo Stock Exchange in October 2024 on the strength of sales later found to be up to 90% fictitious; he resigned in July 2025, and the company filed for bankruptcy protection days after being delisted in October 2025.

## What happened

Alt, a Tokyo AI developer founded a decade earlier, built its pitch around "P.A.I." (Personal AI) digital human clones and a subscription product called AI Gijiroku that auto-transcribes and summarizes meetings. Founder Kazutaka Yonekura took the company public on the Tokyo Stock Exchange's Growth section in October 2024 at a roughly $130 million valuation. The company had been running a circular-transaction scheme: it paid advertising agencies promotion money for AI Gijiroku, then booked that same money flowing back as fictitious sales revenue. Regulators later found sales submitted to the Kanto Local Finance Bureau for January 2022–June 2024 were overstated by about ¥8.4 billion, with total fictitious sales reaching roughly ¥11 billion — up to 90% of reported sales in some years — meaning the IPO itself was approved on the back of numbers that shouldn't have qualified the company for listing. Alt acknowledged a "potential overstatement of revenues" in June 2025; Yonekura resigned as president that same month. The Tokyo Stock Exchange delisted the company in August 2025, and on 6 October 2025 Alt filed for bankruptcy protection (civil rehabilitation) with the Tokyo District Court. Two former executives were later convicted over the scheme and given suspended sentences, with the company itself fined ¥300 million.

## Key decisions / turning points

- October 2024: Yonekura takes Alt public on inflated sales figures, converting a private accounting fraud into a public-market one.
- June 2025: facing scrutiny, the company admits to a "potential overstatement of revenues," and Yonekura resigns as president rather than continue leading the company through the fallout.
- August–October 2025: delisting from the Tokyo Stock Exchange is followed within weeks by a formal bankruptcy filing, closing out the company as an operating, listed business.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

This is a founder who used a specific, engineered scheme — round-tripping ad spend back into the top line — to get a company over the bar for a stock listing, and the decision that ended the company wasn't the fraud itself but the sequence of choices around getting caught: an admission, a resignation, then the company running out of room once public confidence (and stock-exchange listing status) evaporated. It's a sharp illustration of how founder-level accounting decisions, not just product or market failure, can be the entire cause of a company's collapse.
