## One-liner

Mathpresso, the Seoul startup behind the QANDA app that let students photograph a math problem and get an AI-matched solution, turned that single free utility into a subscription-and-tutoring platform used by 9.8 million monthly users across 50+ countries, then used a 2023 telecom partnership on education-specific AI to launch a US-market product, Prep.Pie, in 2024.

## What happened

Mathpresso, Inc. was founded in Seoul in June 2015 by Jake Yongjae Lee and Ray Jongheun Lee, and launched its first and namesake product, QANDA, in January 2016: an app that let a student photograph a math problem and receive an AI-assisted match against a database of worked solutions, aimed at a real, unglamorous pain point — homework help in a country with intense academic pressure but uneven access to tutoring. The core scan-a-problem feature stayed free and became the company's user-acquisition engine, reaching a scale (9.8 million monthly active users, over 80% of them outside Korea, per the company's July 2021 funding announcement) that a pure homework-help utility rarely reaches.

Free scale alone doesn't build a venture-scale business, and Mathpresso's turning point was converting that usage into paid products layered on top of the free core. In March 2021 it launched a premium subscription tier built around short "micro video lectures," and by July 2021 — when it raised a $50M Series C led by Yellowdog, GGV Capital, Goodwater Capital, KDB and SKS Private Equity (bringing total funding to $105M) — the company was explicitly describing itself as building "a multi-faceted education platform with personalized content," expanding into Indonesia and Thailand, and even moving into offline and book publishing. The company kept layering AI further into the core product: in September 2023 it took an $8M (10 billion KRW) strategic investment from Korean telecom operator KT specifically to co-develop an "education-specific Large Language Model" combining Mathpresso's proprietary AI and data with KT's infrastructure, aimed at improving the AI tutor built into QANDA App, QANDA Study and QANDA Tutor while addressing the accuracy problems of general-purpose AI models in an education context. That AI investment fed directly into Mathpresso's next strategic move: in 2024 it launched Prep.Pie, a new platform built to compete directly in the US edtech market against larger incumbents, a market entry the company treated as a deliberate escalation rather than a side experiment — and in May 2024 Mathpresso/QANDA was named to TIME's list of the World's Top EdTech Companies.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Keeping the core "scan a problem, get a solution" feature free (from 2016).** This is what let QANDA reach tens of millions of users across 50+ countries organically — Mathpresso treated the free utility as a distribution engine rather than trying to monetize it directly, deferring revenue to later layers.
- **Launching the Premium micro-video-lecture subscription in March 2021.** This was the first real monetization layer on top of years of free usage, timed deliberately just months before the July 2021 Series C — a sign the subscription launch was built to prove a revenue model to investors, not just to users.
- **Taking KT's strategic (not purely financial) investment in September 2023.** Choosing a telecom infrastructure partner over another pure VC round gave Mathpresso compute/infrastructure backing specifically for LLM development at a moment when general-purpose AI models were becoming commoditized — a bet that domain-specific accuracy in education would be a defensible moat competitors couldn't easily copy by bolting on ChatGPT.
- **Entering the US market with a new product (Prep.Pie) rather than exporting QANDA directly (2024).** Building a separate US-facing product signaled Mathpresso judged the US market needed a different product, not just a localized version of the Korean app — a harder, higher-conviction bet than simple geographic expansion.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Mathpresso's trajectory is a pattern recognizable to any founder building on top of a single free utility feature: the free feature (photograph-a-problem) was necessary to reach scale, but the business only became a venture-fundable, multi-hundred-million-dollar company once the company built and timed paid layers — subscriptions, then an AI-tutor upgrade backed by a strategic infrastructure partner, then a dedicated new product for its hardest new market — on top of that free base, rather than trying to monetize the free feature directly. It's also a specific, timely lesson for AI-era edtech: partnering with an infrastructure player (KT) for domain-specific model development, instead of purely renting a general-purpose AI API, was a deliberate move to build a moat right as generic AI tutoring tools became easy for any competitor to bolt on.
