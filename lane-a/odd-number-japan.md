## One-liner

Tokyo's Odd Number built and ran *Link! Like! Love Live!*, an official Love Live! franchise idol app that attracted what analysts called a "considerable" user base over three years — and still filed for bankruptcy with roughly ¥10.3–10.7 billion in liabilities, because the cost of operating a live-service anime game outran the revenue a devoted audience could produce.

## What happened

Odd Number launched *Link! Like! Love Live!* in April 2023 as an interactive-animation idol app tied to *Love Live! Hasu no Sora Jogakuin School Idol Club*, a spinoff of one of Japan's most durable media franchises. The company was closely held and the app was its headline product.

The audience was not the problem. Tokyo Shoko Research, the credit-research firm that tracks Japanese corporate failures, found that Odd Number had attracted a "considerable" number of users but that heavy development costs still prevented the company from reaching profitability. The published financials show the loss accumulating on a steady curve rather than arriving as a shock: by December 2023 the company carried a loss of about ¥5.6 billion against debt of roughly ¥6.8 billion, and by December 2024 liabilities had grown to approximately ¥10.32 billion (about $65 million).

*Link! Like! Love Live!* ended service on June 30, 2026, a little over three years after launch. Odd Number filed for bankruptcy at the Tokyo District Court on August 5, 2026, with Iinuma & Partners serving as trustee; Tokyo Shoko Research, in a report dated August 13, put liabilities at ¥10.7 billion (about $67.2 million). The reported figures differ slightly by source and date — ¥10.32 billion is the December 2024 book position, ¥10.7 billion the bankruptcy-stage estimate — but either placed Odd Number among Japan's largest bankruptcies of the year by money owed.

The failure was not idiosyncratic. Ten Japanese smartphone-game operators went under between January and July 2026, part of a broader wave of corporate failures that reached a 12-year high, with bankruptcies up 7% year on year. Industry coverage attributed the mobile-game cluster specifically to rising development costs, creator shortages, the expense of running live-service operations, and competition from higher-budget Chinese and South Korean titles.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Choosing a live-service model for a premium-animation product.** Interactive animation is expensive to produce per unit of content, and live-service demands that content ship continuously. The two choices multiply rather than add: the company committed to an open-ended content treadmill at the highest possible cost per step.
- **Running three-plus years with losses compounding visibly year over year.** The ¥5.6 billion loss at end-2023 was already large relative to any plausible recovery; continuing to operate through 2024–2026 roughly doubled liabilities rather than reaching break-even. There is no evidence of a pivot, a cost restructuring, or a model change in that window.
- **Betting on franchise strength as a substitute for unit economics.** Attaching to Love Live! reliably delivered users — and that validation may have been precisely what made the loss curve survivable for as long as it was. Strong engagement masked the fact that the per-user revenue never covered the per-content cost.
- **Shutting the game down two months before filing.** The June 30 service end followed by an August 5 filing suggests the shutdown was the last cost-reduction lever rather than a strategic wind-down — closing the revenue line did not save the company.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Odd Number is the clearest kind of counterexample to "build something people love." People did love it; a considerable user base stayed for three years, and the company still lost roughly ¥10 billion. The determining variable was never demand but the cost structure underneath it — a content pipeline expensive enough that no realistic engagement level could amortise it.

The specific trap is that franchise IP and live-service delivery each individually look like de-risking moves. Franchise attachment guarantees an audience on day one; live service converts a one-off purchase into recurring revenue. Taken together on a premium-animation cost base, they instead guarantee that the company must keep spending at peak rate forever to hold an audience it acquired cheaply. The healthy signals — strong installs, loyal players — are exactly the ones that justify continuing, which is why losses in this model tend to compound for years rather than force an early correction.

For founders, the operative test is whether the thing you are scaling gets cheaper per unit as it grows. Odd Number's did not, and three years of genuine audience love bought nothing but a larger number at the end. The surrounding cluster of ten Japanese mobile-game failures in seven months suggests this is a structural condition of the category in 2026, not one team's misjudgement.
