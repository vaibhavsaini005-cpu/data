## One-liner

Delio, South Korea's first licensed virtual-asset deposit platform, was declared bankrupt by a Seoul court in November 2024, leaving roughly 2,800 customers unable to recover $1.75 billion after its FTX exposure triggered a withdrawal freeze.

## What happened

Founded in 2018, Delio built a crypto lending and deposit business that let retail customers earn yield on virtual assets — deposit crypto, Delio would lend or stake it, customers collected interest. The company became the first Korean crypto firm to receive Virtual Asset Service Provider (VASP) status from the Financial Intelligence Unit in 2022, a regulatory stamp that reinforced customer trust.

That trust was misplaced: a large share of customer-deposited assets had been routed into accounts at FTX. When FTX collapsed in November 2022, Delio's ability to make customers whole quietly eroded. It took roughly seven months for the strain to surface publicly — Delio halted all customer withdrawals on June 13, 2023. Its sister company Haru Invest, which shared exposure and management ties, froze withdrawals around the same time and was itself declared bankrupt on November 20, 2024, two days before Delio's own bankruptcy declaration on November 22, 2024. A Seoul court found Delio owed 245 billion won (~$1.75 billion) to about 2,800 customers, with claims accepted through February 21, 2025 and a first creditors' meeting set for March 19, 2025. Delio's CEO was subsequently convicted and sentenced to 15 years in prison over a separate $49 million fraud tied to the platform's operations.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Concentrating customer deposits in FTX**: Routing a large share of custodied assets into a single counterparty (FTX) turned a third-party exchange collapse into an existential balance-sheet hole for Delio.
- **Seven-month gap between exposure and disclosure**: FTX failed in November 2022; Delio didn't freeze withdrawals until June 2023, delaying the moment customers could act on the risk.
- **Regulatory licensing as false reassurance**: VASP status signaled legitimacy to depositors but did not require the reserve transparency that would have surfaced the FTX concentration risk earlier.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Delio is a textbook case of contagion risk in crypto lending: a platform's own bankruptcy was ultimately decided not by its product or customer demand but by where it parked customer assets. It also shows that licensing (VASP status) is not the same as solvency assurance — regulatory approval covered process compliance, not counterparty concentration risk. The multi-month lag between the triggering event (FTX's collapse) and the customer-facing consequence (withdrawal freeze) is a recurring pattern in crypto-lending failures, where firms attempt to absorb losses quietly before the balance sheet forces disclosure.
