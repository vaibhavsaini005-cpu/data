## One-liner

Wantedly, the Tokyo-based referral-recruiting and business-networking platform founded by ex-Facebook Japan lead Akiko Naka, went public on the Tokyo Stock Exchange's Mothers market in September 2017, turning a decade-old bootstrapped-feeling HR startup into a listed company.

## What happened

Founded in 2010 under the name Fuel by Akiko Naka, who had previously run Facebook's Japan operations, Wantedly built a LinkedIn-style social network oriented around "why you work" rather than resumes and salaries, matching job seekers with companies through informal referrals and casual meetings rather than traditional postings. The platform expanded into adjacent tools — Wantedly Visit for interview scheduling, Wantedly People for digital business cards, and Wantedly Chat for team messaging.

By its fiscal year ending August 2016, the company had grown to roughly 800,000 individual users and 23,000 corporate clients, posting 840 million yen (~$7.6M) in revenue with a modest net profit of 77 million yen. Its IPO application to the Tokyo Stock Exchange was approved in August 2017, with Daiwa Securities as lead underwriter; the offering priced on September 6 and the stock listed on the TSE Mothers market on September 14, 2017, offering a combined 80,000 shares including over-allotment.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Positioning against traditional job boards** with a referral- and culture-fit-driven model, differentiating Wantedly from incumbent Japanese recruiting portals.
- **Reaching profitability before the IPO** (a net profit in FY2016) gave the company a credible growth-plus-profit story for Tokyo's Mothers market, which is more tolerant of smaller listings than the main board.
- **Choosing the Mothers market** (Tokyo's growth-company board) over a later main-board listing let Wantedly go public earlier in its growth curve, in Japan's characteristic path for tech IPOs.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Wantedly's IPO is a success story of a Japanese consumer-HR startup reaching a public listing on a differentiated product thesis (culture/referral matching) rather than competing head-on with entrenched recruiting incumbents like Recruit Holdings. It also illustrates the well-worn Japanese growth-startup path: list early and small on Mothers, then scale post-IPO, rather than raising large late-stage private rounds as is more common in the US.
