## One-liner

Raksul started as a price-comparison site for Japan's fragmented printing industry, pivoted to a transaction-based sharing-economy marketplace, IPO'd on the Tokyo Stock Exchange at a $386M valuation in 2018, then kept pivoting into logistics and advertising — turning one sector-specific insight into a repeatable B2B platform playbook.

## What happened

Yasukane Matsumoto founded Raksul in 2009 with a simple observation: Japan's commercial printing industry was hugely fragmented, with thousands of small regional printers running presses at 50–60% utilisation while SME customers paid inflated prices through middlemen. Raksul launched as a price-comparison website, aggregating quotes from those idle printers.

By 2013, Matsumoto recognised that comparison alone wasn't solving the structural inefficiency — printers still had to manage their own sales and scheduling. Raksul pivoted to a marketplace model that directly allocated print jobs to printers' spare press capacity, optimising against idle time rather than just price. This "sharing economy for industrial capacity" framing proved durable: it was essentially Uber for print, dynamically routing work to where spare capacity existed at any given moment.

The printing platform scaled quickly. Raksul applied for its IPO in April 2018 and listed on the TSE Mothers market on May 31, 2018, at a $386M valuation — roughly five times its FY2017 revenue. Post-IPO, the company applied the same playbook to two adjacent sectors: logistics (Hacobell, connecting SMEs with idle truck capacity) and advertising (Novasell, a B2B marketing platform). By FY2025 the marketing segment had reached full-year profitability. As of 2026 Raksul is expanding further into fintech via Raksul Bank.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Price comparison → marketplace (2013)**: abandoning the referral/lead-gen model in favour of owning the transaction was the foundational pivot; it unlocked unit economics and gave Raksul the data flywheel that drove capacity-matching improvements.
- **"Fabless platform" framing**: Raksul owns no printing assets, trucks, or ad inventory — it is entirely a software layer matching demand to third-party excess capacity. This keeps capex near-zero and makes each new sector expansion a code problem rather than an asset problem.
- **IPO on TSE Mothers (2018)**: listing on the growth exchange gave Raksul public-market credibility and currency for partnership deals with established printers and logistics companies who would otherwise resist giving a startup access to their capacity data.
- **Serial adjacent expansion**: entering logistics then advertising used the same capacity-sharing thesis, reducing the learning curve for each new sector entry and proving the model wasn't printing-specific.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Raksul's trajectory is one of Japan's clearest post-2010 examples of a B2B platform that iterated its business model before scaling, rather than scaling before iterating. The comparison-to-marketplace pivot happened while the company was small enough to absorb the change cleanly. The subsequent multi-sector expansion demonstrates that a genuinely sector-agnostic model (match idle industrial capacity to demand) can be more valuable than domain depth alone — as long as the operator resists the temptation to vertically integrate and dilute the platform's supply-side trust.
