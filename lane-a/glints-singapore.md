## One-liner

Singapore talent startup Glints pivoted from campus internship board (2013) to full Southeast-Asia professional hiring marketplace (2019), raising $50M Series D in 2023 and reaching profitability across its core markets.

## What happened

Three National University of Singapore students — Oswald Yeo, Looi Qin En, and Ying Cong — founded Glints in 2013 to connect undergraduates with internships and entry-level jobs. The product was a campus-first job board layered with skills assessments, popular but thin-margin.

By 2017–2018 the founding team recognised a larger structural problem: companies across Southeast Asia struggled to hire mid-career professionals across borders, and the dominant players (LinkedIn, JobStreet) served large multinationals poorly and ignored sub-$100K roles almost entirely. Glints rebuilt its product around full-time, professional hiring across Vietnam, Indonesia, Taiwan, and Malaysia — effectively abandoning the campus segment that had given it early traction.

The pivot required re-building sales, candidate sourcing, and the product itself from a self-serve board to a managed talent marketplace. By 2021 Glints had grown sufficiently for Monk's Hill Ventures to lead a $22.5M Series C. In June 2023 EDBI led a $50M Series D; management stated the company was profitable in its core markets.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **2013–2017**: Campus/internship model builds brand but reveals low monetisation ceiling — employers pay very little for intern placements.
- **2018–2019**: Strategic pivot to professional hiring across five SEA markets; campus product wound down or deprioritised in favour of full-time job placements.
- **2021**: Series C validates the new positioning; Monk's Hill Ventures cited the underserved $20–80K salary band across SEA as the primary investment thesis.
- **2023**: Series D ($50M, EDBI lead) with profitability claim — the clearest signal the pivot's unit economics worked.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Campus recruitment builds brand recognition but tends to trap platforms in a low-ARPU segment that is both price-sensitive and transient. Glints' willingness to walk away from the segment that gave it early legitimacy — before it was obvious the campus model had a ceiling — is the uncommon move. Most founders confuse early adoption with sustainable business; Glints correctly read campus traction as a bridge, not the destination.
