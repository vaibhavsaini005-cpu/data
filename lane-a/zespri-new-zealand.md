## One-liner

A grower-owned single-desk exporter that was nearly wiped out when the Psa-V vine disease destroyed its flagship gold variety in 2010, bet the industry on an unproven replacement cultivar, and within fifteen years turned that decision into a NZ$5.9 billion sales business whose main strategic problem is now that the same variety is being grown without a licence on some 8,000 hectares in China.

## What happened

**The setup (1980s to 2010).** New Zealand's kiwifruit industry grew fast in the 1970s and then crashed when Italy, Chile and others planted the same green Hayward variety. By 1987, 91% of New Zealand growers were losing money. In 1988 growers voted to sell through a single desk, the New Zealand Kiwifruit Marketing Board, which in 1997 was split into a regulator, Kiwifruit New Zealand, and a 100% grower-owned commercial company, Zespri International. Under the Kiwifruit Industry Restructuring Act 1999 and the Kiwifruit Export Regulations 1999, Zespri is the sole exporter of New Zealand kiwifruit to every market except Australia, a structure that was challenged in the High Court by produce group Turners & Growers and upheld in August 2010, with Zespri CEO Lain Jager calling the case part of a "political campaign to break up the New Zealand kiwifruit industry". The monopoly's justification was differentiation: instead of competing on price with Italian Hayward, Zespri invested in a joint breeding programme with the crown research institute now called Plant & Food Research. Its first output, the yellow-fleshed Hort16A, was released to growers in 1995, first exported in 1998 as Zespri Gold, and became the industry's premium product. By 2010 gold production had reached about 30 million trays a year and the variety was the main source of grower margin.

**The crisis (November 2010 to 2012).** In November 2010 the bacterial vine disease Pseudomonas syringae pv. actinidiae, Psa-V, was confirmed in an orchard near Te Puke in the Bay of Plenty, the centre of the industry. It spread through the region within months. Hort16A turned out to be highly susceptible: vines cankered and died within a season. A Plant & Food Research modeller at the time estimated that within a year or two there might not be a single kiwifruit plant left. Growers such as Te Puke orchardist Jeff Roderick cut out, burnt and buried entire orchards. By March 2012 more than 40% of the country's kiwifruit hectares were infected and about a third of all Hort16A orchards were expected to be removed by the end of that harvest. By June 2012, roughly 1,600 hectares of Hort16A canopy had been cut out. Gold volumes fell from 30 million trays in 2011 to about 11 to 12 million by 2013 and 2014. Orchard values collapsed with the vines.

**The decision (2011 to 2012).** Plant & Food Research assembled a team of more than 100 specialists and screened hundreds of cultivars from the breeding programme for Psa tolerance. One, Zesy002, known as Gold3 and later marketed as Zespri SunGold, had just entered commercialisation when Psa arrived. It showed tolerance in field trials, though nobody could promise it was immune. On 23 March 2012 Zespri announced its "Psa recovery pathway": a wide-scale release of Gold3 licences, with existing Hort16A growers given priority to graft across to the new variety and 400 hectares opened for others to diversify, knowing that grafted vines would take up to three years to return to full production. Chairman John Loughlin said publicly that "this pathway is not without risk. We do not have all the knowledge." On 14 June 2012 Zespri confirmed the release of 2,068 hectares of Gold3 licence, about 15% of the country's entire kiwifruit area, in a single round. Jager described Psa's impact as "devastating" and warned that "no kiwifruit cultivar was immune".

**The recovery (2013 to 2020).** Growers grafted, waited, and were paid. Zespri exported NZ$2.3 billion in the season after the disease was found, about 20% above pre-Psa levels, because green volumes and pricing held while gold was rebuilt. SunGold sales climbed to 48 million trays and NZ$686 million in export value by the 2016/17 season, and to about 75 million trays by 2020. In 2015 the board set a public goal of NZ$4.5 billion in global sales by 2025. Plant & Food Research received the Prime Minister's Science Prize for the Psa response in 2018.

**Monetising the variety (2017 to 2024).** Because Zespri owns the plant variety rights to SunGold, it controls how many hectares are planted, and from the mid-2010s it sold new licence area each year through a tender. The auction became one of the most expensive pieces of agricultural intellectual property in the world: in May 2021, 750 hectares of SunGold licence cleared at an average of about NZ$550,000 per hectare, oversubscribed two to one, generating an estimated NZ$430 to 435 million of licence income that year against NZ$305 million the year before. Prices peaked around NZ$800,000 per hectare in 2021/22 before easing to about NZ$424,000 by May 2024 as Zespri halved the area released and total licence revenue fell from NZ$212 million to an estimated NZ$105 million. Licence income is returned to grower-shareholders as dividends, which is why Zespri's reported net profit swings with the auction.

**The results (2025 to 2026).** For the 2024/25 season, reported 21 May 2025, Zespri passed its 2015 target: global operating revenue of NZ$5.14 billion, fruit sales of NZ$5.03 billion from 220.9 million trays, NZ$3.10 billion paid to New Zealand growers, and per-hectare returns of NZ$89,783 for Green and NZ$170,933 for SunGold. The 2025/26 season, reported 20 May 2026, was a further record: NZ$6.13 billion operating revenue, NZ$5.9 billion fruit sales from 248.1 million trays, NZ$3.56 billion to growers, net profit of NZ$280.1 million (NZ$123.8 million excluding licence revenue, itself a record), and SunGold returning NZ$185,661 per hectare. CEO Jason Te Brake said the results were "something the industry can be proud of, particularly given we delivered a record crop in a much more challenging environment."

**The unresolved threat (2016 to 2026).** The same intellectual property that made SunGold valuable is being copied. Gold3 budwood was smuggled into China around 2016 and by 2021 Zespri estimated more than 5,500 hectares of unauthorised plantings there, against roughly 8,000 hectares of licensed SunGold in New Zealand. In August 2021 Zespri asked its growers to approve a one-year trial of buying, monitoring and marketing fruit from those Chinese orchards under the Zespri label; 70.5% supported the trial and 64.1% supported using the brand, both short of the 75% threshold the Kiwifruit Export Regulations require, so the plan was shelved. Zespri has since pursued enforcement instead: in October 2025 the Wuhan Intermediate People's Court awarded it 5.25 million yuan, about NZ$1.28 million, and ordered 260 hectares of infringing vines removed, in a case Te Brake credited to China's 2022 Seed Law reforms. By July 2026 Zespri's estimate had grown to 7,100 to 8,100 hectares of unlicensed Gold3 in China with class-one packout rates of 70 to 80%, and management was back consulting growers on a "tightly controlled China supply procurement model", with a producer vote not expected before 2027. Te Brake's warning to growers: Zespri "can't take our success in China for granted."

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Choosing a single desk and a differentiated variety over price competition (growers, 1988; Zespri and Plant & Food Research, 1990s).** The 1988 vote is the founding decision. It replaced a fragmented, price-taking industry with one seller that could fund breeding, hold a brand and ration supply. Everything Zespri did in the Psa crisis, from screening hundreds of cultivars to reallocating thousands of hectares in a single season, was possible only because one organisation owned the variety, the brand and the export channel. The Turners & Growers challenge, defeated in August 2010, kept that structure intact three months before it was tested.

- **Committing the industry to an unproven cultivar in one round (Loughlin, Jager and the Zespri board, March to June 2012).** The board could have staged Gold3 slowly while Hort16A was managed with sprays and cut-outs. Instead it released 2,068 hectares at once, roughly 15% of the national crop area, and pointed Hort16A growers at it with priority licences. Loughlin's admission that "we do not have all the knowledge" was accurate: tolerance is not immunity, and a second failure would have finished the gold category. The decision traded a known slow death for an unknown fast recovery, and it was made by an organisation whose owners were the same people bearing the risk.

- **Setting a public NZ$4.5 billion by 2025 target in 2015, mid-recovery.** With gold volumes still well below their 2011 peak, the board committed the industry to nearly doubling sales in a decade. The target organised the licence-release schedule, market investment and grower expectations for ten years. It was exceeded in the 2024/25 season.

- **Auctioning licence area rather than opening the variety (from the mid-2010s).** Zespri chose to ration SunGold planting rights through annual tenders rather than let any grower plant it. That kept supply behind demand, produced per-hectare returns three times those of Green, and created a licence market that at its 2021 to 2023 peak valued a hectare of planting rights at close to NZ$1 million. The same scarcity is what makes the unlicensed Chinese hectares so damaging.

- **Asking growers to legitimise the copied fruit, and being refused (Zespri management, August 2021).** Management's judgement was that the Chinese plantings could not be stopped, so Zespri should control their quality and sell them under its brand. Growers rejected the idea by a margin large enough to matter under the 75% rule. The company has since alternated between litigation and re-proposing a procurement model, and in 2026 is preparing the same question again with the problem 50% bigger.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Zespri is the reference case for **a cooperative that beat a commodity trap by owning intellectual property, then had to defend that IP as its entire margin**. The 1988 single desk and the 1990s breeding programme converted New Zealand kiwifruit from an interchangeable green fruit into a branded, variety-protected product, and SunGold's per-hectare return of roughly NZ$186,000 against Green's NZ$119,000 is the measurable size of that moat. Operators in any category where the product itself can be copied, from seeds to software, will recognise both halves: the premium comes from controlled scarcity, and controlled scarcity invites unlicensed supply.

Second, the Psa response shows **what a concentrated, owner-aligned decision-maker can do in an existential crisis**. Within 18 months of the first infected vine, Zespri had screened its breeding pipeline, chosen a replacement, released licences across 15% of the national area and told growers plainly that the path carried risk. A fragmented industry of competing exporters could not have made that call or funded the three-year wait. The pattern generalises to any founder facing a platform shift that kills the flagship product: the survivors are the ones with a second product already in the pipeline and the authority to bet on it.

Third, the China plantings are a live example of **the limits of enforcement when the copy is good enough**. Zespri has won in court and lost at the ballot box. Its owners, protected by a 75% supermajority rule that exists to stop management diluting the single desk, have so far refused to let the company absorb the pirated supply, and the unlicensed area has grown from 5,500 to around 8,000 hectares in five years. Whether Zespri ends up buying the fruit it did not authorise is the strategic decision that will define its next decade.
