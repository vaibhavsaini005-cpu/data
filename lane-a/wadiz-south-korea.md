## One-liner

Wadiz, South Korea's largest reward- and equity-based crowdfunding platform, grew to 6.8 million users and over 83,000 campaigns worth a combined $928 million, turned its first operating profit in late 2023, and began a global "K-funding" export push in 2025.

## What happened

Wadiz was founded in 2012 by Shin Hye-sung, who left a job at the Korea Development Bank to build a Kickstarter-style platform for the Korean market, supporting both reward-based crowdfunding for new products and equity-based crowdfunding for early-stage companies. Over more than a decade the platform grew into Korea's dominant crowdfunding venue, raising a combined 1.3 trillion won (about $928 million) across more than 83,000 campaigns for 6.8 million users, and building out an ecosystem beyond one-off campaigns: "Wadiz X," a permanent storefront for previously-funded products, and "Wadiz Partners," a venture arm managing roughly 20 billion won in assets. The company raised a 31 billion won ($26.2 million) Series C in May 2019 on top of prior Series B funding, and by late 2023 reported its first monthly operating profit in November, followed by a full half-year operating profit in H2 2023 — a notable milestone in a crowdfunding sector where many platforms globally have struggled to reach sustained profitability. Building on that financial footing, Wadiz announced a global launch on May 21, 2025, opening the platform to users in over 200 countries with English as its first non-Korean language, targeting North America first, and packaging its operating playbook as an exportable "K-funding" model that governments in Southeast Asia, including Thailand, have reportedly expressed interest in importing.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Building adjacent revenue lines (Wadiz X storefront, Wadiz Partners VC arm) instead of relying solely on campaign take-rates**: this diversified income beyond one-time crowdfunding fees and likely contributed to reaching operating profitability in 2023 after over a decade of scaling.
- **Reaching profitability before attempting international expansion**: Wadiz waited until its core Korean business was financially self-sustaining (profitable H2 2023) before committing to a costly global launch in 2025, sequencing growth investment behind proven unit economics rather than the reverse.
- **Packaging its own operating model as an exportable product ("K-funding")**: rather than just opening the existing Korean platform to foreign users, Wadiz is productizing its training programs, policies, and infrastructure for governments and partners abroad — a licensing-style expansion path distinct from typical direct-market-entry crowdfunding expansion.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Wadiz is a rare example of a crowdfunding platform — a business model many investors treat skeptically because of thin take-rates and inconsistent repeat usage — reaching real operating profitability at national scale before expanding internationally. Its slow-delivery model (manufacturing only after a campaign is funded) also directly addresses one of crowdfunding's classic failure modes: overcommitting production before demand is proven. The lesson for other regional platforms is that crowdfunding can become a durable, profitable business if it's treated as retail-commerce infrastructure with adjacent revenue streams, not just a one-time fundraising tool.
