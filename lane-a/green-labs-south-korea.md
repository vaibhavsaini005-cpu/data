## One-liner

A Korean agtech startup raised $140 million at a near-unicorn valuation to
expand globally, then thirteen months later cut 70% of its staff after
running out of cash from that same expansion.

## What happened

Green Labs was founded in 2017 by Sanghoon Shin, Scott Sungwoo Choi, and
Luke Donghyun Ahn to digitize Korea's fragmented small-farm agriculture
sector. Its flagship product, "Farm Morning," aggregated farm data and used
AI to give farmers crop-lifecycle insights, and the company built out both
software and hardware to cover more of the agricultural value chain.

On January 20, 2022, Green Labs closed a $140 million Series C led by BRV
Capital Management, with Skylake Incuvest and SK Square also participating,
at a pre-money valuation of around $700 million — just short of unicorn
status. The round was explicitly earmarked to fund global expansion.

Thirteen months later, on February 21, 2023, TechCrunch reported that Green
Labs had laid off approximately 70% of its workforce — around 350 people —
as part of an emergency restructuring. CEO Sanghoon Shin told staff the
company had overstretched itself on the expansion the Series C was meant to
fund, had run out of money, and was cutting SG&A expenses by roughly 80%
versus year-end 2022 just to survive, leaving about 150 employees. About a
month after the layoffs, on March 30, 2023, Green Labs secured $38.4 million
(50 billion won) in debt financing from its existing investors BRV Capital
and Skylake Incuvest — not new equity, but a lifeline structured to keep the
company solvent rather than to fund further growth.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Raising a large round explicitly for global expansion in a still-early,
  cash-burning business.** Going from a domestic Korean agtech platform to
  a $700M-valuation global growth story in one round put Green Labs on a
  spending trajectory that assumed continued access to capital markets that
  were about to close.
- **Overstretching the expansion itself**, per the CEO's own account — the
  company spent the Series C on scaling into new markets/products faster
  than its underlying agriculture-data business could support, rather than
  proving the model in a second market before a third.
- **Choosing debt over a down-round equity raise to survive.** Taking
  $38.4 million in debt from existing investors, rather than a fresh equity
  round at a lower valuation, let Green Labs avoid marking down the $700M
  valuation publicly — but it also means the surviving, much-smaller company
  now carries repayment obligations its 150 remaining staff have to earn
  their way out of.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Green Labs shows how a strong up-round can accelerate a company straight
into its own crisis: the $140M Series C didn't buy time to find product-
market fit in new markets, it bought the runway to overspend finding out it
didn't have it yet. The 13-month gap between "near-unicorn, funded for
global expansion" and "70% layoffs, out of cash" is short enough that
founders raising large rounds explicitly earmarked for expansion should
treat the round itself as the risk event, not the safety net — the same
capital that removes near-term survival pressure also removes the forcing
function to prove unit economics before scaling them. The debt-over-equity
choice afterward is its own lesson: existing investors will sometimes prefer
propping up a portfolio company's valuation with debt over crystallizing a
loss with a down round, which can mask how close to failure a "survived"
startup actually got.
