## One-liner

Philippine livestreaming and social-commerce app Kumu raised over $100 million by late 2021 — the largest equity raise for a non-conglomerate Filipino startup at the time — then watched losses widen 3.7× in 2022 as the post-COVID engagement boom reversed, forcing a hard pivot from hyper-growth to sustainability.

## What happened

Kumu was founded in 2018 by Roland Ros and Rexy Dorado as a Filipino-first live social platform built for OFW (Overseas Filipino Worker) communities and local creators to connect, compete, and earn through interactive livestreams — combining elements of TikTok Live, Twitch, and group karaoke. The platform's localization ran deep: Filipino-language interfaces, local payment rails (GCash, PayMaya), and dedicated channels for "hugot" (emotional) content and regional talent competitions.

Pandemic-era home confinement accelerated growth dramatically, and the company capitalised: it raised a Series C led by General Atlantic in October 2021 at an undisclosed valuation, pushing total lifetime funding past $100 million — a milestone then unprecedented for a purely Filipino-founded, Filipino-focused consumer-internet startup. The round included participation from Openspace Ventures and SIG, and was framed around an audacious goal: building the world's largest "participatory" social network.

The post-pandemic hangover hit hard. As Filipinos returned to offices, schools, and the streets in 2022, engagement time on livestreaming platforms fell across the board. Kumu's revenue grew 2.4× in 2022, but operating expenses grew faster — selling, administrative, and finance costs ballooned — and losses widened by a factor of 3.7 year-over-year. The company responded with a strategic reset: cutting non-core spending, deprioritising geographic expansion, reducing headcount, and refocusing on creator monetisation efficiency over raw user-count growth. By April 2023, management was projecting profitability by end-2023 and framing the company's new direction as "sustainability over scale."

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Hypergrowth fundraise at peak timing**: The October 2021 Series C was raised at exactly the top of the consumer-social funding market. The capital gave Kumu runway but also created growth expectations that proved undeliverable once engagement normalised.
- **OFW identity moat**: Building for the 10+ million Filipinos abroad, and their families at home, gave Kumu a culturally specific niche that global platforms (TikTok, YouTube Live) could not easily replicate with algorithmic feeds. This moat partially insulated the platform when casual users churned post-pandemic.
- **Pivot to sustainability**: Rather than pursuing another raise to extend the growth narrative, management explicitly chose to optimise for unit economics — a signal of maturity unusual for a company at this stage in Philippine tech.
- **Creator-led monetisation**: Kumu's model relies on virtual gifts and creator revenue-sharing, which is inherently higher-margin than advertising. The sustainability pivot leaned further into this rather than chasing advertising sales.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Kumu's trajectory captures the "pandemic darling to restructuring" arc common across consumer social companies globally, but with a Philippines-specific inflection: a market underserved by global platforms because of language, payment infrastructure, and OFW-specific use cases. The willingness to openly reframe from "world's largest participatory network" to "profitable Filipino community platform" — and to say so publicly — is a notable contrast to companies that kept the hypergrowth narrative alive until funds ran out.
