## One-liner

Encode raised 23.5 billion won across six funding rounds to build Decode, a fashion and luxury pre-order platform, and even acquired a rival French-brand import platform in 2022 to grow faster — but closed in March 2025 after operating losses more than doubled year over year, one of 88 previously-funded Korean startups that shut down in just the first half of 2025.

## What happened

Encode operated Decode, a platform for pre-ordering fashion and luxury goods — letting Korean consumers order items from brands and get them shipped in rather than relying on existing import resellers. The company raised a cumulative 23.5 billion won across six rounds from a strong roster of institutional investors: Woori TA Investment (formerly Yuanta Investment), Shinhan Venture Investment, SV Investment, KT Investment and Mirae Asset Venture Investment. Yuanta's own disclosure shows it invested 2 billion won in Encode's Series B in January 2021.

In 2022, with the capital raised, Encode pursued an aggressive growth strategy: it acquired Now in Paris, a rival platform built around direct imports of well-known French brands, consolidating overlapping demand under one operator rather than competing for the same customers with two platforms.

The acquisition did not fix the underlying economics. Encode's operating loss more than doubled, from roughly 5.7 billion won in 2022 to approximately 12.1 billion won in 2023, as profitability deteriorated against a backdrop industry coverage describes as a worldwide luxury downturn compounded by cutthroat competition between platforms competing for the same shrinking pool of willing spenders. Encode filed for closure in March 2025, citing sustained operating losses and capital impairment. Yuanta booked the resulting 2 billion won as an impairment loss in its 2024 financial statements — one contributor to Yuanta's swing from a 44 billion won operating profit in 2023 to a 4 billion won operating loss in 2024.

Encode's failure was not an isolated case. It was one of 88 Korean startups that had previously raised over 1 billion won in investment and still closed in the first half of 2025 alone — a figure industry coverage flagged as a potential record. Encode's own competitive category illustrates how systemic the pressure was: the three major Korean luxury resale/pre-order platforms collectively nicknamed "Must-Trenbe-Balaan" (Mustit, Trenbe, Balaan) posted similarly large operating losses through 2023, and Balaan filed for corporate rehabilitation (기업회생, a Korean equivalent of Chapter 11) in the same month Encode closed — March 2025.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Acquiring a direct competitor in 2022 to consolidate demand rather than compete for it.** The Now in Paris acquisition assumed that combining two loss-making luxury platforms would produce one platform with a path to profit. Instead, operating losses more than doubled the following year — the merger did not change the category's fundamental economics.
- **Scaling fundraising (six rounds, 23.5bn won, five institutional investors) ahead of unit economics.** A strong and growing investor roster kept the company capitalized through a worsening loss trajectory, which extended its runway without correcting its trajectory.
- **Competing in a category where every major player had the same cost structure problem.** Mustit, Trenbe and Balaan all posted comparable losses in the same period, which means Encode's difficulties were not about being out-executed by a specific rival — the entire luxury pre-order/resale category in Korea shared the same unprofitable unit economics simultaneously.
- **Filing for voluntary closure rather than attempting a restructuring.** Balaan, facing the same pressures in the same month, chose corporate rehabilitation — an attempt to continue operating under court protection. Encode instead wound down outright, a materially different choice given comparable circumstances.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Encode is a clear example of an acquisition used to buy scale rather than to fix a cost structure. Combining two money-losing platforms is sound strategy only if the combined entity gains pricing power, shared fixed costs, or a defensible moat that neither had alone — and the post-acquisition numbers show none of that materialized: losses more than doubled the very next year. For founders evaluating a consolidating acquisition in a declining category, the Encode case argues for stress-testing whether the acquisition changes the category's underlying economics at all, rather than simply adding the acquired company's losses to your own balance sheet under a unified brand.

The wider lesson is in the category-level clustering of failure. When three direct competitors in the same niche (Mustit, Trenbe, Balaan) all report similar losses in the same years, and a fourth operator (Encode) closes in the same month a third files for rehabilitation, the correct diagnosis is a structural problem with the category's economics — likely driven here by a genuine demand contraction (the luxury downturn) meeting a market with too many well-capitalized competitors fighting over it — not a failure specific to any one team's execution. Investors and founders evaluating a niche where every incumbent shows the same financial symptoms should treat that as a signal about the category's ceiling, not about which competitor will eventually "win."
