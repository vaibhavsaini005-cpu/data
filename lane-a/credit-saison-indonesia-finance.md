## One-liner

Indonesia's financial regulator OJK revoked the multifinance licence of PT Credit Saison Indonesia Finance — the lending arm backed 82% by Japan's Credit Saison and 18% by the holding company behind P2P fintech KoinWorks — after the company was placed under special supervision and failed, within the deadline it was given, to fix the problems that put it there.

## What happened

CSIF held an Indonesian multifinance (perusahaan pembiayaan) licence granted by OJK in 2015, operating for most of its life as PT Saison Modern Finance before a name change approved on December 11, 2024. Its shareholder register tied together two very different kinds of backer: Credit Saison Co. Ltd., the Japanese consumer-finance group, with an 82% stake, and PT Koinworks Manajemen Konsultasi — the holding entity associated with Indonesian P2P lending startup KoinWorks — with the remaining 18%.

At some point before 2026 OJK placed CSIF under *pengawasan khusus*, special supervision: the regulator's formal pre-failure state, in which a company keeps operating but must execute an agreed corrective action plan against a deadline. OJK's own account of what followed is procedural rather than dramatic. The regulator said it had given CSIF sufficient time to implement the corrective measures in that action plan, and that the company could not resolve the causes of its special-supervision status before the deadline expired. CSIF was therefore classified under the category Indonesian regulators call *tidak dapat disehatkan* — unable to be rehabilitated.

OJK revoked the licence by decision KEP-57/D.06/2026, dated September 24, 2026, acting under Article 38 of OJK Regulation No. 49 of 2024 on the supervision of financing institutions, as amended by OJK Regulation No. 25 of 2025. The decision was made public on September 30, announced through Sekar Putih Djarot, head of OJK's public communications directorate. CSIF is now barred from conducting any financing activity and is required to settle its outstanding rights and obligations — with customer contact routed through a single phone line, a single email address, and an office at Cyber 2 Tower in South Jakarta.

The failure arrived inside a broader contraction. Indonesia's P2P lending and consumer-finance sector has been squeezed through 2026 by tightened regulation and high non-performing loan levels, with late-stage funding down 60–70% from its peak and a visible rise in operational closures.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Being placed under special supervision and treating the corrective window as survivable.** Special supervision is a last-chance mechanism with a clock on it. The determining event was not the revocation in September but whatever happened — or failed to happen — during the action-plan period, which the company did not complete.
- **A split shareholder structure across a Japanese strategic parent and an Indonesian fintech holding entity.** An 82% owner with deep balance-sheet capacity did not, in the end, recapitalise the licence into compliance. That is the most informative fact in the file: the money to fix it plausibly existed and was not deployed.
- **Rebranding in late 2024, mid-decline.** The rename from Saison Modern Finance to Credit Saison Indonesia Finance tightened the identification with the Japanese parent roughly two years before the parent let the licence go.
- **Operating a regulated lending licence through a sector-wide NPL deterioration.** In multifinance, asset-quality problems are not a bad quarter; they are a supervisory trigger that transfers control of the timeline to the regulator.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

The useful pattern here is that a well-capitalised strategic parent is not the same thing as committed capital. CSIF did not die of insolvency in the ordinary sense — it died because the entity holding 82% of it declined to do what would have been required to clear OJK's bar before the clock ran out. For founders taking strategic investment from a large foreign financial group, this is the risk that rarely gets modelled: the parent's balance sheet is strong precisely because it is disciplined about which subsidiaries it rescues, and a licence in a deteriorating market in a secondary geography is an easy line item to write off.

The second lesson is structural, about regulated businesses specifically. Once a financial regulator places a company under special supervision, the company no longer controls its own timeline — survival becomes a matter of satisfying an external checklist by an external date, and ordinary commercial judgement about what to prioritise stops applying. Entering that state is close to a point of no return: OJK's language ("sufficient time," "could not address the causes," "unable to be rehabilitated") describes a process that ran to completion exactly as designed. Startups that hold licences should treat the first supervisory escalation, not the eventual revocation, as the moment the business is actually at stake.
