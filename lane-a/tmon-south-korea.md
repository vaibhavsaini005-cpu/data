## One-liner

TMON (formerly Ticket Monster), one of South Korea's earliest e-commerce platforms, collapsed into court receivership in July 2024 after its Singapore parent Qoo10 used vendor settlement floats to fund aggressive M&A deals, leaving 60,000+ merchants owed up to ₩1 trillion (~$722M).

## What happened

TMON was founded in 2010 as South Korea's answer to Groupon — a daily-deals flash-sale site that expanded into a full horizontal marketplace. It attracted early backing from LivingSocial and was acquired by KKR in 2015, before being sold to Qoo10 in September 2022. Qoo10 simultaneously acquired Interpark Commerce (March 2023) and WeMakePrice (April 2023), rapidly assembling a Korean e-commerce portfolio.

The fatal flaw: Qoo10's Korean subsidiaries retained merchant settlement funds — money owed to sellers from completed sales — for weeks before remitting. Qoo10 used this rolling float as working capital to service debt from its acquisition spree. When the underlying cash flow tightened in mid-2024, settlements stopped. By July 2024, over 2,700 vendors had not received May payments. TMON and WeMakePrice filed for court receivership with the Seoul Bankruptcy Court on July 29, 2024. Prosecutors raided the offices of both platforms and the personal residences of Qoo10 CEO Ku Young-bae. The government deployed ₩560 billion in emergency support funds for affected small businesses.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **2022 — Qoo10 acquisition**: Selling to a financially stretched acquirer that had no Korean market operating history was the pivotal risk event. KKR and Anchor Equity Partners exited; new capital structure was opaque.
- **Float-as-capital model**: TMON's settlement cycle (typically 30–60 days after transaction) gave Qoo10 a steady cash pool it treated as balance-sheet liquidity rather than a liability — a model that works until it doesn't.
- **Delayed refund freeze**: When TMON stopped issuing consumer refunds alongside merchant payments, public trust collapsed immediately, triggering a run on the platform and accelerating the receivership filing.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Platform businesses that hold merchant settlement funds sit on a structural float that looks like liquidity but is operationally owed. When a parent entity uses that float as M&A financing, the resulting liability is invisible in reported financials until payments stop. TMON is a cautionary case for any market where e-commerce platforms operate on extended settlement windows — and for regulators who approved Qoo10's multi-acquisition rollup without requiring ring-fencing of settlement pools.
