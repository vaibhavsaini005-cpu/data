## One-liner

Vietjet, licensed in 2007 as Vietnam's first private airline and launched on 25 December 2011 only after its AirAsia joint venture was blocked by Hanoi, broke the state carrier's 60-year monopoly with a bikini-marketed budget product, a fleet financed by bulk Airbus and Boeing orders resold to lessors for gains, and a US$167 million 2017 IPO that was then Vietnam's largest; it matched Vietnam Airlines' 42% domestic share by 2016, survived COVID without a state bailout while its rival took a VND12 trillion rescue, and by 2025 carried 28.2 million passengers on VND82 trillion (US$3.16 billion) of revenue with 254 routes and a 100-aircraft A321neo order, though a core business that has leaned on sale-and-leaseback profits remains its standing critique.

## What happened

**The setup (2007–2011).** Nguyen Thi Phuong Thao made her first million at 21 trading fax machines and latex rubber while a student in Moscow, and built the Sovico Holdings group with her husband Nguyen Thanh Hung across banking (HDBank), real estate and energy. On 30 November 2007 Vietjet Aviation received Vietnam's first licence for a privately owned airline. Then nothing flew for four years. The 2008 oil spike and financial crisis made a launch reckless, and Thao chose to wait rather than launch into a fuel-price wall with a thin balance sheet. In February 2010 she found what looked like the shortcut: AirAsia agreed to take a 30% stake and bring its brand, systems and low-cost playbook to Vietnam. The Vietnamese government refused, repeatedly, to let a domestic carrier fly under a foreign brand, and in October 2011 AirAsia walked away citing the failure to obtain regulatory approvals. Vietjet, as CAPA reported on 17 October 2011, announced it would launch in December 2011 without AirAsia. The first flight, Ho Chi Minh City to Hanoi, operated on 25 December 2011.

**The land grab (2012–2016).** Vietjet entered a market where Vietnam Airlines and its Jetstar Pacific affiliate had effectively 100% of domestic capacity and where perhaps 1% of the population had ever flown. Thao's launch marketing put flight attendants in bikinis on an inaugural beach-route flight, earned a US$1,000 fine from the aviation authority in 2012, and generated more brand awareness than any paid campaign; her response was "we don't mind people associating the airline with the bikini image." Underneath the noise the model was orthodox Southeast Asian LCC: single-type A320-family fleet, high-density seating, aggressive ancillaries, 2.3 US cents per available seat kilometre in unit costs and load factors near 88%. The financing was not orthodox. In February 2014 Vietjet ordered 60 A320-family aircraft worth US$6.4 billion at list, added 30 more at Dubai in 2015, then signed for 100 Boeing 737 MAX (US$11.3 billion at list) during President Obama's May 2016 Hanoi visit and 20 more A321s during President Hollande's September 2016 visit. Buying in bulk at steep discounts and immediately selling the aircraft to lessors at closer to market value, then leasing them back, produced cash gains on delivery that flowed through the income statement. First international route, to Bangkok, launched February 2013; 10 millionth passenger December 2014; 25 millionth December 2015. By 2016 Vietjet carried 42% of Vietnam's domestic passengers, level with Vietnam Airlines, and 27% of the total market including international, and had reported a profit every year since 2013.

**The listing (2017).** Vietjet listed on the Ho Chi Minh Stock Exchange as VJC on 28 February 2017 at a reference price of VND90,000, having placed 44.78 million shares for about US$167–170 million with BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, J.P. Morgan and Viet Capital as agents. It was the largest IPO in Vietnam's history at the time and the first marketed to international institutions. Market value at listing was about VND27 trillion (US$1.2–1.4 billion) on 2016 revenue of VND27.5 trillion and net profit of VND2.39 trillion; the stock rose the maximum 20% on day one. Thao became the first self-made female billionaire in Southeast Asia. That same year the domestic market slowed to under 10% growth, Vietnam Airlines' dual-brand strategy clawed back share for the first time since 2011, and Vietjet, having targeted 50% domestic share, instead ceded ground and redirected almost all new capacity to international routes, a pivot CAPA flagged in November 2017. By 2018 Vietjet's revenue was VND54 trillion (US$2.3 billion), up 27%, on 23 million passengers, and it had overtaken Vietnam Airlines in passengers carried.

**The stress test (2020–2021).** COVID cut Vietjet's nine-month 2020 revenue by 63% to VND13.8 trillion and produced a VND2.6 trillion pre-tax loss through September. The state responded asymmetrically. In November 2020 the National Assembly approved a VND12 trillion (about US$520 million) rescue for Vietnam Airlines, combining VND4 trillion of refinanced bank loans and a rights issue. Vietjet asked for VND4 trillion in low-interest loans and got only the sector-wide fee reductions and tax deferrals. It survived instead by selling and leasing back four to five aircraft for roughly VND2 trillion (US$86.6 million), converting passenger aircraft to cargo, cutting hours rather than heads (zero layoffs, about 27,000 training sessions delivered to idle crew) and flooding back into the domestic market when it reopened, adding eight routes by July 2020 to reach 52 domestic routes. It closed 2020 with a small positive result. 2021 was worse, with 5.4 million passengers and VND12.8 trillion of revenue, and the company still reported a marginal net profit of VND80 billion.

**The outcome (2023–2026).** Recovery came fast and international. 2024 consolidated revenue was VND71.9 trillion (US$2.94 billion), up 23%, with consolidated net profit of VND1.43 trillion (US$58 million), up 516%, on 25.9 million passengers, 137,000 flights, 145 routes of which 101 were international, a 94-aircraft fleet, 87% load factor and VND7.7 trillion of cash. 2025 set new records: consolidated revenue VND82.1 trillion (US$3.16 billion), up 14%; consolidated pre-tax profit VND2.63 trillion (US$101 million), up 44%; post-tax profit VND2.12 trillion (US$82 million), up 51%; 28.2 million passengers on 153,000 flights; 254 routes (52 domestic, 202 international); 22 aircraft delivered in one year; total assets VND139 trillion (US$5.4 billion); and a VND5 trillion share placement. At the June 2025 Paris Air Show Vietjet ordered 100 A321neos with 50 options plus a US$3.8 billion Rolls-Royce Trent 7000 engine package for its widebody ambitions, and in May 2025 it announced Vietjet Qazaqstan, a third foreign affiliate after Thai Vietjet. As of July 2026 the group operated 131 aircraft, of which only 9 were owned and 122 leased; leasing was 93% of aircraft expenses in 2025. Founder Thao remains CEO and controlling shareholder through Sovico and HDBank.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Waiting four years to launch, then launching alone (Nguyen Thi Phuong Thao, 2007–2011).** Sitting on a licence through the 2008 fuel spike preserved capital that a 2008 launch would have burned. Then, when Hanoi blocked the AirAsia brand, Thao chose to launch without a partner rather than keep lobbying or shelve the project. This forced Vietjet to build its own low-cost operating system and made it a national rather than a franchised carrier, which later mattered politically.

- **Marketing to a country that had never flown (2011–2012).** The bikini campaign and the US$1,000 fine were a deliberate trade of respectability for awareness in a market where the addressable customer had no prior airline preference to displace. It turned Vietjet into a brand before it was a network.

- **Financing growth through bulk orders and sale-and-leaseback gains (2014–2016 orders; ongoing).** Ordering 200-plus aircraft at list values above US$20 billion while a small private company was the most contested decision in Vietjet's history. It created an asset-light fleet, a near-zero owned balance sheet and a recurring source of non-operating profit; CAPA noted in 2017 that "a large proportion of VietJet's profits have been generated from sale leasebacks," and the critique persists into 2026. The upside is a decade of reported profits; the risk is that lease costs (93% of aircraft expenses in 2025) and delivery-timing dependence make earnings hostage to the order book.

- **Listing internationally on a domestic exchange (February 2017).** Taking a US$170 million IPO to global institutions with bulge-bracket banks, rather than a purely local float, gave Vietjet a currency for fleet growth and a governance discipline unusual for a Vietnamese private company, and set the template Vingroup and others followed.

- **Redirecting capacity to international routes when the domestic market slowed (2017).** Instead of chasing the 50% domestic share target into a price war with a state carrier that could outlast it, Vietjet accepted a lower domestic share and built the international network that produced 202 of its 254 routes by 2025.

- **Surviving COVID without a bailout (2020–2021).** Denied the VND12 trillion its state rival received, Vietjet chose asset sales, cargo conversion and hour cuts over layoffs, kept its crews and slots intact, and was positioned to take share in the 2022–2024 reopening. The decision not to cut staff was expensive in 2021 and decisive by 2024.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Vietjet is the regional reference case for how a private challenger beats an entrenched state incumbent in a regulated industry: not by matching it product-for-product but by expanding the market (first-time flyers), refusing the fights it cannot win (a domestic share war against a bailout-backed rival) and choosing capital structures the incumbent cannot copy. Operators will recognise three transferable patterns. First, the blocked partnership that becomes an advantage: losing AirAsia forced Vietjet to own its playbook, and a decade later the "foreign brand" that Hanoi rejected is a competitor rather than a parent. Second, financial engineering as a growth engine and a reputational liability at the same time; sale-and-leaseback gains funded the expansion that made Vietjet real, and the same gains give every sceptical analyst a permanent question about the quality of its core earnings. Founders using a similar non-operating profit source should expect that question to outlive the phase in which the tactic was necessary. Third, asymmetric state support is survivable if you use the crisis to preserve capacity rather than cash: Vietjet's zero-layoff, sell-the-planes response to a rival's VND12 trillion bailout is a template for private operators in markets where the government is also a competitor.
