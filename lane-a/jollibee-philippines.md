## One-liner

The Filipino fast-food chain that beat McDonald's at home by refusing to copy it, then spent a decade and more than $800 million learning — expensively — how to buy growth abroad: two money-losing US acquisitions cost it $2 billion in market value before a switch to buying profitable Asian franchisors (Highlands Coffee, Tim Ho Wan, Compose Coffee) turned international into the growth engine and set up a US listing of the overseas arm.

## What happened

**Setup: winning at home by being local (1975–2000s).** Tony Tan Caktiong, a civil engineer from a Chinese-Filipino immigrant family, opened a Magnolia ice-cream parlour in Cubao, Quezon City in 1975. Customers kept asking for hot food; in 1978 the family dropped the ice-cream franchise, incorporated Jollibee Foods Corporation (JFC), and started selling hamburgers. When McDonald's entered the Philippines a few years later, the conventional advice was to imitate the American model. Tan Caktiong did the opposite: sweet-style spaghetti with hot dogs, rice meals, and above all Chickenjoy fried chicken with gravy, priced and flavoured for Filipino palates. The bet worked so completely that Jollibee became one of the very few home-grown chains anywhere to out-compete McDonald's in its own market — roughly 50% of the Philippine chicken-and-burger market against McDonald's 29% by the 2020s. Through the 1990s and 2000s JFC rolled up the domestic market: Greenwich pizza (1994), Chowking Chinese fast food (2000), Red Ribbon bakeshops (2005), Mang Inasal grilled chicken (70% stake, 2010), and the Burger King Philippines franchise (2011). Its first international brand was China's Yonghe King in 2004.

**The pressure: a saturated home market and a "top five" ambition.** By the mid-2010s JFC dominated a single, mid-income country of about 110 million people. Tan Caktiong and his brother-in-law Ernesto Tanmantiong (CEO since 2014) set a public "North Star": become one of the world's top five restaurant companies by market capitalisation. That could not be done inside the Philippines. Tanmantiong's stated logic for M&A was blunt — "If it's already available out there, why not just acquire it?" — and the initial targets were American.

**The US gamble (2015–2019).** In October 2015 JFC bought 40% of Denver-based "better burger" chain Smashburger at a $335 million valuation. In February 2018 it paid $100 million for another 45% (to 85%) and in December 2018 bought the remaining 15% from Black Shamrock Partners — around 360 restaurants across 38 states and nine countries, with $338 million in US system sales. Then, on 24 July 2019, it announced its "largest and most multinational acquisition": The Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf (CBTL), bought for $350 million on a debt-free basis. The structure was creative: JFC put in $100 million for 80% of a Singapore vehicle, the family behind its Vietnamese partner Viet Thai International (operator of Highlands Coffee) took 20%, and the remaining $250 million came as advances. CBTL brought roughly 1,180 outlets in 27 countries (336 company-owned, 844 franchised), lifted international to 36% of worldwide sales, and added 14% to system-wide sales in one stroke. The deal closed 24 September 2019.

**The outcome: the market rebels.** Both US brands were losing money when bought — CBTL lost $21 million in 2018 after $26.75 million in 2017, and Smashburger was mid-turnaround — and both kept losing money under JFC. Smashburger's poor store locations and inconsistent quality knocked nearly a quarter off JFC's net income. JFC shares fell 8% on the day CBTL was announced, had their worst year in two decades in 2019, and were down another 18% by early March 2020: about $2.22 billion of market value erased since the end of 2018, against roughly $540 million spent on the two chains. Bloomberg's headline framed it as Asia's fried-chicken king taking "a $2 billion hit with US gamble." Then COVID-19 arrived.

**The reset (2020–2021).** JFC lost ₱11.5 billion in 2020 (versus a ₱7.3 billion profit in 2019) as system-wide sales fell 27.8% to ₱176 billion. Rather than just ride it out, management launched a ₱7 billion "business transformation programme" in Q2 2020: 486 underperforming stores and four commissaries were shut permanently (₱6.7 billion of the budget spent that year), resources were redirected to delivery, and the company swung back to a ₱2 billion profit in Q4 2020. Tanmantiong called it "a very difficult and painful program but the right thing to do for the long term good of the business" — and stated the new strategic centre of gravity outright: "In 2021 and the years ahead, JFC's sales and profit growth will be driven by its international business." The international portfolio was quietly reshaped in the same window: JFC took Tim Ho Wan (Hong Kong dim sum) to 100% in 2021, took a majority of Taiwanese bubble-tea chain Milksha, and formed a 50/50 Philippine JV with Yoshinoya.

**The second international strategy: buy Asian franchisors that already make money (2024).** On 2 July 2024 JFC announced its next big deal — but with the opposite profile to Smashburger and CBTL. Compose Coffee was a South Korean value-coffee chain with 2,600+ stores, every one of them franchised, wholly owned by its founder Yang Jaeseok. In 2023 it made ₩36.7 billion of operating profit on ₩88.9 billion of revenue — a 41.2% margin, on a debt-free balance sheet. JFC paid $238 million for 70% (enterprise value about $340 million), with Elevation Equity Partners Korea taking 25% and JFC's own Titan Fund 5%. Management guided that Compose would add only ~2% to revenue but ~12% to EBIT and ~34% to store count, take international to 41% of revenue, and instantly become JFC's largest brand by number of outlets. The point of the deal was margin and growth, not flags on a map.

**Where it stands (2025–2026).** 2025 was JFC's best year: system-wide sales ₱455.1 billion (+16.6%), revenue ₱305.1 billion (+13.0%), operating income ₱20.2 billion (+19.3%) with a record fourth quarter (+41.9%), and net income ₱10.9 billion. International system-wide sales grew 27.0% against a mid-single-digit domestic market; the coffee-and-tea segment grew 44.9%, with Compose Coffee's system sales up 217% in its first full year and its store count at 2,972 by year-end. JFC opened 1,126 stores in the year — the most in its history — to reach 10,341, of which 6,837 are outside the Philippines. In January 2026 the company announced it would spin off the international business (Jollibee overseas, Smashburger, Tim Ho Wan, CBTL, Compose and the rest) and list it on a US exchange by late 2027, separating the "stable, cash-generative" Philippine business from the "higher-growth but more volatile" international one. Shares jumped 14% in a day, the most since 2008, and another 7.6% the next.

## Key decisions / turning points

1. **Don't copy McDonald's — localise (Tony Tan Caktiong, 1978–1980s).** The founding strategic choice: compete on Filipino taste (Chickenjoy, sweet spaghetti, rice) and price rather than on replicating the American model. It produced one of the world's only home-market defeats of McDonald's, and it is the source of the cash flow (97% of operating profit still came from the Philippines in 2023) that funded every later bet.

2. **Set an explicit global market-cap goal and let it drive M&A (Tan Caktiong / Tanmantiong, mid-2010s).** The "top five restaurant companies by market cap" target was public and unambiguous, and it was the reason JFC bought rather than built abroad. The goal was right; the first execution — buying US chains for reach rather than economics — was not.

3. **Buying Smashburger and CBTL despite both losing money (2015–2019).** The board approved roughly $540 million for two unprofitable, largely company-operated Western brands, on the theory that JFC's operating discipline would fix them. The market's verdict was immediate (−8% on the CBTL announcement) and sustained (−$2.2 billion of market value in 14 months). The CBTL structure — bringing the Viet Thai family in for 20% and funding most of it with advances — shows management was already hedging the risk, but the underlying bet was still "we can turn around US company-operated stores from Manila."

4. **Cutting 486 stores in a single programme during the pandemic rather than protecting the network (Tanmantiong, Q2 2020).** The ₱7 billion transformation programme was a deliberate choice to book the pain in one year. It restored profitability by Q4 2020 and — more importantly — was the moment management publicly re-anchored growth on international, but on different terms.

5. **Switching the acquisition thesis from "Western brands with reach" to "Asian franchisors with margin" (2021–2024).** Tim Ho Wan to 100%, Milksha, and then Compose Coffee at a 41% operating margin and zero company-owned stores. Compose's contribution — 2% of revenue, 12% of EBIT — was the explicit reversal of the Smashburger/CBTL profile (lots of revenue and stores, negative profit). This is the decision that turned the international business from a drag into the growth story.

6. **Spinning off and US-listing the international business (Jan 2026).** By separating the two businesses, JFC is asking investors to value international growth on its own multiple rather than blending it with a mature domestic cash cow — and, notably, doing it in the US market where its earlier acquisitions had cost it so dearly.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

- **Winning at home with a differentiated model does not transfer to buying abroad.** Jollibee's edge — deep local taste knowledge, cheap real estate, a beloved brand — was precisely the thing it could not export to Denver or Los Angeles. Its first international strategy bought brands whose problems (weak US store economics) were in the one area where Jollibee had no advantage. Operators who dominate a home market should be suspicious of acquisitions where their core capability is irrelevant to the target's problem.

- **Distinguish "reach" acquisitions from "economics" acquisitions.** Smashburger and CBTL added stores, countries and revenue headlines; Compose Coffee added profit. JFC's own guidance made the contrast explicit (2% of revenue, 12% of EBIT). The market rewarded the second kind and punished the first. Any founder rolling up a category should be able to say, per deal, which of the two it is.

- **A public, numeric ambition is a double-edged tool.** "Top five by market cap" gave JFC a decade of strategic consistency and helped sell the spin-off story in 2026. It also, plausibly, pushed the company into overpaying for scale in 2015–2019. The fix was not to drop the goal but to change the constraint (profitable franchisors) under which it was pursued.

- **Use a crisis to reset strategy, not just costs.** The 2020 programme is remembered for the 486 closures, but its lasting output was the statement that international would drive growth — and the change in what "international" meant. Companies that only cut in a downturn come out smaller; JFC came out re-pointed.

- **Franchised, asset-light targets scale faster than company-operated ones — and are easier to buy from a distance.** CBTL's 336 company-owned stores were a turnaround job; Compose's 2,600 franchised stores were a royalty stream. For an acquirer managing from another country, the second model is far more forgiving, which is why JFC's Asian coffee-and-tea platform (Highlands, CBTL, Compose, Milksha) is now growing at 45% a year.

- **Separating the growth story from the cash cow is a legitimate way to get paid for a pivot.** The 2026 spin-off decision recognises that a single listed conglomerate — 97% of profit from a mature market, all the growth abroad — was being valued on the wrong business. Whether the US listing succeeds is unresolved; the strategic logic is a pattern other emerging-market champions with an international growth arm will recognise.
