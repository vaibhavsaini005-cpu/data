## One-liner

Owner and director Colin Neal, who had already closed five of Smiths City's stores over the prior months citing collapsed demand ("you don't buy a TV to eat"), put the 107-year-old New Zealand retailer into voluntary administration on 2 September 2025 rather than keep trading through the downturn.

## What happened

Smiths City, a Christchurch-founded furniture and appliance retailer dating to 1918, had been bought in 2020 by investment company Polar Capital — owned by Colin Neal — for roughly $60 million (about $8 million net of liabilities), as part of an attempted brand revival. By August 2025 the retailer had already closed five stores as consumer spending on big-ticket household goods collapsed; Neal told The Post that "it's the economy ... you don't buy a TV to eat," describing spending on whiteware, TVs and furniture as discretionary in a way food spending isn't, with sales down 40-45% over roughly two years. Rather than continue drawing down further, Neal, as director, put the company into voluntary administration on 2 September 2025, appointing Colin Gower and Diana Matchett of BDO Christchurch as joint administrators; all nine remaining stores and the online shop were closed that morning while administrators sought urgent expressions of interest from buyers. No buyer emerged: creditors voted on 1 October 2025 to put the company into liquidation, owing an estimated $25.7 million. A store building was later demolished, closing out 107 years of trading.

## Key decisions / turning points

- August 2025: Neal frames the store closures already underway as demand-driven, not a temporary blip, in an on-record statement to The Post.
- 2 September 2025: Neal, as director, makes the call to stop trading and place the company into voluntary administration rather than continue seeking a buyer while operating.
- 1 October 2025: creditors vote to liquidate rather than restructure, after no acquirer emerged during the administration period.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Neal's own framing — that TVs and furniture are the first things households cut when they're not cutting food — is a specific, named-founder articulation of a broader pattern: a five-year-old buyout-and-revival bet on a century-old retail brand ran into a demand shock it couldn't survive, and the director chose to stop trading at the point he judged further losses wouldn't be recoverable, rather than run the administration process while stores stayed open. The fact that the owner had personally guaranteed at least one store lease also meant the consequences of that call outlasted the liquidation itself — a reminder that a director's decision to close a company doesn't always close out that director's own exposure.
