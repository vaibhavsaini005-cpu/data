## One-liner

WeWork Japan, the SoftBank-backed joint venture that opened the country's first WeWork location in 2018 and grew to about 40 sites, filed for civil rehabilitation at the Tokyo District Court in February 2024 after its US parent's Chapter 11 bankruptcy, and was fully absorbed by SoftBank Corp. rather than wound down.

## What happened

WeWork Japan GK was established in 2017 as a joint venture between SoftBank Corp. and WeWork Inc., and opened its first Tokyo coworking space in February 2018. It expanded to roughly 40 locations across Japan, riding the same aggressive lease-driven growth model that WeWork pursued globally under founder Adam Neumann. That global model collapsed after WeWork Inc. filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the US on 6 November 2023, having gone from a 2019 private valuation near $47 billion to a business its own bankruptcy filing said was no longer viable without shedding hundreds of leases.

The US restructuring left WeWork Japan financially exposed to its own lease obligations without the ability to draw on a US parent going through bankruptcy. On 1 February 2024, WeWork Japan filed a petition for civil rehabilitation (minji saisei), Japan's court-supervised procedure for financially distressed but potentially viable companies, at the Tokyo District Court. SoftBank Corp. agreed to act as sponsor, and structured the rescue by establishing a new subsidiary to absorb WeWork Japan's operations and business-related debts in full, rather than letting the operating company liquidate. SoftBank said current members would see no change to service levels or contract terms. Completion of the business transfer was expected by April 2024.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Riding the WeWork Inc. global growth playbook into Japan (2017–2018).** Launching as a 50/50-style joint venture with SoftBank rather than a directly owned subsidiary let WeWork Japan scale quickly on the parent's brand and capital, but also tied its fate directly to WeWork Inc.'s balance sheet.
- **WeWork Inc.'s Chapter 11 filing (6 November 2023)** cut Japan's operating entity off from parent-level support just as it needed it most, forcing a separate, local insolvency response rather than a coordinated global one.
- **Filing for civil rehabilitation instead of liquidation (1 February 2024).** Choosing Japan's rehabilitation process signalled the entity itself was viewed as viable if freed from its debt load — the goal was continuity of the coworking business, not shutdown.
- **SoftBank absorbing the debt and taking full ownership** rather than letting a third party or the bankruptcy court find a new sponsor. SoftBank had run the venture since 2017 and had the strongest incentive (and local relationships with landlords) to keep the space network intact under its own name.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

WeWork Japan illustrates how a strong local rollout can still be capsized by a parent company's global overreach: the Japan business had a decade-long track record of real occupied space, yet it needed a formal court rehabilitation once WeWork Inc.'s worldwide lease liabilities forced a bankruptcy filing 8,000 miles away. It's also a case study in how a corporate parent can turn a subsidiary's near-failure into a controlled absorption rather than a loss: SoftBank had already written down much of its WeWork equity globally, but chose to swallow the Japan entity's debt directly to keep roughly 40 functioning locations and their existing members rather than exit the market.
