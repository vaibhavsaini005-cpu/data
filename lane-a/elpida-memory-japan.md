## One-liner

Japan's last independent DRAM maker, formed by merging the memory-chip units of NEC, Hitachi and Mitsubishi, collapsed into the largest post-war bankruptcy by a Japanese manufacturer in 2012 and was sold off to Micron for a fraction of the state money spent trying to save it.

## What happened

Elpida Memory was created in 1999 as NEC Hitachi Memory, merging NEC's and Hitachi's DRAM (dynamic random-access memory) businesses to consolidate Japan's fragmented, money-losing chip industry into one national champion able to compete with Samsung and other lower-cost Asian rivals. It took the Elpida name in 2000 and absorbed Mitsubishi's DRAM business in 2003. For most of the 2000s the strategy looked plausible: Elpida became Japan's sole remaining DRAM maker and the world's third-largest, running a 300mm fabrication plant in Hiroshima and a majority-owned Taiwan fab (Rexchip) to chase scale.

The pressure that eventually broke Elpida was structural: DRAM is a commodity business where price is set by the lowest-cost producer, and Samsung and other rivals kept undercutting Elpida on cost while diversifying into flash memory, where Elpida had comparatively weak capabilities. Under CEO Yukio Sakamoto, Elpida bet on staying in premium, higher-margin DRAM products manufactured in Japan while shifting commodity production to the Taiwan fab — but orders for the higher-margin chips fell short, forcing Elpida to keep running low-margin commodity lines anyway, which starved the restructuring of the cash it needed. Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) approved a ¥30 billion bailout in 2009 to keep the company alive through the global financial crisis, but a subsequent attempt to broker a partnership with Taiwan Memory Co. fell apart, and METI did not force a more aggressive fix.

By February 2012, with ¥448 billion in liabilities it could not service, Elpida filed for bankruptcy protection — the biggest bankruptcy by a Japanese manufacturer in the post-war era. Micron Technology, the US chipmaker that Japan's own industrial policy had effectively helped position for the opportunity, moved to acquire it; the deal closed in 2013 with Micron paying roughly ¥60 billion (about $750 million) upfront plus contingent installment payments through 2019, versus the far larger sums Japan Inc. had put into keeping Elpida independent.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Merging three weak DRAM units into one national champion (1999-2003).** Combining NEC's, Hitachi's, and later Mitsubishi's chip businesses solved fragmentation on paper but did not fix the underlying cost-competitiveness problem against Samsung — it consolidated the weakness rather than eliminating it.
- **Betting on premium Japan-made DRAM over commodity volume (under CEO Yukio Sakamoto).** Shifting commodity production to Taiwan to protect margin in Japan required premium orders that never materialized at the needed scale, leaving Elpida stuck funding a transition with the low-margin business it was trying to exit.
- **METI's 2009 bailout without a forcing function (¥30 billion).** Government money extended Elpida's runway but wasn't paired with a structural fix (like the abandoned Taiwan Memory Co. tie-up), so the same underlying cost problem simply resurfaced three years later as full insolvency.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Elpida is a textbook case of **consolidation as a substitute for a real cost-structure fix in a commodity industry** — merging weak competitors together, even with state backing, does not change the economics that made each of them weak individually. It's also a cautionary counterpoint to Japan's later, more successful state-backed chip rescue (Renesas, 2012-13, logged separately in this lane): a bailout without a credible path back to cost or product-mix competitiveness just delays the reckoning, and in Elpida's case handed the eventual winner (Micron) a discounted entry into DRAM that Japanese industrial policy had spent years and hundreds of billions of yen trying to prevent.
