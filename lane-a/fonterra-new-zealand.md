## One-liner

Fonterra, the farmer-owned co-operative that handles most of New Zealand's milk, spent the 2010s under CEO Theo Spierings trying to become a global consumer-brands and offshore-milk company, culminating in a NZ$754 million purchase of 18.8% of Chinese infant-formula maker Beingmate in 2015; after that stake lost NZ$519 million, the co-op posted its first ever loss in 2018 and a NZ$605 million loss in 2019, then under Miles Hurrell and chairman Peter McBride reversed course over seven years, selling Tip Top, DFE Pharma, its Chilean business Soprole, its China farms and finally its entire global consumer business (Anchor, Mainland, Western Star) to Lactalis for NZ$4.22 billion in March 2026, returning NZ$3.2 billion to farmers and re-founding itself as a business-to-business ingredients and foodservice supplier of New Zealand milk.

## What happened

**The setup (2001–2015).** Fonterra was created in 2001 by merging New Zealand's two largest dairy co-operatives and the Dairy Board, giving it roughly 80% of the country's milk and a mandate that has always been contested: is it a processor that exists to pay farmers the highest possible price for their milk, or a global food company that should reinvest earnings in brands and offshore assets? Under Theo Spierings, chief executive from 2011, the answer was the latter. The stated strategy was "volume to value": move away from commodity milk powder toward higher-margin consumer products and take positions in the fast-growing markets, above all China. Fonterra built and bought dairy farms in China, held integrated consumer businesses in Chile (Soprole), Australia, Sri Lanka and Brazil (the DPA joint venture), and in February 2015 completed the deal that defined the era, a partial tender offer for 18.8% of Shenzhen-listed Beingmate Baby & Child Food: 192.4 million shares at 18 yuan each, 3.5 billion yuan, about NZ$754 million. The stake was framed as a strategic partnership to give Fonterra's infant formula a route into Chinese retail. It coincided with a second problem still working its way through arbitration: in 2013 Fonterra had warned customers that a whey protein concentrate might be contaminated with botulism-causing bacteria, forcing Danone to recall infant formula. The scare turned out to be a false alarm, but Danone sued.

**The pressure (2016–2018).** Beingmate lost 781 million yuan in 2016 and 1.1 billion yuan in 2017 as China's infant-formula market was reshaped by new registration rules and cross-border e-commerce, and its share price collapsed. Fonterra had no operational control and, as it later conceded, could not get the information it needed from the company. In March 2018 the co-op reported a NZ$348 million half-year loss after writing the Beingmate stake down by NZ$405 million; the same month Spierings announced he would step down. In July 2018 chairman John Wilson stepped down for health reasons and was replaced by John Monaghan, and in August Miles Hurrell, a 25-year Fonterra employee who had been running the ingredients and consumer businesses, became interim chief executive. The full-year result in September 2018 was a net loss of NZ$196 million, the first in the co-operative's 17-year history: revenue was NZ$20.4 billion, up 6%, but earnings before interest and tax fell 22% to NZ$902 million, the Beingmate write-down reached NZ$439 million for the year, and the Danone arbitration cost NZ$232 million. Hurrell's first public line was that "Fonterra's current performance is not good enough." He was formally appointed chief executive in March 2019.

**The reset (2019).** The 2019 financial year was the year the board stopped defending the old strategy. On 7 August 2019 Fonterra announced it would sell down Beingmate on the Shenzhen exchange in increments, with Hurrell stating that the holding was now viewed "as a financial investment only." On 26 September 2019 the co-op reported a NZ$605 million net loss after NZ$826 million of write-downs across DPA Brazil, Fonterra Brands New Zealand and China Farms, and paid no dividend. The same announcement contained the new strategy: focus on New Zealand milk, build the ingredients business around paediatrics, medical and ageing, sports and core dairy, grow foodservice in Asia-Pacific, and rationalise the offshore milk pools. The operating discipline behind it was explicit. Capital expenditure was cut by NZ$261 million against a NZ$200 million target, operating expenses fell NZ$185 million year on year, and a NZ$800 million debt-reduction target was exceeded through asset sales: the Tip Top ice-cream business went for NZ$380 million, the stake in DFE Pharma for NZ$633 million, Venezuela was exited and the Dennington plant in Australia closed. Return on capital was 5.8%. Monaghan set a five-year target of 50 cents of earnings per share and guided 15 to 25 cents for the following year.

**Finishing the retreat from China (2020–2023).** By January 2021 the Beingmate stake was down to 3.94%, then 2.82%, with a full exit before the end of the 2021 financial year. The shares sold over two years fetched NZ$273 million, a NZ$519 million loss on a six-year holding. In March 2021 Fonterra also announced it would sell both its own China farms and its joint-venture farms, while reporting normalised half-year profit of NZ$418 million, up 43%, and net debt of NZ$5.6 billion. In September 2021 the co-op published its strategy to 2030, which made the New Zealand-milk focus permanent: Chile would be sold outright because Soprole needed neither New Zealand milk nor New Zealand expertise, Australia would be reviewed for a possible partial float, and about NZ$1 billion would be invested in emissions and water at New Zealand plants over the decade. The Soprole sale to Peru's Gloria Foods, roughly NZ$1 billion, was announced in November 2022 and completed on 31 March 2023, funding a capital return of about NZ$800 million, 50 cents per share and unit.

**The second, bigger decision (2024–2026).** On 16 May 2024 chairman Peter McBride and Hurrell announced a strategic review that went further than anyone outside the board had expected: Fonterra would explore full or partial divestment of its entire global consumer business, including Anchor, Mainland, Kāpiti, Anlene, Anmum, Fernleaf, Western Star and Perfect Italiano, together with Fonterra Oceania and Fonterra Sri Lanka, and withdrew its 2030 financial targets because they had assumed those businesses stayed. The rationale, in Hurrell's words, was that the co-op could create more value "by focusing on being a B2B dairy nutrition provider." The numbers behind that were stark: in the 2023 financial year ingredients generated NZ$21.3 billion of revenue against NZ$3.3 billion for consumer, and about 80% of the milk went to ingredients versus 15% to consumer products, while the consumer business consumed a disproportionate share of capital and management attention. On 22 August 2025 Fonterra agreed to sell the businesses, collectively branded Mainland Group, to France's Lactalis, led by Emmanuel Besnier, for an enterprise value of NZ$3.845 billion, rising to NZ$4.22 billion if the Bega cheese licences held by the Australian business were included, which they were. McBride called Lactalis "the highest value option for the Co-op, including over the long-term." The co-op promised a tax-free capital return of NZ$2.00 per share, about NZ$3.2 billion. The FY25 result on 25 September 2025 showed what the reset had produced before the sale: revenue of NZ$26 billion, profit after tax of NZ$1,079 million, return on capital of 10.9% against 5.8% in 2019, a fully imputed dividend of 57 cents, and a farmgate milk price of NZ$10.16 per kilogram of milk solids, NZ$15.3 billion paid to farmers. On 30 October 2025 farmer shareholders approved the sale with 88.47% of votes cast in favour. The deal closed on 30 March 2026 after approvals in eight jurisdictions; the NZ$3.2 billion was paid on 14 April 2026, about NZ$400,000 for the average farm. Two weeks before completion Hurrell announced he would step down after eight years as chief executive and more than NZ$7 billion of asset sales; Richard Allen, the head of the ingredients business, took over on 1 May 2026.

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Buying 18.8% of Beingmate for NZ$754 million without control (Spierings and the board, February 2015).** The defining bet of the expansion era and the trigger for everything that followed. A minority stake in a listed company in a market undergoing regulatory upheaval gave Fonterra all the downside of an owner with none of the levers, and the co-op later admitted it could not even obtain reliable information from the company. The NZ$519 million loss was the visible cost; the larger cost was that it forced farmers to ask what the offshore strategy was for.

- **Changing the leadership and calling the loss a strategy failure rather than a bad year (board, 2018–2019).** With Spierings, Wilson and much of the executive gone within twelve months, Monaghan and Hurrell had the freedom to book NZ$826 million of write-downs in one go and pay no dividend. The decision to take the pain in FY19 rather than spread it is what made the September 2019 strategy credible to farmers and lenders.

- **Reclassifying Beingmate as a financial investment and selling in the market (Hurrell, 7 August 2019).** Small in money, decisive in meaning. It ended the pretence that the stake was strategic and set the precedent that every offshore asset would be judged on whether it needed New Zealand milk or New Zealand expertise. That single test later dictated the Soprole and China farms sales.

- **Writing the test into the 2030 strategy (Hurrell and McBride, September 2021).** Turning "focus on New Zealand milk" from a slogan into a rule for capital allocation is why the co-op could sell Chile, a good business, without controversy: it failed the test.

- **Extending the logic to the crown-jewel consumer brands (McBride and Hurrell, 16 May 2024 to 22 August 2025).** The hardest call, because Anchor and Mainland are national identity as much as assets. The board chose to follow the return-on-capital evidence over sentiment, withdrew its own 2030 targets, and made the case that a farmer's capital earns more in ingredients and foodservice supplied to customers like Lactalis than in running supermarket brands against them. The 88.47% vote showed farmers agreed.

- **Returning the proceeds rather than redeploying them (board, 2025–2026).** Fonterra returned NZ$3.2 billion and kept about NZ$1 billion for projects, instead of using the money to buy something new. For a co-operative whose owners are also its suppliers, that choice re-anchored the company on the farmgate milk price as the primary return.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

Fonterra is a case of an organisation discovering which of its two identities it actually is. For a decade it tried to be a consumer-brands multinational financed by a captive milk supply; the Beingmate loss, the Danone settlement and the first-ever loss in 2018 exposed that the co-op did not have the control, the capital or the mandate for that game. The reset that followed was not a single decision but a rule applied consistently for seven years: keep only what needs New Zealand milk or New Zealand expertise, and sell everything else regardless of how good the business is or how attached the owners are to it. Operators will recognise the shape. Strategy drift usually shows up as a portfolio of assets that each made sense in isolation and together answer no single question; the fix is rarely a new vision, it is an explicit test that every asset has to pass, applied first to the easy cases (Venezuela, Tip Top) and then, once trust is rebuilt, to the ones that feel untouchable (Anchor and Mainland). The second lesson is about minority stakes as market entry: a large, non-controlling holding in a listed partner in a volatile regulatory market combines the capital exposure of an acquisition with the influence of a customer, and Fonterra's NZ$519 million loss is the reference price for that mistake in Asian consumer markets. The third is that a founder-style reset can be led by an insider. Hurrell had spent 25 years inside the old strategy, but once the board changed the question from growth to return on farmers' capital, he ran the retreat more thoroughly than an outsider might have, and return on capital went from 5.8% to 10.9% before the final sale.
