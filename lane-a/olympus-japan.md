## One-liner

A 100-year-old Japanese optics conglomerate that survived one of the country's largest accounting frauds, then — under activist pressure from ValueAct — sold off the camera business that made its name and the microscope business it was founded on, to become a pure-play medical-device company; the pivot roughly tripled operating profit but left it exposed to a single regulator whose enforcement it has still not cleared.

## What happened

**Setup: a company hiding its past.** Olympus was founded in 1919 as a microscope maker, produced its first camera in 1936, and by the 2000s was two very different companies under one roof: a world-leading medical business (roughly 70% share of the global gastrointestinal endoscope market) and a consumer camera business fighting a losing war against smartphones. In the 1990s, bubble-era investment losses ("zaiteku" speculation) had been hidden off the balance sheet through a "tobashi" scheme. To pay for that concealment, management ran the losses through absurdly priced acquisitions: a $2 billion purchase of British medical-device maker Gyrus in 2008 came with a $687 million "advisory fee" (about 31% of the deal, against a 1–2% norm), and three small Japanese companies bought for ¥73.5 billion were written down by ¥55.7 billion almost immediately.

**The whistle.** In October 2011, Michael Woodford — the first non-Japanese president, promoted to CEO only two weeks earlier — confronted chairman Tsuyoshi Kikukawa over the fees and was fired by unanimous board vote on 14 October 2011. Woodford went to the press and to UK and US authorities. On 8 November 2011 Olympus admitted its accounting was "inappropriate" and that the payments had been used to hide losses going back to the 1990s. Market capitalisation fell from ¥673 billion to ¥422 billion in a single day and was down roughly 80% within a month; Tokyo Stock Exchange delisting was a live possibility. Investigators later put "the true magnitude of losses" at ¥481 billion (~$6.25 billion) in questionable payments between 2000 and 2009. In September 2012 Kikukawa, executive vice president Hisashi Mori, and auditor Hideo Yamada pleaded guilty; in 2013 they received suspended sentences and the company was fined ¥700 million. Kikukawa's line in court: "There is no mistake. The entire responsibility lies with me."

**The rebuild that didn't fix the structure (2012–2018).** Olympus survived — Sony took an equity stake in 2012 and the medical business kept printing money — but the company remained a conglomerate: Medical, Scientific Solutions (microscopes, industrial NDT), and Imaging (cameras). The camera division lost money in most years; Olympus's own later disclosure put cumulative imaging operating losses at ¥103 billion over twelve years. Governance had been reformed on paper, but strategy hadn't. The company was, in effect, using endoscope profits to subsidise a consumer electronics business it was sentimentally attached to.

**The activist arrives (2018).** In May 2018, San Francisco's ValueAct Capital disclosed a 5.04% stake — its first-ever investment in Japan. Activism in Japan had a track record of failure, and the initial reaction inside Olympus was alarm. But rather than fight, incoming leadership engaged.

**"Transform Olympus" (January 2019).** On 10 January 2019, Olympus announced a full transformation programme. Yasuo Takeuchi — a career Olympus executive who had run the European business and was CFO during the post-scandal clean-up — would become President and CEO on 1 April 2019, replacing Hiroyuki Sasa. The board would migrate to a US-style three-committee structure (nominating, audit, compensation), and three new outside directors would be nominated, including ValueAct partner D. Robert Hale — the first time a Japanese blue-chip had voluntarily seated an activist. The medical business was split into Endoscopic Solutions (run from Japan) and Therapeutic Solutions (run from the United States), explicitly to chase the US market for procedure-driven devices. The first hard action was a freeze on FY2020 SG&A and R&D at the FY2018 level. The stated ambition was to become a "truly global medtech company" with margins "in line with global medical device industry peers."

**Cutting off the camera (2020).** The camera business was the emotional test. On 24 June 2020, Olympus signed an MOU with Japan Industrial Partners (JIP) — the Tokyo private-equity firm known for buying Sony's VAIO PC business in 2014 — to carve out Imaging after three consecutive years of operating losses. Olympus's stated reason was "rapid market shrink caused by the evolution of smartphones." The definitive agreement was signed 30 September 2020; the transfer closed 1 January 2021, with 95% of the new company going to JIP's vehicle OJ Holdings and the business rebranded as OM Digital Solutions (keeping the Zuiko and OM names). The exit was not cheap: the FY2021 divestiture charge was another ¥53 billion, bringing total imaging losses recognised to about ¥156 billion (~$1.7 billion) — almost exactly the size of the 2011 fraud.

**Cutting off the microscopes (2022).** With cameras gone, the next question was whether Scientific Solutions — the business Olympus was literally founded on in 1919 — belonged in a medtech company. Management's answer was no. The unit was carved out as Evident in April 2022 and on 30 August 2022 Olympus agreed to sell it to Bain Capital for ¥427.6 billion (~$3.1 billion). Evident was profitable (revenue ¥119.1 billion, operating profit ¥17.5 billion), so this was a portfolio decision, not a rescue: a good business that didn't fit the strategy was sold at a good price to fund the one that did. The deal closed 4 January 2023.

**The pivot delivers — and then meets the FDA (2023–2026).** Financially the transformation worked. For the fiscal year ended March 2025, revenue was ¥997.3 billion (+7.7%) and operating profit ¥162.5 billion, up 216% year on year; Endoscopic Solutions alone earned ¥141.4 billion on ¥636.1 billion of revenue, an adjusted margin of 25%. The share price had more than doubled since ValueAct's 2019 board win, and Takeuchi publicly credited the activist directors with strengthening the company. Takeuchi handed the CEO role to Stefan Kaufmann — a German, the first foreign CEO since Woodford — in April 2023, and moved to chairman.

But the same period exposed the flip side of being a pure-play. Between November 2022 and March 2023 the FDA issued three warning letters to Olympus Medical Systems and its Aizu plant over medical-device-reporting failures and quality-system violations across duodenoscopes, bronchoscopes, urological and GI scopes, and endoscope reprocessors — the core of the business. Late 2023 brought recalls of bronchoscopes and insufflation devices. In October 2024 Kaufmann was forced out after an internal probe into an allegation he had bought illegal drugs; shares fell more than 7% and Takeuchi came back as CEO. In June 2025 the FDA escalated to import alerts blocking 58 Olympus device models made at a Japanese plant from entering the US, citing "outstanding Quality System regulation violations." Olympus spent ¥11 billion on regulatory remediation in FY2025 and set an end-of-FY2026 target to complete its FDA commitments. The FY2026 results (published 12 May 2026) show the cost: revenue flat at ¥1,010.6 billion, operating profit down 40% to ¥97.1 billion, and the newly created Surgical and Interventional Solutions segment swinging to a ¥15 billion loss.

## Key decisions / turning points

1. **Firing the whistleblower (Oct 2011, Kikukawa and the board).** The board's unanimous decision to remove Woodford rather than answer his questions turned a containable historical fraud into a global scandal that destroyed 80% of market value in weeks. The lesson Olympus drew — that its old board structure could not police itself — is the direct ancestor of the 2019 governance overhaul.

2. **Engaging the activist instead of fighting (2018–2019, Takeuchi and the board).** When ValueAct showed up with 5%, the Japanese playbook was to stonewall. Olympus instead put ValueAct's Hale on the board and adopted a three-committee structure. Takeuchi later said the activist directors "bolstered" the company. This decision is what made the subsequent divestitures politically possible internally: an outside voice on the board gave management cover to sell sacred businesses.

3. **The SG&A/R&D freeze as the first move (Jan 2019).** Rather than opening with a dramatic sale, Transform Olympus started with a cost discipline that forced every division to justify itself at flat budgets — which made the camera business's economics undeniable.

4. **Selling the camera business to a turnaround PE firm rather than closing it (June 2020, Takeuchi).** Olympus chose JIP — a specialist in revitalising orphaned Japanese businesses — over a shutdown, which preserved the OM/Zuiko brands and engineering staff and limited the reputational hit in Japan. The trade-off: a ¥53 billion charge and an admission that twelve years of losses (¥103 billion) had been carried for sentiment, not strategy.

5. **Selling a profitable, founding business because it didn't fit (Aug 2022).** Evident was making ¥17.5 billion a year. Selling it for ¥427.6 billion to Bain was the clearest statement of what "pure-play medtech" meant: portfolio logic over heritage. This is the decision that most distinguishes Olympus's pivot from typical Japanese restructurings, which usually only shed loss-makers.

6. **Splitting medical into a Japan-run endoscopy business and a US-run therapeutic business (2019).** By moving the therapeutic device business's management to the US, Olympus tied its growth strategy to the US market — and therefore to the FDA. That bet is now the source of both its 25% endoscopy margins and its import alerts.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

- **A fraud recovery and a strategic pivot are different projects; Olympus needed both, seven years apart.** From 2012 to 2018 the company fixed its books and its board but not its portfolio, and kept subsidising a dying consumer business with medical profits. Founders who survive a near-death event often mistake "we're stable again" for "we've fixed the strategy." The real pivot only started when an outsider forced the portfolio question.

- **Activists can be a tool, not just a threat.** Olympus is the reference case in Japan for using an activist investor as internal leverage: management that already wanted to divest used ValueAct's board seat as cover to do things a consensus-driven Japanese board would never have initiated. For operators in consensus cultures, the move is to recruit the external pressure you need rather than resist it.

- **The hardest divestiture is the profitable one.** Killing the loss-making camera business was emotionally hard but analytically obvious. Selling Evident — profitable, founding heritage, no crisis — was the decision that proved the strategy was real. A pivot that only sheds losers is a clean-up, not a pivot.

- **Concentration cuts both ways.** Going pure-play took operating profit from ¥51 billion to ¥162 billion in a year, and then a single regulator's quality findings took 40% of it back. A diversified conglomerate would have absorbed the FDA problem; a focused one wears it. The quality-system failures were not new — the warning letters cover practices from years earlier — but they only became existential once there was nothing else in the portfolio. Any founder narrowing to one product line should assume their regulator, platform, or channel becomes a board-level risk the moment they do.

- **Governance scandals recur in new forms.** Thirteen years after firing a foreign CEO for asking questions, Olympus fired its next foreign CEO over personal conduct. The three-committee board did its job in 2024 in a way the 2011 board did not — but the fact that Takeuchi had to return as CEO, six years after starting the transformation, shows how thin the succession bench under a founder-like transformation leader can be.
