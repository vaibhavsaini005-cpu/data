## One-liner

Japanese autonomous-driving company TIER IV, founded by Shinpei Kato out of his open-source Autoware project, unveiled a hardware-agnostic, data-centric AI software stack for Level 4 autonomy and began simultaneous real-world testing in Tokyo, Pittsburgh and Munich.

## What happened

Shinpei Kato began developing Autoware, open-source software for autonomous driving, at Nagoya University in 2013, releasing it publicly in August 2015 before founding TIER IV that December to commercialize a full self-driving platform built on it. In 2018 Kato also founded the nonprofit Autoware Foundation, on the reasoning that open-source software "does not show its true value if it belongs to a single company" — deliberately keeping Autoware's governance independent of TIER IV itself even as TIER IV built commercial products (software, vehicle procurement, operational support) around it.

On March 20, 2026, TIER IV announced a new AI-based Level 4 autonomous driving stack designed specifically to be hardware-agnostic — compatible with different system-on-chip and sensor configurations rather than one fixed hardware setup — paired with a "data-centric AI" approach and a collaborative MLOps platform. Kato framed the shift as a technical necessity for the next capability tier: "To achieve Level 4+ autonomy, we need technology that evolves autonomously alongside the environments it serves," adding that the new models and platform "provide a common language and a shared foundation for the entire industry." TIER IV backed the announcement with simultaneous testing programs in three cities: Tokyo (with the University of Tokyo, using a Toyota JPN TAXI for urban travel), Pittsburgh (with Carnegie Mellon University, using a Hyundai IONIQ 5 for airport-to-campus robotaxi routes) and Munich (with the Technical University of Munich, using a Volkswagen T7 Multivan for urban safety evaluation).

## Key decisions / turning points

- **Keeping Autoware's governance in an independent nonprofit foundation rather than folding it fully into TIER IV**: Kato's 2018 decision protected the open-source project's industry-wide credibility, which TIER IV's 2026 "common language for the entire industry" positioning still depends on.
- **Designing the new stack to be hardware-agnostic rather than optimized for one SoC/sensor configuration**: a specific technical bet that portability across automakers' hardware choices would matter more than performance tuned to a single platform.
- **Launching identical, simultaneous test programs in three different regulatory and academic environments (Japan, US, Germany) rather than proving the technology in one market first**: used university partnerships (Tokyo, CMU, TU Munich) as the testing infrastructure in each region instead of building it independently.

## Why it matters (pattern / lesson)

TIER IV's approach shows a specific way an open-source-rooted company sustains commercial relevance over a decade: keep the open-source core governed independently (avoiding the appearance of a single-vendor lock-in) while the commercial entity builds proprietary layers (data-centric AI, MLOps) on top, and use that credibility to run parallel, multi-region pilots through academic partnerships rather than a slower single-market rollout. For autonomous vehicle software specifically, betting on hardware-agnosticism is a direct response to automakers' fragmented SoC and sensor choices — a portability bet aimed at becoming infrastructure across manufacturers rather than a feature within one.
